## 8. COMPLICATIONS (26:00–37:00)
[NORMAL]

By the end of twenty twenty-five, a French court had convicted a man of murdering a woman whose body had never been found. What nobody had was the one thing that would answer every doubt: Delphine.

[HALFWAY RESET ENDS]

Some in the French press had started calling it "the perfect murder." [SRC: 11] No body, no crime scene, no confession, and yet a conviction. To some, that phrase was a criticism of the investigation. To others, it was a description of a killer who had nearly gotten away with it. Either way, the phrase stuck.

And so the question that hung over the appeal was this: was the first verdict right? Or had a country's fascination with a missing nurse turned a set of suspicious coincidences into a conviction?

[NORMAL]

To understand how a French jury could convict without a body, you need to understand a phrase at the heart of French criminal law. Intime conviction. [SRC: 4]

It translates, roughly, as "inner conviction" or "deep-seated conviction." In France's assize courts, which try the most serious crimes, the judges and jurors aren't asked to tick off a list of required types of evidence. They're asked to examine all the evidence, and to decide, in their own conscience, whether they are convinced. [SRC: 4] There's no formal rule that you need a body, or a weapon, or a confession. What matters is whether the evidence as a whole, taken together, persuades them.

In the first-instance assize court, the verdict is decided by three professional judges sitting together with six citizen jurors. On appeal, the number of jurors rises to nine. The judges and jurors deliberate together, and a conviction requires a qualified majority, not just a simple one. It's a system designed so that ordinary citizens, not just professional judges, carry the weight of the most serious verdicts.

[SLOW]

History gives both sides ammunition. In England in sixteen sixty, in a case known as the Campden Wonder, a man named William Harrison vanished. A servant confessed to his murder, implicating his own mother and brother. All three were hanged. Two years later, William Harrison came home, alive, with a strange story of having been kidnapped. The case haunted English law for centuries, and helped create a lasting caution among judges about convicting anyone of murder without a body.

But modern forensic science has changed the calculation. Phone data, DNA, digital records and careful investigation can now prove a death, and who caused it, without a body. Courts in many countries, including France, Britain and the United States, have convicted killers in no-body cases. Some of those convictions have later been confirmed when the victim was finally found.

Supporters of that system say it's exactly how justice should work. Real murderers hide bodies. If you could only convict with a body, then the most careful killers would go free. Circumstantial evidence, when there's enough of it pointing the same way, can be as powerful as any eyewitness.

Critics say it's dangerous. Without a body, you can't establish how someone died, or even for certain that they're dead. People have disappeared voluntarily and turned up years later. A jury influenced by a media storm, by a defendant's unappealing manner, by a story that feels right, could convict an innocent person on a pile of maybes.

Both of those positions were argued across France in the weeks after the verdict.

[NORMAL]

The defence's case, in fairness, was not weak.

They could point out that there was no physical evidence of a killing in the house. No body. No murder weapon. No forensic proof of what happened. Broken glasses could have been broken any number of ways. Washing a duvet is something families do. Phone data can be interpreted in more than one way. Neighbours hearing screams on a curfew night couldn't say exactly what they'd heard, or from exactly where. A frightened six-year-old's memory, recorded by investigators, is precious but fragile. Threats said in anger are ugly, but they aren't murder. And the accounts of former partners came from people with their own relationships and motives.

Taken one by one, the defence argued, every piece had an innocent explanation. And together, they argued, a pile of innocent explanations is still not proof.

[SLOW]

For Delphine's family, none of the legal debate changed the most painful fact. They had no body to bury. No grave to visit. No funeral. For five years, there had been the chance, however small, that she was alive somewhere. And there was the knowledge that, if she wasn't, someone knew where she was.

For her children, growing up with their mother missing and their father in prison, the situation was even harder to describe. A mother missing. A father convicted of killing her, and saying he hadn't.

[NORMAL]

The appeal trial was set for September twenty twenty-six, in Toulouse. [SRC: 8] A new court. A new jury. Everything argued again from the beginning. France prepared for round two.

And then, in July twenty twenty-six, two months before the appeal, Cédric Jubillar's lawyers received a letter.

## 10. RESOLUTION (43:00–49:00)
[NORMAL]

[ON SCREEN: As of October 2026]

So, can you convict a man of murder with no body, no weapon, and no confession? In this case, a French court did. And when the body was finally found, it was found because of the man they had convicted.

But this story isn't over, and it's important to be precise about where things stand.

[SLOW]

Cédric Jubillar's appeal was postponed. The appeal trial that had been set to begin in September twenty twenty-six in Toulouse was moved. It is now scheduled to take place from April thirtieth to May fourteenth, twenty twenty-seven, before the assize court of the Haute-Garonne. [SRC: 8]

An appeal in France is a full retrial. The new court can confirm the verdict, change it, increase the sentence, or reduce it. Prosecutors can argue for a different outcome too. Everything is open.

Until that appeal is decided, his conviction is not final, and under French law he remains presumed innocent of the charge as it will be judged on appeal. The confession, in some ways, makes the appeal more complicated, not less. A defendant who has admitted causing a death but denies planning it is asking the court to believe one part of his account and reject another reading of the evidence. Prosecutors will test whether his version of the night fits the physical facts. His lawyers will argue that his confession, and the fact that it led to Delphine being found, should weigh in his favour.

What will be argued, now, is likely to be different. Not whether he caused his wife's death, which he has admitted, but how. Whether it was premeditated or not. What exactly happened. And what sentence fits.

[NORMAL]

That distinction matters in French law. Meurtre, murder, is an intentional killing, and it carries a maximum of thirty years in prison, the sentence he received in Albi. Assassinat is a killing with premeditation, and it can be punished by life imprisonment. In his confession, Cédric Jubillar insisted that what happened was not planned. ## 11. AFTERMATH + MEANING (49:00–53:00)
[NORMAL]

It would be easy to take the wrong lesson from this case. To say: the jury convicted without a body, and they were right, so convicting without a body is fine.

That's not quite the lesson. In this case, the evidence pointed the right way. In other cases, in other countries, convictions without a body have turned out to be wrong. People have been convicted of killing someone who later turned up alive. Circumstantial evidence is powerful, but it's also where confirmation bias does its worst work: once investigators and the public are convinced, every fact starts to look like it fits.

What made this case hold up wasn't the absence of a body. It was the number and independence of the pieces. The glasses, found by gendarmes. The phone data, from a machine. The neighbours, with no connection to each other. A child's account, recorded carefully. Each came from a different source. None depended on the others. And they all pointed the same way.

That's the standard every no-body case should be held to.

[SLOW]

There's another lesson in this case, and it's one that experts on domestic violence have been repeating for years. The most dangerous time for a woman in an abusive or controlling relationship is often the moment she decides to leave. Separation can be the trigger. In France, as in many countries, official figures record dozens of women killed by a current or former partner every year, and advocates have campaigned hard for the word "féminicide" to be used, so that these deaths are seen as a pattern, not a series of private tragedies.

Delphine had decided to leave. The couple had reportedly been heading toward a separation, described in some reports as an amicable divorce. [SRC: 11] She had started a new relationship. And on the night she died, according to her husband's own later account, there was a dispute. Whatever the appeal court finds about premeditation, her death fits a pattern that's tragically familiar.

But let's end with Delphine.

She was thirty-three. A girl from Gaillac, one of four children, who grew up without much and became a night nurse. A nurse, in the year when nurses were holding a frightened world together. A mother of two. A woman who had decided her marriage was over and that she was going to start again. She had a new relationship, and a future she was planning.

On the first night of a curfew, in a house with a Christmas tree in the living room, that future was taken from her. And then, for five and a half years, she was taken from her family a second time: hidden, searched for, argued over, made into a national story and a legal debate, while her children grew up without her.

In the summer of twenty twenty-six, she was found. Her family could finally bring her home.

Her children are growing up now in a world where everything about that night is public. One day they'll read the articles, watch the documentaries, perhaps watch videos like this one. That's a responsibility for everyone who tells this story. They lost their mother. Whatever else they read, they should be able to find in it the truth about who she was: a nurse who worked nights so that other people's families could sleep, and a mother who, in the last evening of her life, was planning a future.

[NORMAL]

It's worth remembering, too, how much the pandemic shaped this story. The curfew that emptied the streets. The exhausted health system Delphine worked in. The months of lockdown that had kept families like hers shut in together, with whatever tensions they carried. Domestic violence services in France, as in many countries, reported surges in calls during the lockdowns. Delphine's death came on the very first night after the second lockdown ended. That isn't an explanation. But it's part of the world she lived in, and died in.

So, the question we started with. Can you convict a man of murder with no body, no weapon and no confession? And what happens when, after the verdict, the body is found?

Here's the most honest answer the record allows. You can, if the circumstantial evidence is strong enough, independent enough, and pointing the same way. That's what happened in Albi. And when the body was found, it didn't overturn that verdict. It confirmed what the evidence had said all along: that Delphine Jubillar never left her house that night of her own accord.

There's a lesson here for investigators, too. In the years before the confession, critics argued that the case against Cédric Jubillar was built on bias: that once the gendarmes and the public had decided he was guilty, every ambiguous fact was read against him. Sometimes that criticism is exactly right; this channel has covered cases where it was. In this case, the investigators' reading of the evidence turned out to be correct. But the right answer doesn't make the process beyond question. The test of an investigation isn't whether it gets lucky. It's whether it would have caught its own mistake if it had been wrong.

What's striking about this case is that, in the end, the most important piece of evidence came from the person who had tried hardest to hide it. For five and a half years, the answer to where Delphine was had been with one man. The investigators, the jury, the whole country, had been right about him. They just couldn't find her without him.
