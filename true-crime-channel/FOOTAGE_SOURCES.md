# Footage Sources — Body Cam, Interrogation, Court, Victim Interviews

Research date: 2026-10-21. Companion to `TRUE_CRIME_SCRIPT_RULES.md` §2 (source hierarchy) and §13 (visuals).

**Short answer:** there is no single site where body cam footage is "uploaded every month" for anyone to download. The footage reaches the public through four channels: (1) police departments' own critical-incident release pages, (2) oversight-agency portals, (3) public-records (FOIA) requests, and (4) court exhibits and courtroom livestreams. Everything below is organised by how much effort each route takes.

---

## 1. Free, recurring, no request needed (check these first)

| Source | What it posts | Cadence | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chicago COPA Case Portal** — chicagocopa.org/case-portal | Body cam, dash cam, 911 audio, OEMC dispatch, police reports for every officer-involved shooting / in-custody death | Rolling; releases within 60 days of incident (city policy) | Best single portal in the US. Files are downloadable. Redacted. |
| **LAPD Critical Incident Community Briefings** — LAPD YouTube channel | Edited briefing video with body cam, 911 audio, narration | Within 45 days of each incident (Board of Police Commissioners policy) | Edited by LAPD, so the raw footage must be requested separately. |
| **NYPD** — nyc.gov/nypd newsroom + YouTube | Critical-incident body cam releases | Within 30 days of incident | Edited releases. |
| **Portland PB** — portland.gov/police | Deadly-force body cam | Within 14 days | Edited releases. |
| **Salt Lake City PD** — police.slc.gov press releases | Body cam from every officer-involved critical incident, each camera angle linked | Per incident, by city ordinance | Note the first 30 seconds have no audio (buffer), not a malfunction. |
| **Hayward PD (CA)** — hayward-ca.gov Critical Incident Briefing page | Briefing videos | Per incident | California SB 1421 / AB 748 force most CA agencies to release use-of-force video within 45 days. |
| **Phoenix PD** | Body cam for critical incidents, blurred | Per incident | All public-records video now released with full-frame blur, audio redacted separately. |
| **Seattle PD** — "SPD BodyWornVideo" YouTube | Hundreds of hours of body cam, audio stripped, faces blurred | Irregular since 2015 | Useful as generic b-roll because it is already anonymised. |
| **Minnesota Judicial Branch — High-Profile Cases** — mncourts.gov | Court exhibits (incl. body cam, interrogation video) in high-profile cases | Per case | Example: State v. Lane (George Floyd) exhibits. |
| **Fulton County Superior Court (GA) YouTube**; individual judges (e.g., Judge McBurney's channel) | Full trial livestreams and archives | Per trial | Georgia is the most camera-friendly state court system. Florida and Arizona courts also stream many trials; check the county court's own YouTube. |
| **State AG / prosecutor "decision" pages** (e.g., Washington AG, Minnesota BCA, California DOJ) | Body cam attached to charging or no-charge decisions | Per case | Usually a PDF report plus video links. |

**Where to find more of these:** the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press map at **rcfp.org/bodycams** shows, state by state, whether body cam footage is a public record and under what law. Start there to see which states (and therefore which cities) release freely.

## 2. Request-based (free or low cost, 2–12 weeks)

This is how almost every body-cam YouTube channel (PoliceActivity, Code Blue Cam, Bodycam Files, Midwest Safety) actually gets footage. They file public-records requests under **state** open-records law (not federal FOIA, which covers only federal agencies).

**How to file**
1. Identify the agency (city PD, county sheriff, state patrol) and the incident date / case number from local news.
2. Use the agency's portal. Most run **NextRequest (CivicPlus)**, **GovQA**, or **JustFOIA**. Google `"<city> police" "public records request"`.
3. Ask for: "all body-worn camera, in-car camera, 911 audio, CAD/dispatch logs and incident reports for incident #___ on <date> at <location>." Ask that any redaction be marked and its legal basis stated.
4. Expect fees for redaction time. Ohio allows up to $75/hour, capped at $750 per request (HB 315). Wisconsin, Washington (Spokane County) and others have added similar fees since 2024 because of YouTube-channel demand.

**Tools**
- **MuckRock.com** — files and hosts requests publicly. Search its FOI database for "body camera" to see what others have already obtained; many completed requests have the video attached for free download.
- **Request templates** on MuckRock and on the RCFP site.

**Friendliest states for release:** California (SB 1421 / AB 748), Washington, Florida (Sunshine Law), Texas (but must give date, location, names; deadly-force video withheld until all proceedings end), Georgia, Ohio, Illinois (60-day rule in Chicago), Connecticut (96 hours after use of force), Minnesota (partial).

**Hard or closed:** New York outside NYPD critical incidents, Massachusetts (Boston has no release policy as of April 2026), Pennsylvania (Act 22 makes requests expensive and slow), Delaware, most of the South for non-fatal incidents.

**Outside the US:** UK forces release only at senior-officer discretion, usually after a trial. India (Maharashtra, UP) and Pakistan have no public-release framework. For UK/EU/Japan cases in the series (02, 04, 05, 06), do not expect body cam. Use court sketches, news stills, and reconstructions.

## 3. Interrogation video and victim / family interviews

### Interrogation footage
- Comes out as a **court exhibit** once admitted at trial. Ask the court clerk for the exhibit list, then request copies (some courts, like Minnesota, post them; most charge per DVD).
- Also obtainable by public-records request **after** the case is closed. While open, almost every state exempts it as an investigatory record.
- JCS-style channels rely on exhibits from Florida, Georgia, Texas, Ohio, Arizona — states where trial exhibits become public on admission.

### Victim and family interviews
Honest answer: there is **no public-domain archive** of victim interviews. The options are:

| Source | Status | How to use |
|---|---|---|
| **Victim impact statements** read in open court (sentencing) | Public record; livestreamed in GA/FL/AZ/OH courts | Quote or clip from the court's own stream. |
| **Press conferences** by family with police/prosecutor | Public event; the department's own recording is a government work | Use the agency's upload, not a TV station's. |
| **Police department / prosecutor YouTube uploads** | Government work; generally free to reuse (check state copyright, see §4) | Download the official version. |
| **Local TV news interviews** (Dateline, 48 Hours, 20/20, local affiliates) | **Copyrighted.** Fair use is possible for short clips with commentary, but YouTube Content ID will often flag them. | Keep clips short (<10 s), transformative, with on-screen attribution. Expect claims. |
| **Family-run memorial pages / foundations** | Private | Ask permission by email. Many families say yes to respectful coverage. |
| **Podcast / documentary interviews** | Copyrighted | Paraphrase and credit; do not lift audio. |
| **Court transcripts of testimony** | Public record | Read as narration, credit the transcript. This is what Casefile does. |

Rule for this channel (§7 of the script rules): never use an interview the family has asked creators not to use.

## 4. Copyright and platform risk (read before downloading anything)

- **Federal** government works (FBI, DOJ, federal courts) are public domain in the US.
- **State and local** government works (city PDs, sheriffs, state courts) are **not automatically** public domain. Copyright in them varies by state. California, Florida and a few others treat most public records as free to reuse; others are unsettled. In practice, agencies almost never pursue copyright on body cam, and the Freedom of the Press Foundation's position is that they have no valid claim, but it is not settled law.
- **News-media courtroom pool footage** (Court TV, Law&Crime, local stations) is copyrighted by the broadcaster even though the trial is public. Admission as evidence does not strip copyright.
- **YouTube policy (2025–26):** reused-content and "inauthentic content" rules target channels that are *only* raw footage with no commentary. Our format (50-minute scripted narrative, footage as b-roll) is on the safe side, but body-cam-only channels have been demonetised. Keep b-roll to short clips under narration, blur bystanders and minors, and never show the moment of death (§0.4).
- **Fees and backlash:** multiple states are adding fees specifically because of creator requests. File narrow, specific requests, not bulk "all DUI arrests" requests; the latter is what triggered the Ohio and Wisconsin laws.

## 5. Suggested workflow per episode

1. Read the Case Sheet: state, agency, court, year.
2. Check rcfp.org/bodycams for that state's rule.
3. Search the agency's YouTube and newsroom for an existing release.
4. Search MuckRock for a completed request on the same case.
5. Check the court's exhibit list; request admitted video exhibits.
6. If still nothing, file one narrow public-records request (allow 4–8 weeks).
7. Log every file's origin in the Case Sheet SOURCES list with the URL or request number. Footage is a source like any other (§2.2).
