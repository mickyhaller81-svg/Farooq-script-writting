## 1. COLD OPEN (0:00–1:00)
[SLOW]

September, twenty seventeen. The edge of a small city in northern Germany. A garage behind an ordinary house.

The floor of that garage has an inspection pit, the kind of trench a mechanic stands in to work under a car. Today, there are no cars. There is a jackhammer, a team of forensic specialists, and a pile of broken concrete growing in the driveway.

Standing at the garage door is a man who spent his entire career finding people. He ran one of the biggest criminal police forces in the country. Thousands of cases. Thousands of families who got answers because of him.

He never got one himself.

The woman he's waiting to find has been missing for twenty-eight years. She is his little sister. And for most of those twenty-eight years, the people who could have found her didn't look here.

## 3. THE WORLD BEFORE (1:30–5:00)
[NORMAL]

Her name was Birgit Meier. She was born Birgit Sielaff, on July ninth, nineteen forty-eight. [SRC: 6]

By the summer of nineteen eighty-nine, she was forty-one years old and living in Brietlingen, a village just outside Lüneburg. [SRC: 11] Lüneburg is an old Hanseatic town of brick gables and church spires, about an hour south-east of Hamburg. Quiet, prosperous, the kind of place people move to when they want the city close but not too close.

Birgit was a photographer. [SRC: 7] It's worth pausing on that. A photographer is someone whose whole job is noticing. The light on a face. The thing at the edge of the frame that everyone else walks past. Birgit spent her working life looking carefully at the world. The tragedy of her story is how long it took for anyone to look that carefully at what happened to her.

She had a daughter, Yasmin. [SRC: 6] And in the summer of nineteen eighty-nine, she was in the middle of ending her marriage.

Her husband, Harald, was a businessman. The two of them were separating, and like most separations, it was still in the stage of practical details. Money. Property. Who keeps what. [SRC: 6]

Birgit also had an older brother. And her brother wasn't just anyone.

Wolfgang Sielaff was born in Hamburg in nineteen forty-two. [SRC: 12] He joined the Hamburg police in nineteen sixty, at eighteen, as a patrol officer in St. Pauli, the city's famous red-light district, where you learn very quickly how people lie. He moved to the criminal police. He taught criminology. He ran the city's drug squad.

And in nineteen eighty-two, he built something that had never existed in Germany before: a dedicated police unit for organized crime. The first of its kind in the country. [SRC: 12]

Then, in nineteen eighty-nine, Hamburg founded a new state criminal police office, its own equivalent of a state bureau of investigation. And the man chosen to lead it was Wolfgang Sielaff. [SRC: 12]

So understand the timing. In the very year his sister disappeared, Wolfgang Sielaff had just become the head of criminal investigation for Germany's second-largest city. Murders, organized crime, missing persons. If something terrible happened in Hamburg, his people were the ones sent to find out why.

That's the backdrop. A photographer. Her daughter. A marriage coming apart. And a brother who knew, better than almost anyone in Germany, what it looks like when a disappearance is not what it seems.

There's one more person to place in this picture, and for now he stays in the background, exactly where he wanted to be.

In the neighbourhood around the Meiers' home, some of the families had their gardens looked after by the same man. A gardener. He worked at the Lüneburg cemetery, tending graves, and took private jobs on the side. [SRC: 5]

And at some point before that summer, Birgit had met him once. At a party. [SRC: 5]

That's all. A face at a party. A man trimming hedges a few houses away.

On the evening of Sunday, August thirteenth, nineteen eighty-nine, Birgit's husband was due to come by the house to talk through the details of the separation. [SRC: 6]

When he got there, Birgit was gone.

## 5. THE DAY (5:30–13:30)
[SLOW]

It's hard to describe the particular quiet of a house someone has just left.

Lights may be on. A cup on the table. A handbag where it always is. Everything looks normal, and that's exactly what makes it wrong. Because the person who belongs in the middle of all of it isn't there.

Investigators never established exactly what happened inside Birgit's home that night. What they knew was this. On the night of August thirteenth into August fourteenth, nineteen eighty-nine, Birgit Meier disappeared from her house in Lüneburg. [SRC: 1] She didn't leave a note. She didn't call anyone. She didn't turn up anywhere.

Her daughter, Yasmin, reported her missing. [SRC: 7]

[NORMAL]

Now put yourself in the position of the local police in a quiet town like Lüneburg in nineteen eighty-nine. An adult woman, in the middle of a divorce, has left her house without a word. What do you think?

They thought what police very often think, and there's a reason it's often right. They had two theories, and they both seemed obvious. [SRC: 1]

The first was that Birgit had left on her own. Possibly that she had taken her own life. A painful separation, emotional strain. It happens, and police see it far more often than they see murder.

The second was the husband. The two of them were separating. He was due at the house that very night. In any disappearance where a marriage is breaking down, the spouse is the first person investigators look at.

Two theories. Both reasonable. Both, as it would turn out, completely wrong.

And there's a hidden cost to starting with the wrong theory. If you believe a woman has left on her own, you don't seal her house as a crime scene. You don't canvass the street for strange cars. You don't ask the neighbours who came and went that night. You wait for her to call. Every hour spent waiting for a call that won't come is an hour the real trail goes cold.

[SLOW]

Fifty kilometres to the south-east, something else was happening that summer.

The Göhrde is a huge state forest in Lower Saxony, an old royal hunting ground of oak and beech and pine, criss-crossed by sandy tracks. In nineteen eighty-nine it was a place people went to walk, to cycle, to get away from the city.

On Sunday, May twenty-first, nineteen eighty-nine, a married couple from Bergedorf, on the edge of Hamburg, drove out to the Göhrde for a walk. Ursula Reinold was forty-five. Her husband, Peter, was fifty-one. [SRC: 8] Investigators later believed they had gone to a sunny clearing deep in the forest, perhaps for a picnic.

They never came home. For seven weeks, nobody knew where they were.

On July twelfth, berry pickers found them, hidden in a hollow in the ground. [SRC: 8]

And on that same day, July twelfth, another couple went into the same forest. Ingrid Warmbier, forty-six, and Bernd-Michael Köpping, forty-three. [SRC: 8] They were killed too.

Two couples, in the same forest, in the space of a few weeks. In both cases, the killer took the victims' car and abandoned it later in a small town with a railway station. One of them, a Toyota, was left in the spa town of Bad Bevensen, about thirty kilometres from the forest. [SRC: 8] Police believed one person had killed all four. The newspapers called them the Göhrde murders. The forest picked up a darker nickname, the forest of the dead.

Nobody connected the Göhrde murders to the disappearance of a photographer in Lüneburg. Why would they? One was a double killing in a forest, the other a missing woman in the middle of a divorce. Different places, different police files.

Hold on to the Göhrde forest. It matters later.

[NORMAL]

Back in Lüneburg, one man refused to accept either of the easy theories from the first day.

Wolfgang Sielaff knew his sister. She was not, in his view, someone who would walk away from her daughter without a word. And Wolfgang knew disappearances. He had spent his career watching cases like this. He knew how often "she probably just left" turns out to be the most expensive assumption in a police investigation.

From the very beginning, he treated it as what he believed it was. A crime. [SRC: 1]

But there was a problem, and it's one of the quiet structural traps in this story. Wolfgang Sielaff was a senior police officer in Hamburg. Lüneburg is in a different German state, Lower Saxony, with its own police, its own prosecutors, and its own way of doing things. Wolfgang had no authority there. He was a family member. A very well-informed, very persistent family member, but a family member.

And as anyone who has ever tried to tell a professional how to do their job knows, being right isn't the same as being listened to.

There's an awkwardness to it, too, that's easy to underestimate. Imagine you're a local detective in Lüneburg. A missing-persons case lands on your desk, and within days the head of Hamburg's criminal police is on the phone, telling you it's murder. Some officers would welcome the help. Others would hear pressure, or a big-city boss throwing his weight around, or a grieving brother who can't accept the obvious. Every one of those reactions is human. Not one of them helps find Birgit.

[SLOW]

The first days of a disappearance are everything. Witnesses remember what they saw. Neighbours remember which cars were parked where. Physical traces are fresh. Every day that goes by without a serious investigation, those things fade, get cleaned away, get forgotten.

The days went by.

And somewhere in Lüneburg, a man who had met Birgit once at a party was about to be asked where he had been that night. He was going to give an answer. And the answer was not going to add up.

## 6. FIRST RESPONSE (13:30–22:30)
[NORMAL]

Let's start with the husband, because that's where the investigation started.

Harald Meier was looked at closely. He had been due at the house. The marriage was ending. On paper, he was the classic suspect.

He did not kill his wife. He was never charged with anything. The evidence, when it finally came, pointed somewhere else entirely, and that needs to be said plainly, because he lived for years under the shadow of a suspicion he did not deserve.

The second theory, suicide, had a fatal flaw. If Birgit had taken her own life, where was she? No body. No vehicle abandoned at a lake, no note, no sign of her anywhere. People who die by suicide are, tragically, usually found. Birgit wasn't.

[SLOW]

Then, a few weeks into the investigation, a new name came up. [SRC: 5]

According to Birgit's husband, she had met a man at a party. That man turned out to be the gardener who worked for some of the Meiers' neighbours. The cemetery gardener. [SRC: 5]

Detectives went to talk to him.

He was forty years old. Married. Lived on the outskirts of Lüneburg. And he had a criminal record. [SRC: 4]

In nineteen seventy-one, as a young man, he had been convicted of rape and sentenced to five and a half years in prison. He was released in nineteen seventy-four. [SRC: 4]

So the police had in front of them a man with a conviction for a violent sexual crime, who knew Birgit, who worked a few houses away from her. They asked him the obvious question. Where were you that night?

He had an answer. He'd spent the evening at home with his wife. Later, he'd taken the dog out for a walk. [SRC: 9]

That was it. An evening at home, and a dog walk. An alibi that couldn't really be proven or disproven, provided by a man whose wife was his only witness.

Detectives have a name for this kind of alibi. It's a closed-circle alibi. The only people who can confirm it are the suspect and someone close to him. A spouse can be honest. A spouse can also be mistaken about times, or frightened, or simply unaware of what her husband did after he walked out of the door with the dog. A closed-circle alibi doesn't clear anyone. It just means you have to check everything around it.

And the dog walk itself is a gap. Nobody watches a man walk his dog at night. For how long was he out? Which way did he go? Did he come back on foot, or by car? Those questions have answers, if someone asks them on the first day.

And there was something else. Something he didn't mention.

At the time Birgit disappeared, the gardener was on sick leave from work. [SRC: 9] He was not at the cemetery. He was free during the day, with no one keeping track of where he was. He did not tell the police that.

And the police, as far as the record shows, didn't find out, or didn't ask. [SRC: 9]

[NORMAL]

Think about how this looks from the outside. A man with a conviction for violent rape. A personal connection to the missing woman. A thin alibi from his wife. And an undisclosed sick leave. Any one of those things might mean nothing. Together, they are exactly the pattern that a seasoned investigator is trained to push on.

Nobody pushed. [SRC: 9]

It's worth being specific about what pushing would have looked like, because none of it was exotic. Call the cemetery and ask whether he was at work that week. Speak to his wife separately, and ask what time he went out with the dog and what time he came back. Ask the neighbours he gardened for whether they'd seen him near the Meiers' house. Look at his car. These are the first pages of any detective's training. The record doesn't show that they were done thoroughly. [SRC: 9]

The gardener was questioned, and the gardener went home.

And remember what else was happening that summer. Fifty kilometres away, a large team of detectives was hunting whoever had killed four people in the Göhrde forest. They were looking for a man who could overpower two adults at once, who knew the back roads of the region, and who was comfortable with weapons. They were looking for him in the forest, in the villages, at the railway stations where the stolen cars had been left.

Nobody on that team, as far as the record shows, was told that a man with a conviction for a violent sexual crime, and a thin alibi in a missing-woman case, was living less than an hour away. The two investigations ran side by side, in the same summer, and never touched.

Back in Hamburg, Wolfgang Sielaff was not happy. He tried, again and again, to point the investigators in Lüneburg at the open questions and discrepancies he could see in the file. According to later accounts, his suggestions were taken up half-heartedly, if at all. [SRC: 4]

[COMMENT TRIGGER #1]
Here's something worth thinking about. When a senior detective's own family member goes missing in another jurisdiction, should he have a say in the investigation? Or is keeping him at arm's length exactly what protects a case from bias? Tell me where you land.
[/COMMENT TRIGGER]

[SLOW]

And so the year turned. Nineteen eighty-nine became nineteen ninety. On television, the Berlin Wall had come down, and Germany was a country in the middle of reinventing itself. In early nineteen ninety, Birgit's case was even featured on a national television crime-appeal programme, asking the public for help. [SRC: 10]

For a German family in nineteen ninety, that programme was the last resort. It went out to millions of homes. Detectives sat in the studio, waiting by the phones. If someone in the country had seen Birgit, or seen something on the night of August thirteenth, this was the moment they'd be asked to say so.

No one came forward with what was needed.

Nineteen ninety became nineteen ninety-one. Then nineteen ninety-two. Birgit's daughter grew up without her mother. Her brother kept his own copy of the questions in his head. And the gardener kept tending the graves at the Lüneburg cemetery.

For four years, very little happened.

Then, in Lüneburg, the prosecutor's office got a new face.

## 7. THE MIDPOINT TURN (22:30–26:00)
[SLOW]

In nineteen ninety-three, a new public prosecutor took a fresh look at the file on Birgit Meier. [SRC: 5] And for the first time, someone in Lüneburg looked at the gardener the way Wolfgang Sielaff had been looking at him for four years.

A formal murder investigation was opened against him. A judge signed a warrant to search his house and property, for a body, and for weapons. [SRC: 9]

And then something happened that, more than thirty years later, is still hard to believe.

Before the search, the police told him it was coming. By telephone. [SRC: 9]

The man they were about to search as a murder suspect was given advance notice. And he did exactly what you would expect. He ran.

Who made that call, and why, has never been fully explained in public. It may have been carelessness. A misunderstanding about procedure. An officer treating a search like an appointment. But whatever the reason, the effect was the same. Four years after he should have been looked at properly, the one moment when police finally moved against him began with him being told they were coming.

If you're keeping count, that's three opportunities lost. The alibi nobody checked. The sick leave nobody asked about. And now, a warning phone call before a murder search.

When officers arrived, the gardener was gone.

His name was Kurt-Werner Wichmann. [SRC: 4]

[PAUSE]

What the police found inside his house that day is the moment this case changes shape.

On the upper floor, there was a room with a door that had been padded and soundproofed. [SRC: 9]

In the basement, there was a section of wall that had clearly been built recently. [SRC: 9]

Specially trained cadaver dogs, dogs that alert to the scent of human remains, were brought onto the property. They alerted. More than once. [SRC: 5] But the officers didn't find a body.

They found weapons. Guns. A crossbow. They found stun devices. They found sedatives. And they found handcuffs. [SRC: 5]

Take those items one at a time and most of them have an innocent explanation. People own guns. People collect crossbows. People have trouble sleeping. But put them together, in the house of a man with a conviction for a violent sexual crime, alongside a room built so that nobody outside could hear what happened inside it, and investigators saw something else. Not a hobby. A plan.

Then, in the garden, they found something buried in the ground. A car. A bright red, nearly new Ford sports coupe, buried in the earth on his property. On the back seat there appeared to be blood. [SRC: 5]

It wasn't Birgit's car. It had been leased long after she disappeared. [SRC: 5] Which raised a different and much darker question. If it wasn't about Birgit, what was a buried car with blood on the seat doing in this man's garden?

[NORMAL]

Wichmann didn't stay free for long. He drove south, putting the length of the country between himself and Lüneburg. In April nineteen ninety-three, more than five hundred kilometres away, near the city of Heilbronn in Baden-Württemberg, he was involved in a traffic accident while on the run. Police found weapons in his vehicle, and he was arrested. [SRC: 4]

He was taken into custody. Finally, after four years, the man Wolfgang Sielaff had been pointing at was in a cell, facing questions he couldn't walk away from.

Ten days later, on April twenty-fifth, nineteen ninety-three, Kurt-Werner Wichmann took his own life in his cell. [SRC: 3] He was forty-three.

He never answered a single question about Birgit Meier.

And what happened next would be even more damaging to the case than his death.

[AD BREAK OK]

## 8. COMPLICATIONS (26:00–37:00)
[NORMAL]

So by the spring of nineteen ninety-three, police had a prime suspect, a house full of disturbing evidence, and a soundproofed room. What they didn't have was Birgit, or anyone left alive to tell them where she was.

[HALFWAY RESET ENDS]

And the question has sharpened. It's no longer just who took Birgit. It's how a case with a suspect this strong could still end up with her missing for another twenty-four years.

The answer starts with a principle in German law.

In Germany, criminal proceedings are against a person. When the suspect dies, the proceedings end. There is no trial of the dead, no posthumous verdict. The file against Kurt-Werner Wichmann was closed. [SRC: 4]

That principle has good reasons behind it. A dead person cannot defend himself. But it had a consequence in this case that nobody seems to have thought through.

Birgit was still missing. Her case had been, in practical terms, a case against Wichmann. When the case against him closed, the urgency around her disappeared with it.

[SLOW]

And then came the decision that would cost this case more than anything else.

The evidence from the case was ordered destroyed. [SRC: 9]

Think about everything that had come out of that house. The material from the soundproofed room. The weapons. The handcuffs. The red car from the garden, with what looked like blood on the back seat. Traces that in nineteen ninety-three could tell investigators little, but that twenty years later, with modern DNA analysis, might have told them everything.

Almost all of it was disposed of. [SRC: 9]

It's worth being careful here about blame. Destroying evidence in a closed case was, in some contexts, routine administrative practice. Storage space is finite. Files close. But there's a difference between routine and wise. Birgit's body had never been found. A woman was still missing. And the man police believed responsible had been searched with cadaver dogs that alerted on his own property.

The box should have stayed on the shelf. Instead, the box was emptied.

There's an irony in this, too. In Germany, murder has no statute of limitations. A murder can be prosecuted ten, thirty, fifty years later. The law itself assumes that the answer might come late. But the law can only work with the evidence that still exists. A country that never stops the clock on murder had, in this case, thrown away the means of solving one.

[NORMAL]

Wolfgang Sielaff did not stop thinking about his sister. But he was still a working police officer, with a city's worth of crime on his desk. The case belonged to another state. And now it was closed.

His career kept rising. In nineteen ninety-seven, he became Hamburg's most senior uniformed police officer, the state police inspector, and with it, the city's deputy police president. [SRC: 12] He was, by any measure, one of the most powerful police officers in Germany.

And none of that power reached across the state line into Lower Saxony, into a closed file about his sister.

In two thousand and two, Wolfgang Sielaff retired. [SRC: 12]

Most people who retire after forty-two years of police work want rest. Wolfgang went to work for the Weisser Ring, the White Ring, Germany's largest charity for victims of crime. [SRC: 12] For ten years he chaired its Hamburg branch, supporting families who had lost someone to violence. Families like his own.

And at the same time, a retired senior detective did something that almost no retired detective does. He opened his own investigation. Into his own sister's case. [SRC: 1]

He didn't do it alone. Over the following years, he gathered a team. [SRC: 4] Reinhard Chedor, who would himself go on to lead Hamburg's state criminal police office. [SRC: 13] Professor Klaus Püschel, one of Germany's best-known forensic pathologists, head of forensic medicine in Hamburg. Gerhard Strate, a prominent criminal defence lawyer. And Martin Köhnke. People with decades of experience, and nothing to gain except an answer.

A private task force. Retired professionals, meeting to work a case the state had closed.

[SLOW]

In two thousand and three, Wolfgang finally got access to the files from his sister's case. [SRC: 9]

He'd waited fourteen years to read them. And what he found, as he later described it, appalled him. Lead after lead that had been followed half-heartedly. Gaps. And the destruction order. The evidence from Wichmann's house, the material that might have held the answer, was gone. [SRC: 9]

Imagine sitting down, in retirement, to finally read the file on your sister's disappearance, and discovering that the most important evidence in it no longer exists.

Most people would have stopped there.

[NORMAL]

The team didn't. Instead, they started rebuilding the case from what was left. The interview notes. The photographs from nineteen ninety-three. Property records. The layout of the house. Wichmann's movements. His history.

This is what cold-case work really looks like, and it's nothing like television. No sudden flash of insight in a dark room. It's weeks spent cross-referencing dates in old files. It's finding the officer who took a statement in nineteen eighty-nine and asking what he remembers that he didn't write down. It's comparing photographs of a building taken years apart to see what changed. It's writing formal letters to prosecutors and waiting months for answers. For a team of people in their sixties and seventies, working without pay and without any official power, it was years of that.

And the more they looked, the larger the picture became. This wasn't just a man who might have killed one woman. The weapons, the soundproofed room, the buried car, the handcuffs. To the investigators studying it, it looked like the property of someone who had planned to hurt people, and possibly had, more than once.

Which brought the team back, inevitably, to the summer of nineteen eighty-nine. And to the forest fifty kilometres away, where two couples had been killed in the same few weeks that Birgit disappeared.

The Göhrde murders were still unsolved.

Was it possible?

[SLOW]

For years, the private team pushed, wrote, asked, and argued. Wolfgang knew how police institutions worked from the inside. He knew which doors to knock on and how to make a case for reopening a file. Slowly, attitudes began to shift.

But they kept running into the same wall. The evidence was gone. You can't test what doesn't exist.

Unless something had survived.

Somewhere in Germany, in a forensic institute in the city of Hanover, there was a basement full of old materials. And sitting in it, overlooked during the great clear-out of nineteen ninety-three, was the one object from Wichmann's house that nobody had remembered to destroy.

## 9. THE BREAK (37:00–43:00)
[SLOW]

It was a pair of handcuffs. [SRC: 4]

In the autumn of twenty sixteen, the handcuffs from Wichmann's house were found at the Institute of Legal Medicine in Hanover. [SRC: 4] Somehow, when the evidence from his case was disposed of, this one item had been missed. It had been sitting there for twenty-three years.

And on those handcuffs was a tiny trace of blood. [SRC: 9]

In nineteen ninety-three, a drop of blood on a piece of metal could tell you very little. By twenty sixteen, DNA analysis could work with traces that small and that old.

The blood was tested.

It was Birgit's. [SRC: 9]

[PAUSE]

Sit with what that means for a moment. For twenty-seven years, there had been no physical evidence that Birgit Meier had ever been anywhere near Kurt-Werner Wichmann. Just a party, a weak alibi, and a brother's conviction.

Now there was her blood, on handcuffs, taken from his house.

And think about how close that object came to not existing. An order had gone out to destroy the evidence in this case. Someone, somewhere in the chain, either never received it for this one item, or overlooked a single bag on a shelf in Hanover. A clerical slip. The kind of mistake that, in almost any other story, would be a footnote about sloppy record-keeping.

In this story, that mistake is the reason Birgit was found.

[NORMAL]

With that result, everything changed. The police in Lüneburg committed fully to the case. The question was no longer whether Wichmann was involved. It was where Birgit was.

The team went back to the property where Wichmann had lived. A house on the outskirts of Lüneburg, with a garage. [SRC: 3]

Inside that garage was an inspection pit. A trench in the concrete floor, so a person could stand beneath a car and work on it. Thousands of German garages have them.

But this one was odd. It was shallow. About ninety centimetres deep, roughly three feet. [SRC: 4] Too shallow for a grown man to stand in comfortably and work. Why would anyone build a mechanic's pit that a mechanic couldn't use?

Unless the pit had once been deeper. And something had been put in the bottom of it, and covered with concrete.

There was a second detail. The concrete plate at the bottom of the pit was a lighter colour than the concrete around it. [SRC: 14] Concrete poured at different times, from different batches, cures to slightly different shades. Somebody had put that floor in later.

A shallow pit. A newer floor. In the garage of a man whose house had made cadaver dogs alert twenty-four years earlier.

[SLOW]

In late September, twenty seventeen, the excavation began. [SRC: 3] And we're back where this story started. The garage. The jackhammer. The pile of broken concrete in the driveway. And Wolfgang Sielaff, standing at the door.

A forensic excavation is not a demolition. It's slow, deliberate, and almost painfully careful. The concrete comes up in sections. Every layer underneath is removed by hand, sieved, photographed, and logged, because whatever is down there may be the only evidence anyone will ever have. Professor Püschel, the forensic pathologist on Wolfgang's team, had spent his career at scenes like this one. [SRC: 4] The difference was that this time, the person they were looking for was the sister of the man who had brought them all together.

Beneath the floor of the inspection pit, the team found human remains. [SRC: 3]

DNA analysis confirmed it. They were the remains of Birgit Meier. [SRC: 3]

Reports differ on whether the find was made on the twenty-seventh or the twenty-ninth of September. [SRC: 3, 4] Either way, it was twenty-eight years and six weeks after she had disappeared from her home. She had been a few kilometres away, under a garage floor, for almost the entire time.

The brother who had spent a career finding other people's missing had finally found his own.

But in that same moment, the investigators faced a much bigger question. Because if Wichmann had done this, and hidden it this well, what else had he done?

## 10. RESOLUTION (43:00–49:00)
[NORMAL]

So, the question this whole story turns on. How did a woman whose own brother ran a criminal police force stay missing for twenty-eight years, a few kilometres from home?

Part of the answer is in what came next.

On January nineteenth, twenty eighteen, the results of the post-mortem examination at Hannover Medical School were made public. Birgit Meier had been shot. [SRC: 3]

[SLOW]

And then, the Göhrde forest.

In December twenty seventeen, Lüneburg police announced that Kurt-Werner Wichmann was now the prime suspect in the Göhrde murders. [SRC: 8]

The breakthrough came from evidence that had been collected in nineteen eighty-nine and, crucially, had not been destroyed. When the victims' car was examined that summer, investigators used adhesive strips to lift fibres from the seats. Standard practice at the time. Those strips had been kept.

Nearly thirty years later, a fresh team re-examined them and found skin cells. [SRC: 8] Modern DNA analysis could read them. The DNA belonged to Kurt-Werner Wichmann. In twenty eighteen, police said they had found two separate traces of his DNA in the victims' car. [SRC: 8]

Go back to the victims and say their names again. Ursula and Peter Reinold. Ingrid Warmbier and Bernd-Michael Köpping. Four people who went into a forest on summer days in nineteen eighty-nine and never came out. Their families had waited twenty-eight years for even this much. A name, and the knowledge that it belonged to a man who had died long ago.

Investigators also pointed to another kind of trace. Dog hairs were found in the victims' cars. None of the victims owned a dog. [SRC: 13] Wichmann did. Remember his alibi for the night Birgit disappeared: an evening at home, and then a walk with the dog.

The Lüneburg police president, Robert Kruse, put the conclusion in stark terms. In the investigators' view, the man they were dealing with was a serial killer, one who may have committed crimes beyond Germany's borders. [SRC: 3] By the autumn of twenty eighteen, investigators were linking Wichmann to as many as five deaths. [SRC: 8]

And the review didn't stop at the Göhrde. Once police accepted that a suspected serial killer had lived and worked in Lüneburg for decades, every unsolved case in the region looked different. Reporting at the time described investigators looking again at around twenty-one unsolved killings in Lüneburg and the surrounding area, comparing each one with what was known about Wichmann's movements and methods. [SRC: 14] That doesn't mean he was responsible for them. It means that, for the first time, someone was asking.

[NORMAL]

And now, the part of the story that has to be told with discipline.

Kurt-Werner Wichmann was never tried for the murder of Birgit Meier. He was never tried for the Göhrde murders. He died in nineteen ninety-three, before he could be charged with any of it. Under German law, there is no trial of the dead.

The evidence in Birgit's case is about as strong as evidence gets without a courtroom: her blood on handcuffs from his house, and her remains under a garage on his former property. Police and prosecutors regard him as her killer.

The Göhrde murders are different. Officially, they remain unsolved. Wichmann is the prime suspect, based on DNA from the victims' car. [SRC: 8]

If he had a defence lawyer, what would that lawyer say? That DNA in a car shows contact, not murder. That the Göhrde investigation has never been tested by a court. That a man who cannot respond should not be convicted by a press conference. Those are fair points, and they're why this video uses the words investigators use. Prime suspect. Police believe.

[SLOW]

And there's one more thread that keeps the Göhrde file open today.

Investigators noticed something about the logistics of the forest murders. As they've described it, the evidence suggests Wichmann drove into the Göhrde in his own vehicle, but left in the victims' car. [SRC: 8]

One man. Two cars. Someone else had to drive one of them.

Think about how it would have worked. If you arrive at a forest in your own car, and leave in someone else's, your own car is still sitting in the forest. Either someone drove you there and dropped you off, or someone came back later to collect your car. Either way, there's a second person. And it's a pattern: both stolen cars ended up in small towns with railway stations, as though whoever left them there needed a train to get home.

Police have said they believe Wichmann may have had an accomplice. [SRC: 8] Someone who knew. Someone who helped. Someone who, unlike Wichmann, might still be alive.

[AD BREAK OK]

## 11. AFTERMATH + MEANING (49:00–53:00)
[NORMAL]

A cold-case team at the Lüneburg police has continued working the Göhrde murders and the wider question of what else Wichmann may have done. [SRC: 8] Appeals have gone out. Phone numbers have been checked. The second person, if there was one, has not been publicly identified.

Outside the police, Reinhard Chedor, the former head of Hamburg's state criminal police office and a member of Wolfgang's original team, has kept going too. His group has looked at unsolved cases across northern Germany. They have raised questions about killings near Cuxhaven between nineteen seventy-seven and nineteen eighty-six, and about a double murder in Bremen and Bremerhaven in September nineteen ninety-one, where, again, dog hairs were found on victims who had no dog. [SRC: 13] None of those cases has been officially linked to Wichmann. They are questions, raised by experienced investigators. Not conclusions.

But they show how far the shadow of one cemetery gardener might reach, and how much may still be unknown.

In twenty twenty-one, the story reached a global audience through a Netflix documentary series. And with that, Birgit Meier's name, which for decades had been a line in a closed German police file, travelled around the world.

[SLOW]

But let's bring it back to her.

Birgit Meier was a photographer. She spent her life noticing things other people missed. She was the mother of a daughter who reported her missing in nineteen eighty-nine, and then had to grow up and grow older without ever knowing what had happened.

Think about what twenty-eight years means for a daughter. Yasmin went from a young woman to middle age with a missing mother. Every birthday, every holiday, every milestone in her own life happened with that empty chair. Missing is a particular kind of grief, because it never gets to begin properly. There's no funeral. There's no grave to visit. There's only a question, and the small, unreasonable hope that comes with it. In September twenty seventeen, that question was finally answered. And the answer was that her mother had been a few kilometres away all along.

She was a younger sister. And her older brother, who was trained and paid to bring answers to strangers, spent the best part of three decades trying to bring one to his own family.

In twenty twenty-five, Germany awarded Wolfgang Sielaff the Federal Cross of Merit, one of the country's highest civilian honours. [SRC: 12] It's the kind of medal given for a lifetime of service. A large part of that service was done after he had officially stopped serving.

He did it without official authority. He did it in retirement. He did it after learning that the evidence he needed had been thrown away. He found people who would work for nothing. He kept knocking on doors that had been closed for twenty years. And in the end, the answer came out of the ground of a garage a few kilometres from where his sister had lived.

[NORMAL]

So, how did Birgit Meier stay missing for twenty-eight years?

Here's the most honest answer the record gives.

It wasn't for lack of a suspect. Investigators had the right name within weeks. It was the follow-through. An alibi that went unchecked. Sick leave nobody asked about. A brother's warnings treated as interference. A suspect warned before a search. A closed file, and an order to destroy everything in it.

And then, against all of that, one forgotten object. A pair of handcuffs in a basement in Hanover. Because somebody, somewhere, didn't finish the job of throwing things away.

What's striking about this case is that the system didn't fail because it was too weak to catch a killer. It caught him, briefly. It failed in the decisions made after. And it was rescued by a mistake.
