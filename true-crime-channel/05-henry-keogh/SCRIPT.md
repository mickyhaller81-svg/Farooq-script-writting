December twenty-second, twenty fourteen. Adelaide, South Australia. High summer, three days before Christmas.

## 3. THE WORLD BEFORE (1:30–5:00)
[NORMAL]

To understand how we got to that prison gate, we have to go back twenty years, to the autumn of nineteen ninety-four, and to the woman at the centre of it all.

Her name was Anna-Jane Cheney.

On March fifteenth, nineteen ninety-four, she turned twenty-nine. [SRC: 2]

Anna-Jane was a lawyer. And not just any lawyer. At twenty-nine, she was Head of Professional Conduct at the Law Society of South Australia. [SRC: 3] It's a serious job. When members of the public complained about lawyers, when a solicitor was accused of misconduct, of dishonesty, of breaking the profession's rules, the complaint came across her desk. Anna-Jane's working life was about holding people to account.

She came from a well-known Adelaide family. Her father, Dr Kevin Cheney, was a respected specialist in haematology pathology, the medicine of blood. [SRC: 3] She was, as one later account put it, a daughter of the establishment, and well liked. [SRC: 3]

There's something in that detail about her father that's worth holding on to. Anna-Jane grew up in the house of a pathologist. A man whose professional life was the careful, microscopic reading of what the human body can tell you. Years later, the question of her death would come down entirely to pathology. To one doctor's reading of what her body could tell him.

[NORMAL]

In the autumn of nineteen ninety-four, Anna-Jane was engaged.

Her fiancé was Henry Keogh. He was thirty-eight, nine years older than her, and he worked in finance. [SRC: 3] He had business interests in insurance. They lived together in a house in Magill, a leafy suburb in Adelaide's north-east.

Their wedding was five weeks away. [SRC: 3]

From the outside, they were a successful couple in their prime. She was rising fast in the legal world. He was a businessman. They had a home together in a good suburb, and a date set.

Think about what five weeks before a wedding looks like. A seating plan. Dress fittings. Phone calls about flowers and caterers. Relatives booking flights. It's a time when a life is about to tip over into its next chapter, and every day is filled with small, specific, future plans.

And there's one more set of documents from that time that will matter enormously. Not wedding invitations.

Insurance policies.

In the months before the wedding, five life insurance policies had been taken out on Anna-Jane's life. [SRC: 7] The person who would benefit if she died was Henry Keogh. And Anna-Jane's signature on the applications, the court would later hear, had been written by him. [SRC: 7]

Keep that in mind. Both sides of this story will explain it very differently.

Three days after her twenty-ninth birthday, on the evening of Friday, March eighteenth, nineteen ninety-four, Anna-Jane Cheney ran a bath in the house in Magill.

She never got out of it.

## 5. THE DAY (5:30–13:30)
[SLOW]

What follows is what was reported and what was put before the courts about the evening of March eighteenth. Some of it comes from Henry Keogh's own account. Remember that as you hear it, and remember too that his account has never been disproved in a court that's still standing.

That evening, Anna-Jane had been drinking. Not to excess. Investigators would later describe her as mildly intoxicated. [SRC: 3] She ran a bath.

At some point, Henry Keogh went to the bathroom and found her in the water. Motionless. Unresponsive. [SRC: 3]

Emergency services were called. Nothing could be done. Anna-Jane Cheney was dead.

Picture what follows a death like that in a family home. Paramedics working, and then stopping. Police officers arriving to take details, because every sudden death has to be reported. A doctor. Phone calls to parents, late on a Friday night. A wedding that, an hour earlier, had been five weeks away, and now never would be.

In the middle of all of it, a man who said he had walked into a bathroom and found the woman he was about to marry under the water.

[NORMAL]

Now, here's something that often gets lost in retellings of this case.

The police officers who first attended the house in Magill didn't see a murder. According to accounts of that night, they saw a tragedy. An accident. A young woman who'd had a few drinks, relaxed in a warm bath, fallen asleep, slipped under, and drowned. [SRC: 3]

Accidental drownings in baths are rare, but they're not unheard of. Alcohol, warm water, tiredness. It happens. And there was nothing at the scene, in those first hours, that screamed homicide. No signs of a fight in the house. No obvious injuries on Anna-Jane that told officers someone had attacked her.

If the story had stopped there, Anna-Jane's death would have been recorded as a terrible accident five weeks before her wedding. Her family would have grieved, Henry Keogh would have grieved, and you would never have heard any of these names.

But the story didn't stop there. Because every sudden, unexplained death in South Australia goes to one place. The state's forensic pathology service. And in nineteen ninety-four, the man in charge of that service, the man who would examine Anna-Jane's body and tell the authorities how she died, was Dr Colin Manock.

[SLOW]

An autopsy, at its best, is one of the most rigorous procedures in medicine. A forensic pathologist looks at the body, inside and out. They document every mark. They take tissue samples, look at them under a microscope. They consider the person's medical history: allergies, conditions, medications. Ideally, they look at the scene where the person died, because a bathroom or a bedroom can explain things a body alone can't.

Dr Manock examined Anna-Jane. And on her left leg, he found bruises.

Small bruises, on the lower part of the leg. [SRC: 6]

Bruises are some of the most ordinary marks on a human body. People bump into furniture, knock against car doors, catch a shin on a step, and never notice. Most of us have a bruise or two somewhere at any given time.

But Dr Manock looked at the pattern of these bruises and came to a conclusion that would change everything.

He saw fingers.

## 6. FIRST RESPONSE (13:30–22:30)
[NORMAL]

Here was Dr Manock's theory, as it was put to the court. [SRC: 6]

The marks on Anna-Jane's lower leg were not random bruises. They were, in his opinion, the marks of a hand gripping her leg. Grip marks. Fingertip bruises. And if someone had gripped her leg, he reasoned, there was a method of drowning that fitted.

Imagine a person lying in a bath. If someone standing at the end of the bath suddenly grabs their legs and lifts them up, the person's head and shoulders slide down under the water. They're caught off guard. They can't easily sit up, because the person holding their legs is keeping them off balance. It can happen very quickly, and it may leave very few other marks.

That, in the forensic opinion that went before the jury, is what happened to Anna-Jane Cheney. Someone took her by the left leg and pulled her under. [SRC: 6]

But for that theory to point at murder, the bruises had to be fresh. If they were days old, they could be from anything. So Dr Manock went further. He told the court the bruises had been inflicted within about four hours of Anna-Jane's death. [SRC: 12] And another pathologist, Dr Ross James, supported him, expressing the view that the bruises had been sustained within an even shorter time before she died. [SRC: 12]

Within hours of death. On the leg. In the shape of a grip. In a house where only two people were present.

To a jury, that sounds like science. Precise, confident, expert. It would take nearly twenty years for a court to say how far beyond the science it really went.

There's a well-documented effect at work in courtrooms, and in everyday life. It's called authority bias. When someone with a title, a white coat, and decades of experience tells us something with confidence, we tend to believe it, especially when it's in a field we don't understand. A juror can follow an argument about insurance policies. A juror can judge whether a witness seems honest. But a juror has no way to check whether a bruise is four hours old or four days old. They have to trust the expert. And in nineteen ninety-five, the expert was the most senior forensic pathologist in the state.

And there was only one other person in the house.

[SLOW]

Dr Manock's opinion turned an accident into a homicide investigation. And when detectives began looking at Henry Keogh, they found the insurance policies.

Five life insurance policies on Anna-Jane's life. The prosecution put their combined value at around one point one five million Australian dollars. [SRC: 7] The beneficiary was Henry Keogh. And the signatures on the applications weren't Anna-Jane's. Keogh had signed her name. [SRC: 7]

To the prosecution, this was the motive. A man who stood to collect more than a million dollars if his fiancée died, who had signed her name on the paperwork himself, and whose fiancée was then found dead in the bath, with what the state's chief pathologist said were grip marks on her leg.

Henry Keogh had a very different explanation. And it deserves to be heard in full, because a jury heard it too.

He said Anna-Jane knew about the policies. She knew he was going to sign her name on the applications, and she was fine with it. [SRC: 7] He worked in insurance. He said the policies were taken out to keep insurance agencies he had set up from lapsing, a business arrangement. And he said the amount that could actually have been claimed was much lower than the prosecution claimed, closer to four hundred thousand dollars. [SRC: 7]

Two stories. Same documents. And that's how this case would stay, in one form or another, for the next thirty years.

[NORMAL]

There was one more piece of context the jury would hear about. Some members of Anna-Jane's family had come to distrust Henry Keogh. They had suspicions about his faithfulness to her. [SRC: 3] They did not believe her death was an accident.

On July seventh, nineteen ninety-four, Henry Keogh was taken into custody, charged with the murder of Anna-Jane Cheney. [SRC: 2]

[SLOW]

His first trial took place in February nineteen ninety-five.

The jury heard the evidence. The bruises. The grip-mark theory. The insurance. Keogh's explanation.

And they couldn't agree. The jury was hung. No verdict. [SRC: 2]

It's worth sitting with that fact, because it's rarely emphasized. The first group of ordinary South Australians who heard the whole case could not all agree that Henry Keogh was guilty beyond reasonable doubt.

So the State tried him again.

Later in nineteen ninety-five, a second jury heard the case. Again, at the centre of it was Dr Manock and his reading of the marks on Anna-Jane's leg.

The appeal court would later describe exactly how central it was. Dr Manock's evidence was not conclusive, the judges said, but it strongly supported the prosecution case of murder. And during his closing address, the prosecutor returned to Dr Manock's evidence again and again. [SRC: 12]

This time, the jury was unanimous.

Guilty of murder. [SRC: 2]

Henry Keogh was sentenced to life imprisonment, with a non-parole period of twenty-five years. [SRC: 2]

Here's a question worth pausing on. The first jury couldn't agree. The second jury convicted. When the same evidence produces two different results, how much should a single expert's opinion be allowed to decide someone's freedom? I'd like to know what you think.
[/COMMENT TRIGGER]

[NORMAL]

For the Cheney family, the verdict was an answer. Their daughter, their sister, hadn't just slipped under the water. A jury had found that someone had killed her, and that it was the man she had been about to marry.

For Henry Keogh, it was the beginning of twenty years in prison, insisting, every day of it, that he hadn't done it.

And at the centre of the whole case was one man's interpretation of a few bruises on a woman's leg. A man the jury trusted because he was the state's chief forensic pathologist, the most senior expert in his field in South Australia.

Which raises a question nobody on that jury seems to have been in a position to ask.

Who exactly was Dr Colin Manock?

## 7. THE MIDPOINT TURN (22:30–26:00)
[SLOW]

Colin Manock arrived in Adelaide in nineteen sixty-eight, and was appointed South Australia's Chief Forensic Pathologist. [SRC: 4]

He held a medical degree. But according to his critics, and according to the detailed account in Drew Rooke's twenty twenty-two book, *A Witness of Fact*, he did not have the specialist training in forensic pathology and histopathology that the job would normally demand. [SRC: 5]

Histopathology is the study of tissue under the microscope. It's the discipline that lets a pathologist examine a bruise and estimate how old it is. Whether it happened minutes before death, or days before. In a case built on bruises, it's arguably the most important skill in the room.

And how did Dr Manock eventually obtain his formal qualification in forensic medicine? According to Rooke's account, not through the years of training and written examinations most doctors face, but through an oral examination lasting about twenty minutes, several years after he had already arrived in Adelaide and taken charge. [SRC: 5]

[PAUSE]

Now consider the scale of what followed.

Dr Manock held the position from nineteen sixty-eight to nineteen ninety-five. Twenty-seven years. In that time, he performed more than ten thousand autopsies, and gave expert evidence in around four hundred criminal cases. [SRC: 4]

Four hundred cases. Murders. Manslaughters. Deaths in custody. In each one, juries heard from the state's top forensic expert, and in each one, they had every reason to believe him.

It's worth understanding the world he worked in. In the late nineteen-sixties, forensic pathology in Australia was a tiny field. A state like South Australia might have one senior forensic pathologist, maybe two. There was no independent second opinion down the hall. No peer reviewing each autopsy. Defence lawyers often couldn't afford their own experts, and when they could, there were few to choose from. The chief forensic pathologist wasn't just an expert witness. In practice, for many cases, he was the only expert witness.

In a system like that, one person's habits, assumptions and blind spots don't get corrected. They get repeated. Case after case. For twenty-seven years.

Over the years, his peers and the lawyers who studied his work would use words about him that this video will only repeat as theirs, not ours. Unqualified. Unreliable. Dogmatic. [SRC: 4]

His tenure ended in nineteen ninety-five. [SRC: 4] The same year Henry Keogh was convicted. The man whose opinion helped put Keogh in prison for life left the job just as Keogh's twenty years were beginning.

[SLOW]

So the marks on Anna-Jane Cheney's leg, the marks that turned an accident into a murder, that sent a man to prison for life, had been read by a pathologist whose training and methods would later be called into serious question.

And one detail sharpened that question even further. According to the later review of the case, Dr Manock never visited the scene in Magill. He never saw the bath. And his autopsy did not consider Anna-Jane's medical history in the way a careful examination should. [SRC: 6]

If you were Henry Keogh, sitting in a cell, believing you were innocent, this would be everything. But knowing something is wrong with the evidence and getting a court to listen are two very different things.

Henry Keogh was about to find out just how different.

[AD BREAK OK]

## 8. COMPLICATIONS (26:00–37:00)
[NORMAL]

By the late nineteen-nineties, Henry Keogh had a conviction, a life sentence, and serious doubts about the forensic evidence against him. What he didn't have was any legal way to put those doubts in front of a court.

[HALFWAY RESET ENDS]

And that's the question that drives the second half of this story. Not just "what happened in that bathroom," but this: if the evidence that convicted you might be wrong, and you've already used your appeal, how do you get back into court?

In Australia at the time, the answer was: you mostly couldn't.

[NORMAL]

Keogh had appealed his conviction in the normal way. That appeal failed. He tried to take his case to the High Court of Australia, the country's highest court. The High Court refused to hear it. [SRC: 8]

And that was it. In South Australia, as in the rest of the country, a convicted person got one appeal. After that, the courts were closed. The only remaining path was something called a petition for mercy: a request to the Governor, on the advice of the government, to refer the case back to the courts or to intervene. It wasn't a right. It was a request for a favour from politicians.

Henry Keogh's supporters filed one petition for mercy. It was refused. Then another. Then another. Four in all, each one arguing that the forensic evidence at his trial was unreliable. [SRC: 8]

None of them succeeded.

It's easy to see why governments were reluctant. A petition for mercy in a murder case lands on a politician's desk. Granting it means overruling a jury, and two levels of courts. It means telling a victim's family that the conviction they've relied on may be wrong. And it means, potentially, admitting that the state's own chief forensic pathologist got things badly wrong, which raises the question of every other case he touched. For any government, saying no is the safer answer. Saying yes opens a door that may not close.

So the answer kept being no.

[SLOW]

Meanwhile, outside the prison, questions about Dr Manock were growing.

After the Keogh trials, the South Australian coroner, Wayne Chivell, released a report that heavily criticised some of Dr Manock's autopsies in other cases. [SRC: 4]

And in two thousand, an academic named Dr Bob Moles, working with Associate Professor Bibi Sangha at Flinders University in Adelaide, began looking at the Keogh case. It became the start of a long-running project on miscarriages of justice. [SRC: 9]

In October two thousand and one, Moles and Sangha appeared on the ABC's flagship investigative programme, *Four Corners*. [SRC: 9] They argued, on national television, that Henry Keogh had been wrongly convicted. And they made a broader claim: that the state's chief forensic pathologist for almost thirty years had not been properly qualified, and had given evidence they described as plainly incorrect in a number of important cases. [SRC: 9]

Dr Moles would go on to write books about the case and about the wider system, including one titled *A State of Injustice*. [SRC: 9] The Flinders project became a long campaign, not just for Henry Keogh, but for the idea that a justice system needs a way to correct its own mistakes after the appeals have run out.

That broadcast should have been an earthquake. In a way, it was. The questions were now public. People in the legal profession in Adelaide were talking about them.

But public questions don't open prison doors. Henry Keogh stayed inside.

[NORMAL]

It's worth pausing on why the system was built like this, because it wasn't designed to be cruel.

The legal principle is called finality. At some point, a case has to end. Victims' families need to know it's over. Courts can't re-hear every case forever. If every conviction could be reopened indefinitely, the system would grind to a halt, and nobody would ever have certainty.

All of that is reasonable. Until you're the person in the cell, and the evidence that put you there starts to look wrong. Then finality looks less like a principle and more like a locked door with no handle on your side.

And on the other side of that door were the Cheney family, for whom the conviction was the one certainty they had. Every new petition, every television programme, every academic paper reopened something they believed had been settled. Finality protected them too.

[SLOW]

Years went by. Henry Keogh passed his fiftieth birthday in prison.

He has since spoken about what those years did to him. He described despair that turned into depression, and a long, dark struggle that lasted years. [SRC: 13] And then, eventually, a kind of surrender. "I only gained some peace," he later said, "when I came to terms with and accepted the reality of dying in prison." [SRC: 13]

Think about what that sentence means. Not that he'd stopped believing he was innocent. That he'd stopped believing anyone would ever agree. That the most realistic future in front of him was a cell, and then a prison hospital, and then a grave.

[If you or someone you know is struggling, please reach out to a local crisis line. Support is available.]

His supporters kept writing, arguing, campaigning. Experts from outside South Australia looked at the evidence.

One of them was Professor Derrick Pounder, a forensic pathologist from the University of Dundee in Scotland. His view of the nineteen ninety-four autopsy was blunt. He said it was inadequate, and that the detail and description in Dr Manock's report fell far below the standard expected at the time. [SRC: 1]

And on the bruises themselves, the foundation of the whole case, Professor Pounder wrote something devastating in its plainness. Of the bruises to the front of Anna-Jane's right shin, he said they "are typical of those which result from day to day living, and there is no objective reason to suggest otherwise." [SRC: 12]

Day-to-day living. The knocks and bumps every one of us collects without noticing. A coffee table. A car door. A step. In the opinion of a senior pathologist from the other side of the world, that is all the evidence could honestly show.

And there was something else. Photographs taken at the scene reportedly showed marks and swelling on Anna-Jane's body that, in the view of experts for the defence, might indicate the possibility of a severe allergic reaction. [SRC: 1] That raised an entirely different possibility about how she died, one that Dr Manock's autopsy, according to the critics, had never properly ruled out.

But none of this had anywhere to go. Not without a law that let a second appeal be heard.

[NORMAL]

In May twenty thirteen, South Australia's Parliament passed exactly that law.

The Statutes Amendment (Appeals) Act created something new in Australian law: a right to a second or subsequent appeal, if there was fresh and compelling evidence that a substantial miscarriage of justice had occurred. [SRC: 8] It was the first law of its kind anywhere in Australia. And it had been shaped, in large part, by the campaign around Henry Keogh. [SRC: 9]

Keogh applied under the new law almost immediately.

For the first time in nearly two decades, a court would hear the forensic evidence again. Properly. With experts on both sides.

[AD BREAK OK]

## 9. THE BREAK (37:00–43:00)
[SLOW]

The case went before the Full Court of the Court of Criminal Appeal of South Australia. Three judges. [SRC: 6]

The fresh evidence went to the heart of the prosecution's case: Dr Manock's opinion.

The defence experts argued that the bruises on Anna-Jane's leg could not be reliably dated to the time of her death. Bruises are not timestamps. Without proper microscopic examination, a bruise found at autopsy might be minutes old, or hours, or days. A mark from bumping into furniture days earlier could look, to an unwary eye, like it fitted a story.

They argued that the autopsy had been inadequate. That Dr Manock had not visited the scene, had not seen the bath, had not considered Anna-Jane's medical history properly. [SRC: 1, 6]

And they raised serious questions about the drowning theory itself: whether lifting a person's legs, in that particular bath, in that particular bathroom, would actually work the way the prosecution had described.

[PAUSE]

On December nineteenth, twenty fourteen, the court delivered its decision.

It set aside Henry Keogh's conviction for murder. [SRC: 1]

The court found there was fresh evidence suggesting that the forensic pathology evidence presented at his trial nearly twenty years earlier was flawed, and that his conviction amounted to a miscarriage of justice. [SRC: 1] The grip-mark evidence, the court found, was circumstantial evidence that could not, by itself, prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt. [SRC: 6]

Dr Manock's evidence about the age of the bruises, the claim that they were inflicted within hours of death, was now accepted to be incorrect. [SRC: 12] And his "mechanism of murder," the vivid picture of a man lifting a woman's legs to pull her under the water, the picture the jury had carried into the deliberation room, was described in the course of the appeal as no more than prejudicial speculation. [SRC: 12]

Prejudicial speculation. Two words, nearly twenty years too late.

To understand why this ruling mattered so much, think about the bar the new law set. It wasn't enough for Keogh's lawyers to show that a different expert disagreed with Dr Manock. Experts disagree all the time. The evidence had to be fresh, meaning it couldn't reasonably have been presented at the original trial. It had to be compelling, meaning reliable, substantial, and highly probative. And it had to show a substantial miscarriage of justice. That's a high bar, designed to stop every disappointed prisoner from relitigating their case forever. Henry Keogh cleared it.

It was the first time the new law had been used to quash a conviction. [SRC: 8]

[NORMAL]

But listen carefully to what the court did next, because this is where many retellings get it wrong.

The court did not declare Henry Keogh innocent. It did not say Anna-Jane's death was an accident. It did not say the prosecution's theory was impossible.

It ordered a retrial. [SRC: 1]

In other words, the court said: the trial you had was unfair, because the evidence it relied on was flawed. If the State still believes it can prove this case, it can try again, with sound evidence, in front of a new jury.

Imagine hearing that decision from both sides of the courtroom.

For Henry Keogh, it was the first time in twenty years that a court had said the evidence against him couldn't be trusted. The man who had made peace with dying in prison now had a chance of walking out of it.

For the Cheney family, it was the opposite. Twenty years after they had buried Anna-Jane, the conviction that told them what had happened to her was gone. Not replaced with a different answer. Just gone. And the man they believed had killed her was about to walk out into the summer sun.

Three days later, on December twenty-second, twenty fourteen, Henry Keogh was released on bail, after twenty years in custody. [SRC: 2]

And we're back at that prison gate, with the reporters and the summer heat.

The question now was simple. Would the State of South Australia try Henry Keogh for murder a third time?

## 10. RESOLUTION (43:00–49:00)
[NORMAL]

So, with the grip-mark evidence discredited, what did anyone actually know about what happened in that bathroom in March nineteen ninety-four?

The Director of Public Prosecutions had to answer that question in practical terms. Could the State prove, beyond reasonable doubt, without Dr Manock's opinion, that Anna-Jane Cheney was murdered?

On November thirteenth, twenty fifteen, the DPP gave its answer. It entered what's called a nolle prosequi. A formal notice that the prosecution would not proceed with a retrial. [SRC: 7]

The murder charge against Henry Keogh was dropped.

Think about the decision from the prosecutor's chair. Take Dr Manock's evidence out of the case, and what's left? A woman found in a bath. No eyewitness. No confession. No physical evidence that a court had accepted as showing force. What remains is the insurance: five policies, more than a million dollars by the prosecution's figure, and a signature that wasn't hers.

That is suspicious. It may be very suspicious. But suspicion isn't proof that a death was a murder. A jury would first have to be satisfied, beyond reasonable doubt, that Anna-Jane didn't die by accident or from a medical event. Without credible pathology evidence of force, that first step becomes very hard. A motive is evidence of why someone might kill. It is not evidence that anyone did.

The DPP concluded the State couldn't clear that bar.

[SLOW]

Here, again, precision matters. A nolle prosequi is not an acquittal. It's not a finding of innocence. It's a decision by the prosecution not to go ahead. In theory, a charge can be brought again if new evidence emerges.

And South Australia Police made their own position clear the very next day. They said they were investigating no one else in relation to Anna-Jane Cheney's death. [SRC: 7]

Read that line twice. The police were not looking for another suspect. They were not treating her death as an accident. They simply had no case they could take to court.

[NORMAL]

In July twenty eighteen, the South Australian government agreed to pay Henry Keogh two point five seven million dollars in compensation for the twenty years he had spent in prison. [SRC: 10]

"You can't put a price on twenty years," he said. [SRC: 10]

He had come out into a world that had moved on without him. Mobile phones, the internet, prices, streets, friends who had aged two decades in his absence. He said he hoped the payment would let him finally move on. [SRC: 10] Part of that new life, he has said, was a relationship that began while he was still behind bars, and that he carried with him out of prison. [SRC: 15]

But the compensation didn't settle the question either. A month later, in August twenty eighteen, South Australia's Police Commissioner, Grant Stevens, was asked whether Keogh remained a person of interest in Anna-Jane's death. He said it would be an "understatement" to say so. [SRC: 7]

The same state that had paid him millions for a wrongful imprisonment had a police commissioner saying, in effect, that his officers still regarded him as the person most likely to be responsible.

Those two positions sound contradictory, but legally they can both stand. The compensation acknowledged that Henry Keogh had spent twenty years in prison on the strength of a conviction that the courts had found to be a miscarriage of justice. It did not declare him innocent. The police view was a statement about suspicion, not proof. In the space between those two things, a man and a grieving family were left to live out the rest of their lives.

[SLOW]

In September twenty eighteen, Anna-Jane's family went public. They asked the Director of Public Prosecutions to pursue a retrial. [SRC: 7] They also planned civil action over the insurance policies taken out in Anna-Jane's name, some of them, as the court had heard, with her signature written by Henry Keogh. [SRC: 7]

For the Cheney family, nothing about the appeal ruling had answered the questions they'd had since nineteen ninety-four. The ruling said the pathology was flawed. It didn't explain the policies. It didn't explain the signatures. It didn't bring Anna-Jane back, and it took away the one certainty they had.

[NORMAL]

And Henry Keogh answered too. In November twenty eighteen, he spoke to a committee of the South Australian Parliament examining his compensation payment. He told them two point five seven million dollars was not enough. And he asked the politicians a question.

What price would they put on twenty years stolen from them? [SRC: 10]

He has always maintained that he did not kill Anna-Jane Cheney. And no court has ever found, on sound evidence, that he did.

Both of those statements are true. So is everything the Cheney family has said. That's what makes this case so difficult.

[AD BREAK OK]

## 11. AFTERMATH + MEANING (49:00–53:00)
[NORMAL]

Henry Keogh's case was not the only one that depended on Dr Manock.

Remember the number. Around four hundred criminal cases in which he gave evidence. [SRC: 4] Campaigners and some lawyers have called for every one of them to be reviewed. A local member of parliament described the situation as a scandal nobody wants to touch. [SRC: 4]

One of those cases is Derek Bromley, an Aboriginal man convicted of murder in Adelaide in the nineteen-eighties, partly on pathology evidence from Dr Manock. Experts later commissioned in his appeal agreed that Dr Manock's diagnosis of drowning in that case was wrong, and that the cause of death should have been recorded as undetermined. [SRC: 11] Bromley's appeals have failed. In twenty twenty-three, the High Court of Australia refused to hear his case by a split decision of three judges to two. Two of the judges said they believed a substantial miscarriage of justice had occurred. [SRC: 11] His conviction stands. He has since been released on parole after more than four decades in custody. [SRC: 11]

The Keogh ruling also opened the door for others to try. In twenty sixteen, the new appeal law was used to challenge the forensic evidence in a South Australian murder case dating back to nineteen seventy-one. [SRC: 14] Within a few years, other Australian states had begun introducing similar second-appeal laws of their own. [SRC: 9] A man who spent twenty years unable to get back into court had, in the process, created a way back in for everyone who came after him.

That's the legacy of one expert. Not one case. Possibly hundreds.

And it isn't only an Australian problem. In two thousand and nine, a landmark report from the United States National Academy of Sciences warned that many forensic disciplines had been used in courtrooms for decades without rigorous scientific validation, and that expert testimony was often presented with more certainty than the science could support. The Keogh case is a near-perfect illustration of that warning. The bruises were real. What was wrong was the confidence placed on top of them.

[SLOW]

But let's end where this story should end. With Anna-Jane.

She was twenty-nine. She had just had a birthday. She was a lawyer whose job was to make sure the legal profession behaved honestly. She was the daughter of a pathologist. She had a wedding five weeks away, a dress, a guest list, a future that was all arranged.

There's a quiet, painful symmetry in her story. Her father was a pathologist, a man who spent his career reading the evidence the human body leaves behind, in blood and in tissue. His daughter's death came down entirely to whether another pathologist had read that evidence correctly. A family that knew, better than most, what careful pathology looks like, had to watch the reading of their daughter's body become the most argued-over autopsy in South Australian history.

And in the thirty years since, her death has been turned into a legal landmark, a law-school case study, a symbol of forensic failure, a cause for campaigners, a compensation hearing. Very rarely has it been remembered simply as what it was. The loss of a young woman, to a family who have never accepted that she died by accident.

[NORMAL]

So, the question we started with. If the evidence that convicted Henry Keogh was wrong, what does anyone actually know about what happened in that bathroom?

Here's the most honest answer the record allows.

We know that Anna-Jane Cheney died in her bath on March eighteenth, nineteen ninety-four. We know that the forensic evidence that turned her death into a murder conviction was found by the state's highest appeal court to be flawed. We know the insurance policies existed, and that Henry Keogh signed her name, and that he has an explanation for that. We know he spent twenty years in prison. We know the State paid him for it. And we know the police still regard him as a person of interest.

What we don't know, and what no court has determined on sound evidence, is how Anna-Jane died.

And notice what that leaves for the two families at the centre of this. Henry Keogh walked free, was compensated, and has never been convicted on evidence that survived scrutiny. But he lives with a police commissioner calling him a person of interest, and with a family across town who believe he killed the woman he was about to marry. He will never get the certainty of a verdict saying he's innocent.

The Cheney family lost a daughter and a sister. They had a conviction for twenty years, then watched it dissolve. They can't get a retrial. They will never get the certainty of a verdict saying he did it.

Neither side gets an ending. That's what one flawed autopsy did. It didn't just put the wrong evidence in front of a jury. It took away the possibility of a right answer.

And that's the real cost of what went wrong in nineteen ninety-four. Not just that one man may have spent twenty years in prison for something the evidence could never properly prove. But that, because the case was built on one expert's certainty, when that certainty collapsed there was nothing left to try. The truth, whatever it is, may now be beyond reach. For the Keogh side, and for the Cheney side.

What's striking about this case is that it's not really a whodunit. It's a warning about what happens when a courtroom trusts a title more than it tests the science.
