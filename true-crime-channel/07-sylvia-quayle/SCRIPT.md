## 3. THE WORLD BEFORE (1:30–5:00)
[NORMAL]

[OBJECT ONE: THE COFFEE CUP]

Her name was Sylvia Quayle. In the summer of nineteen eighty-one, she was thirty-four years old. [SRC: 1]

She'd grown up in the area and graduated from Englewood High School, just south of Denver. [SRC: 2] And she'd built something of her own.

Sylvia was a very good cook. And she'd opened a small business called The Buttery, specialising in wedding cakes. [SRC: 1]

Think about what that means. Sylvia's job was making the centrepiece of the happiest day in other people's lives. The tiered cake that everyone gathers around. The one in all the photographs. Somewhere in the Denver area, there are wedding albums from the late nineteen-seventies and early nineteen-eighties with Sylvia's work in them, and the couples in those pictures may never have known her name.

Decades later, the chief of the Cherry Hills Village Police Department, Michelle Tovrea, would describe her this way. Sylvia was "ambitious, friendly, vibrant," and she "lit up the room when she walked in." [SRC: 1]

She was very close to her family. Her parents. Her younger sister. [SRC: 1] And she had a habit with her parents that tells you a lot about that closeness.

Every morning, Sylvia had coffee with them. [SRC: 1]

It's such an ordinary detail. Not a holiday, not a birthday. Just a daily ritual. A grown woman with her own home and her own business, who still started her day at her parents' table. You don't keep a habit like that unless you enjoy it.

[NORMAL]

Sylvia lived in Cherry Hills Village. If you don't know the Denver area, Cherry Hills Village is something like its Beverly Hills. Large homes on large lots. Horse properties. Mature trees. A golf club that has hosted major championships, including the nineteen-sixty US Open, where Arnold Palmer made one of the most famous comebacks in the history of the sport. It's a small, very wealthy, very quiet community, just south of the city.

Her house was on the thirty-eight hundred block of South Ogden Street. [SRC: 3]

It's the kind of place where people feel safe. Where the biggest police business is usually a speeding ticket or a burglar alarm going off by mistake. The Cherry Hills Village Police Department was small, as you'd expect for a town like that. [SRC: 3]

Hold that thought. In the summer of nineteen eighty-one, that small department was about to face the kind of case that would challenge a big-city homicide unit.

And think about where Sylvia was in her life that summer. Mid-thirties. Her own home. Her own business, built on her own skill. A family she was close to, a few minutes away. By any measure, she had made a good, independent life for herself. The Buttery wasn't a hobby. It was a business with customers, orders, deadlines, couples counting on her for one of the most important days of their lives.

Wedding season in Colorado runs through the summer. In early August, Sylvia would likely have been in the busiest stretch of her year.

On the evening of Monday, August third, nineteen eighty-one, Sylvia was at home on South Ogden Street.

## 5. THE DAY (5:30–13:30)
[SLOW]

Here is what investigators and prosecutors would later say happened in the night between August third and August fourth, nineteen eighty-one. And notice, as you hear it, how much of it was planned.

[OBJECT TWO: THE GARDEN HOSE]

In nineteen eighty-one, there were no mobile phones. If you were in trouble in your home at night, the telephone on the wall or by your bed was your only connection to the outside world. Your only way to call the police, a neighbour, your parents.

Someone made sure Sylvia wouldn't have that.

According to the prosecution's account, the man who came to her house that night used a garden hose to pull down the telephone lines leading to the house, and severed them. [SRC: 3]

Think about what that tells you. This wasn't a burglar who panicked. It wasn't a moment of rage. Somebody arrived at Sylvia's house with a plan. They thought, before they went in, about how to stop her calling for help. They took the time, in the dark, to find the lines, and to bring them down.

[OBJECT THREE: THE WINDOW SCREEN]

Then they needed a way in.

According to investigators, he removed a screen from a bathroom window and climbed through. [SRC: 3]

A bathroom window. Small, often left partly open on a summer night for air. Usually at the side or back of a house, out of view of the street. Again, a choice. Again, a plan.

[SLOW]

What happened inside the house, this video will state, and not describe.

Sylvia Quayle was sexually assaulted. She was stabbed, and she was shot. [SRC: 3] She did not survive.

Her killer left. Into the night, through a wealthy suburb where nobody was awake to see him. Down a road, onto a highway, and, as it would turn out, out of her family's reach for forty years.

[NORMAL]

Think about how alone that leaves a person. In nineteen eighty-one, there was no mobile phone on the bedside table. No smart doorbell. No home security app. No camera on the porch. In many homes, there wasn't even an alarm system. A house in a quiet suburb at night was a dark box, and the phone line was the only wire connecting it to help. When that line was cut, Sylvia's house became an island.

And the man who came that night seems to have understood that. He didn't just come in. He came in after making sure that, whatever happened inside, nobody outside could be called.

The next morning, at her parents' table, the third cup of coffee went cold.

Sylvia hadn't called to say she couldn't make it. She always called. Her father, William Quayle, decided to go and check on her. [SRC: 1]

He drove to South Ogden Street. And it was William Quayle who found his daughter. [SRC: 1]

That moment is the centre of this story, even though it's the part we'll spend the least time on. A father who went to check because his daughter missed coffee, and found what he found. He would live the rest of his life with it.

Forty years later, the man whose company would finally identify the killer stood at a press conference and tried to talk about that morning. Mitch Morrissey, a former district attorney for Denver, became emotional. "I can't imagine," he said, "as a father myself of a young woman about the same age, to have a morning like that." [SRC: 8]

[SLOW]

The Cherry Hills Village police arrived at a scene that told them three things very quickly.

First, this was a murder of extraordinary violence. The killer had used multiple methods. It wasn't a single act of panic.

Second, the cut phone lines and the removed window screen meant it was planned. Whoever did this had thought ahead.

And third, the killer had left something of himself behind. Biological evidence, collected from the scene and preserved. [SRC: 2] In nineteen eighty-one, the best a lab could do with that kind of evidence was establish a blood type. A clue, but not a name.

So there was a puzzle. A man who planned carefully enough to cut the phone lines, but who left his own DNA at the scene. A man who knew how to get in, but who, as far as anyone has ever established, had no known connection to Sylvia at all.

Who plans like that? And how do you find a man who has no reason to be on anyone's list?

## 6. FIRST RESPONSE (13:30–22:30)
[NORMAL]

The Cherry Hills Village Police Department in nineteen eighty-one was not built for a case like this. A small suburban force, more used to traffic and burglary alarms than to homicide. And now it had one of the most violent murders in the region on its hands, in its own quiet streets.

Detectives did what they could. They canvassed the neighbourhood. They interviewed Sylvia's family, her friends, her business contacts. They looked at everyone in her life.

And, as happens in so many cases, they looked hardest at the men she had known.

[SLOW]

Years later, at trial, the man eventually charged with Sylvia's murder would argue that there was another obvious suspect, and that police had overlooked him. His defence lawyers would point to a former boyfriend of Sylvia's. They would claim that this man had been violent toward her in the past, and that a relative of Sylvia's had told police early on that he might be responsible. [SRC: 3]

That man was never charged with any crime connected to Sylvia's death. [SRC: 3] And because he was never charged, this video won't name him. But the defence's argument is part of the record, and it matters, because it shows how this case looked in the years before DNA could speak. Police had theories. They had people they wondered about. What they didn't have was anything that could prove, one way or the other, who had been in that house.

[SLOW]

And then, in nineteen eighty-three, the case was solved. Or so it seemed.

Two years after Sylvia's murder, a drifter named Ottis Elwood Toole confessed to killing her. [SRC: 9]

If that name sounds familiar, it's because Ottis Toole was one of the most notorious figures in American crime in the nineteen-eighties. Together with his companion, Henry Lee Lucas, he had begun confessing to murders. Not one or two. Scores of them. Across state after state. [SRC: 9] Police departments from all over the country, with unsolved killings on their books, came to interview the pair, and the pair kept confessing.

And Toole's confession to Sylvia's murder came with detail. He described, for investigators from the Colorado Bureau of Investigation, the trees and the hedges around her home. [SRC: 9]

Ottis Toole was formally charged with first-degree murder in the death of Sylvia Quayle. [SRC: 9]

[NORMAL]

For Sylvia's family, that should have been the answer. A confessed killer. Charged. The kind of man who, by his own account, travelled the country killing strangers. It fitted the picture of a crime by someone with no connection to her.

Except it wasn't true.

Over the following years, the Lucas and Toole confessions began to collapse across the country. Investigators found that the two men had confessed to crimes in places they couldn't possibly have been. That they'd picked up details of crime scenes from the very officers interviewing them, from photographs, from questions that gave away the answers. That some police departments, eager to close cases, had accepted confessions that didn't stand up. Many of those so-called solved cases had to be reopened.

In Texas, where much of the confessing happened, a special task force had at one point credited Lucas with hundreds of killings. In nineteen eighty-six, the Texas Attorney General's office issued a report that picked those claims apart, comparing the confessions with records showing where Lucas actually was. Again and again, the dates didn't fit. Lucas's only death sentence was eventually commuted in nineteen ninety-eight, after doubts about whether he had committed that murder at all.

It's one of the most damaging episodes in the history of American policing. Two men, who were genuinely dangerous and genuinely guilty of some killings, became a convenient answer for dozens of departments who wanted their unsolved cases closed. And every time a case was wrongly closed with their names, the real killer was left free.

That includes Toole's description of the trees and hedges. A detail like that sounds convincing, until you remember that a man being shown around, or asked leading questions, or shown photographs, can pick up exactly that kind of detail without ever having been there.

[SLOW]

In Sylvia's case, the end came from science. In nineteen ninety-three, DNA testing on the evidence from her house proved that Ottis Toole was not the source of the DNA found at the scene. [SRC: 9] The Arapahoe County district attorney dropped the charge against him. [SRC: 9]

Ten years. For ten years, the official answer to who killed Sylvia Quayle had been a man who, the science now showed, wasn't there. Ten years in which nobody was looking for the real killer, because on paper the case was closed.

That's the hidden cost of a false confession. It doesn't just risk convicting the wrong person. It stops the search for the right one.

And in nineteen ninety-three, Sylvia's family went back to having no answer at all.

Ottis Toole died in a Florida prison in nineteen ninety-six. His story didn't end cleanly there either. In two thousand and eight, police in Florida officially named him as the killer in one of the most famous child abduction cases in American history, a case he had confessed to and then recanted. So the same man was a genuine killer in some cases, and a false confessor in others. That's what made the Lucas and Toole confessions so dangerous. You couldn't simply dismiss everything they said, and you couldn't believe it either. Every case had to be tested on its own evidence.

In Sylvia's case, the evidence had spoken. It wasn't him.

[NORMAL]

The months passed. Then the years.

A murder case without a suspect doesn't end. It just goes quiet. The file goes into a cabinet. The officers who worked the scene are promoted, retire, move away. Sylvia's parents grew old with an empty chair at their breakfast table. Her sister grew up, married, built her own life, and carried her sister's absence through every part of it.

"For the past forty-one years," Sylvia's sister, Jo Hammit, would later say, "Sylvia missed out on family celebrations and numerous social occasions." [SRC: 4] Weddings. Births. Holidays. The ordinary days that make up a family's life. A woman who made wedding cakes for a living never got to see the next generation of her own family's weddings.

[SLOW]

[OBJECT FOUR: THE SWAB]

But the evidence from the scene had been kept.

And in two thousand, almost twenty years after the murder, a lab developed a male DNA profile from it. [SRC: 2]

In nineteen ninety-three, the science had been good enough to say who the killer was not. Now, in two thousand, it could produce something more powerful: a full genetic profile that could be searched against a national database. For the first time, the man who killed Sylvia Quayle had something like a signature. A genetic barcode. Unique to him.

It was entered into CODIS, the national DNA database that holds profiles of people convicted of crimes.

No match. [SRC: 2]

That meant one of two things. Either the killer had never been convicted of a crime serious enough to get him swabbed. Or he had, but not in a way that put his DNA in that database.

Either way, the profile sat there. Year after year. Checked, and checked again. Nothing.

[NORMAL]

Through the two-thousands and into the twenty-tens, the case stayed open, but quiet. Every so often, a detective would pull out the file, re-read the reports, check whether the DNA profile had picked up a hit. Every anniversary, the family remembered. Every year that passed, the people who might have remembered something from August nineteen eighty-one got older, moved away, or died.

It's hard to describe the particular frustration of that kind of cold case. It's not that you have no evidence. You have the best evidence there is: the killer's DNA. You just can't read the name on it. It's like holding a letter written in a language nobody speaks.

Metro Denver Crime Stoppers announced a partnership with a Denver-based company called United Data Connect. [SRC: 8] And one of the company's co-founders was a name everyone in Colorado law enforcement knew.

## 7. THE MIDPOINT TURN (22:30–26:00)
[SLOW]

[OBJECT FIVE: THE FAMILY TREE]

Mitch Morrissey had spent years as the district attorney for Denver. And he had built a national reputation around one idea: that DNA could reopen cases everyone else had given up on.

In two thousand and three, he helped start Denver's Cold Case Project, working with the city's police and crime lab. Over the years, it reviewed more than four thousand two hundred unsolved sexual assaults and murders, using DNA to look at them again. [SRC: 10]

And in two thousand and eight, Morrissey did something no district attorney in the United States had done before. He used what's called a familial DNA search. [SRC: 10] Instead of looking for an exact match in a criminal database, investigators looked for a near-match: a close relative of the person they were looking for. The case was a car break-in. Blood left at the scene matched no one in the database, but it was close to a convicted criminal. That led investigators to his brother. [SRC: 10] It was believed to be the first conviction in the country obtained that way.

A car break-in. A tiny crime. But the principle behind it was enormous: if you can't find the person, find their family.

After leaving office, Morrissey co-founded United Data Connect, a company built around that principle, applying family-based DNA searching and genetic genealogy to cold cases. [SRC: 8]

The Cherry Hills Village police teamed up with Morrissey's company. [SRC: 2] The DNA from the scene was sequenced, turned into the kind of detailed genetic profile that genealogy websites use. Then it was uploaded to two public databases: FamilyTreeDNA, and GEDmatch. [SRC: 2]

These are sites where ordinary people upload their own DNA to find relatives. Adopted children looking for birth parents. Retirees tracing their roots. And, crucially, both sites allow, under their rules, profiles to be compared for law enforcement purposes.

That wasn't always the case, and the rules are still evolving. After the Golden State Killer was identified through GEDmatch in twenty eighteen, there was a public debate about whether people who uploaded their DNA to find cousins had agreed to help police search for criminals. In twenty nineteen, GEDmatch changed its policy so that users had to opt in before their profiles could be used in law-enforcement searches. FamilyTreeDNA, around the same time, publicly acknowledged working with the FBI and gave customers ways to opt out.

So the people whose DNA helped find Sylvia's killer had, in one way or another, agreed to let their data be used this way. None of them knew Sylvia. Most of them, almost certainly, had never heard of David Anderson. They were simply curious about where their families came from.

The killer wasn't in those databases. But some of his relatives were.

[NORMAL]

From there, the genealogists did what genealogists do. They took the distant cousins who matched, and built their family trees. Back through parents, grandparents, great-grandparents. Then forward again, through every branch, looking for the one person who fit. A man. The right age to have been an adult in nineteen eighty-one. Someone whose life could have put him near Denver.

It's painstaking work. Each match on a genealogy site comes with a number: how many centimorgans, a unit of shared DNA, two people have in common. A parent and child share about half their DNA. A first cousin, around an eighth. A third cousin might share less than one percent. Every number is consistent with several possible relationships, and a genealogist has to test each possibility against birth records, marriage certificates, obituaries, census pages and old newspaper clippings until only one family tree fits all the evidence.

On January twenty-ninth, twenty twenty-one, the result that mattered would come in. [SRC: 2] But first, the tree gave them a name.

[PAUSE]

David Dwayne Anderson. [SRC: 2]

In nineteen eighty-one, he would have been about twenty-two years old.

In twenty twenty-one, he was sixty-two, and living in an apartment in a small town in Nebraska called Cozad. [SRC: 3]

[SLOW]

Look at a map, and you'll see something about Cozad. It sits right beside Interstate Eighty, the great east–west highway across the plains. Follow that interstate west and it connects with the highway that runs straight down to Denver. [SRC: 3] A few hours' drive.

A man who, forty years later, lived on the road that leads to Cherry Hills Village.

But a name from a family tree isn't proof. Genealogy points. It doesn't convict. The detectives now needed his DNA, a direct sample, to compare against the evidence from nineteen eighty-one.

And they needed to get it without David Anderson knowing they were looking at him.

## 8. COMPLICATIONS (26:00–37:00)
[NORMAL]

By January twenty twenty-one, Cherry Hills Village had a name, and a town in Nebraska. What they didn't have was proof that the man in that town had ever been inside Sylvia Quayle's house.

[HALFWAY RESET ENDS]

So the question that drives the rest of this story is: how do you take a DNA sample from someone who doesn't know they're a suspect, legally, quietly, and in a way that will stand up in court?

The answer, in case after case across America, has been the same. You wait for them to throw something away.

[SLOW]

[OBJECT SIX: THE VANILLA COKE CAN]

In January twenty twenty-one, an investigator travelled to Cozad. [SRC: 7]

The investigator watched the apartment complex where David Anderson lived. Watched who came and went. Watched for the moment the trash went out.

On January eighteenth, twenty twenty-one, the investigator saw Anderson put trash into the complex's dumpster. Two bags. [SRC: 7]

When Anderson had gone, the investigator took the bags. [SRC: 7]

Picture it. Mid-winter on the Nebraska plains. An investigator going through someone's household rubbish, piece by piece, looking for anything a person might have put to their mouth. A fork. A cigarette. A tissue. A cup.

Around fifteen items from that trash were sent for testing. [SRC: 11]

And among them, there was a can. A Vanilla Coke. [SRC: 2]

[NORMAL]

It's worth explaining why investigators can do this. In nineteen eighty-eight, in a case called California versus Greenwood, the US Supreme Court held that when you put your trash out for collection, you give up your reasonable expectation of privacy in it. Anyone can go through it: scavengers, animals, neighbours, and police. In the decades since, courts have applied that rule to trash bags at the curb and in shared dumpsters. Police don't need a warrant to take it. It's how countless investigations, from drug cases to cold-case murders, have obtained evidence.

Some people find that unsettling, and it's a fair thing to debate. You don't really think of your recycling as public property. But the law, for decades, has treated it that way. And in this case, it meant that a soda can, drunk and thrown away without a second thought, became the most important piece of evidence in a forty-year-old murder.

[SLOW]

The can went to the lab.

On January twenty-ninth, twenty twenty-one, eleven days after the trash pull, the result came back. [SRC: 7]

The DNA on the Vanilla Coke can matched the DNA from the scene of Sylvia Quayle's murder. [SRC: 2]

Thirty-nine and a half years after Sylvia missed her morning coffee, the man who had been in her house had a name, a face, and an address.

[NORMAL]

In February twenty twenty-one, David Dwayne Anderson was arrested in Nebraska and charged with Sylvia's murder. [SRC: 2]

Cozad is a small farming town on the Platte River, a few thousand people, grain elevators, a main street, and the interstate running past. The kind of place where people know their neighbours. Whatever his neighbours knew about David Anderson, it's very unlikely any of them knew this. A man in his sixties, living in an apartment complex, putting his trash out like anyone else, with a forty-year-old secret that ended up in that trash.

He was brought back to Colorado to face the charges, in the county where Sylvia had died.

At the press conference announcing the arrest, Sylvia's younger sister and her husband stood alongside Chief Tovrea and the investigators. [SRC: 2] They were still living in Cherry Hills Village. [SRC: 1] Forty years on, still in the same community where it happened.

And the police made a public appeal. They asked anyone who remembered seeing David Anderson in the Denver area in the early nineteen-eighties to come forward. [SRC: 8]

That appeal tells you something important. Even after the match, investigators didn't have a full picture of who Anderson was in nineteen eighty-one, or how he came to be at Sylvia's house. There was, as far as the public record shows, no known connection between them. No business dealing. No friendship. No reason for their lives to cross.

Which brings back the question from the night itself. The planning. The phone lines. The window. If he didn't know her, how did he choose her house? And how much did he know about it before he arrived?

Stranger killings are the hardest murders for police to solve. When someone is killed by a partner, a relative, a business rival, or a friend, investigators can work outward from the victim's life, and usually the answer is somewhere in it. When the killer has no connection to the victim at all, there's no thread to pull. No motive to trace. The killer's life and the victim's life touch only once, for a few hours, and then separate forever.

That's exactly the kind of case DNA genealogy was made for. It doesn't need a motive or a connection. It doesn't care who the victim knew. It only needs the killer to have left a trace of himself, and to have relatives somewhere who were curious about their family history.

Those questions would have to be answered, if they could be answered at all, in a courtroom.

## 9. THE BREAK (37:00–43:00)
[NORMAL]

You might expect, with a DNA match like that, that the trial would be a formality.

It wasn't.

In March twenty twenty-two, David Anderson went on trial in Arapahoe County District Court, before Judge Darren Vahle. [SRC: 6]

The prosecution's case was built on the science. Sylvia's killer had left DNA at the scene. The DNA matched David Anderson. And they laid out the night: the hose, the phone lines, the bathroom window, the attack.

[SLOW]

The defence had a hard task. They couldn't easily attack the DNA match itself. So they did something else. They told the jury the story of the man the police had overlooked.

Anderson's lawyers argued that, from the very beginning, investigators had ignored the most obvious suspect: a former boyfriend of Sylvia's who, they said, had been violent toward her before. They pointed out that a relative had told police they thought this man might be responsible, and argued that police had never seriously treated him as a suspect. [SRC: 3]

It's worth being careful here. That was the defence's argument, made in court on behalf of a client facing a murder charge. The man they pointed to was never charged with anything to do with Sylvia's death. And the DNA left at the scene didn't belong to him. It belonged to David Anderson.

But the jury still had to weigh it. A defence doesn't have to prove someone else did it. It only has to make the jury unsure.

[NORMAL]

The jury went out to deliberate.

One day. Two. Three. Four. Five.

After five days, the jury told the judge they couldn't reach a verdict. [SRC: 6]

Judge Vahle declared a mistrial. [SRC: 6]

[SLOW]

Think about what that meant for Sylvia's family. Forty-one years of waiting. An arrest. A DNA match. A trial. And then, at the very end, a jury that couldn't agree. No verdict. No conviction. No acquittal either. Nothing.

We don't know exactly what divided that jury. Jury deliberations are secret. It may have been the defence's alternative-suspect argument. It may have been doubts about evidence handled and stored for forty years. It may have been the shadow of a case that had already once been "solved" with the wrong man's name, back in nineteen eighty-three. Any of those could give a careful juror pause.

It's one of the cruellest outcomes in the justice system, and one of the most important ones. It means that twelve ordinary people took their job seriously enough to hold out, rather than give in to the pressure of a cold case and a sympathetic family. The system is designed to make convicting someone hard. Sometimes that design hurts the people who have waited longest.

The prosecutors decided to try him again.

## 10. RESOLUTION (43:00–49:00)
[NORMAL]

So: who cut the phone lines to Sylvia Quayle's house, and could a jury be sure enough to say so?

The second trial began in late June twenty twenty-two. Same courthouse. Same evidence. A new jury. Deputy District Attorney Grant Grosgebauer was among the prosecutors who took the case back into court. [SRC: 11]

This time, the trial moved quickly. The evidence was presented over three days, from June twenty-seventh to June twenty-ninth. [SRC: 11]

On June thirtieth, twenty twenty-two, that jury returned its verdicts. [SRC: 6]

Guilty of first-degree murder after deliberation. And guilty of felony murder. [SRC: 7]

David Dwayne Anderson, the man from the family tree, the man whose Vanilla Coke can had been pulled from a Nebraska dumpster, was convicted of murdering Sylvia Quayle.

The two verdicts mean slightly different things. First-degree murder after deliberation means the jury was satisfied he decided to kill, and then did it. Felony murder means a killing that happened during another serious crime, in this case the sexual assault and the break-in. Either one, on its own, was enough to make him guilty of murder. The jury found both.

Think about that word, deliberation, in light of the hose and the window screen. The prosecution's story had always been one of planning. The jury agreed.

[SLOW]

[OBJECT SEVEN: THE CALENDAR]

Then came the sentencing. And here, the calendar did something that no screenwriter would dare to invent.

The sentencing hearing was held on Thursday, August fourth, twenty twenty-two. [SRC: 5]

August fourth. The same date Sylvia's father found her in nineteen eighty-one.

Forty-one years to the day. [SRC: 5]

Sylvia's family spoke. Her sister, Jo Hammit, described what the murder had done. It had "turned my family's world upside down," she said. [SRC: 4] And she talked about the years Sylvia never had. "For the past forty-one years, Sylvia missed out on family celebrations and numerous social occasions." [SRC: 4]

Think about what it means to hear those words in a courtroom, with the man responsible sitting a few feet away. Forty-one years of birthdays, holidays and weddings, condensed into a statement read aloud. It's the closest a family ever gets to making the court feel what the crime actually took from them.

Then Judge Vahle passed sentence.

Here, there was one more twist, and it's one many people find hard to accept.

Because the murder happened in nineteen eighty-one, the judge had to sentence Anderson under the laws that were in force in nineteen eighty-one. Not the laws of twenty twenty-two. And under Colorado's sentencing law at that time, the maximum sentence available for this crime was life in prison, with the possibility of parole after twenty years. [SRC: 6]

Judge Vahle imposed the maximum. [SRC: 6]

Life, with eligibility for parole after twenty years.

[SLOW]

David Anderson was in his early sixties at sentencing. Twenty years would put him in his eighties before he could even be considered for release. Parole eligibility isn't parole. A parole board may never let him go. But it's a very different sentence from what a man convicted of a murder like this would receive today.

The principle behind it is an old and important one. You can't be punished more harshly than the law allowed at the time of your crime. It protects everyone from governments changing the rules after the fact. But in cold cases, it can produce sentences that feel, to families, like one last injustice.

Sylvia's family had their verdict. They had the date. And they had a sentence shaped by the year their daughter died.

## 11. AFTERMATH + MEANING (49:00–53:00)
[SLOW]

On Sylvia Quayle's grave marker, her family had words engraved.

"Beauty seen is never lost." [SRC: 1]

Think back to the beginning of this story. A kitchen table. Three cups of coffee. A father getting into his car because his daughter hadn't called.

William Quayle found his daughter that morning. And for the decades that followed, his family lived with a case that went from unsolved, to wrongly solved, to unsolved again, and then, finally, forty years on, to an answer. A drifter's false confession. A swab. A family tree. A can. Every turn in this case was a turn in that family's life.

Sylvia's younger sister stood beside the police at the press conference when the arrest was announced. [SRC: 2] She had carried the case for most of her life. She had watched her sister miss every celebration that came after nineteen eighty-one. And at the sentencing, her words were part of the record, the last thing the court heard about who Sylvia had been before the judge decided what her killer deserved.

It's a line that fits a woman who made wedding cakes. Things that are beautiful for a day, then gone, but remembered in photographs and in people's memories for the rest of their lives. And it fits a family that spent forty-one years refusing to let her be lost.

[NORMAL]

This case also says something about how cold cases get solved now.

Cherry Hills Village is a small department. In nineteen eighty-one, it had no realistic way to solve a case like this. In twenty twenty-one, it solved it with the help of a private company founded by a former prosecutor, using public genealogy websites built for hobbyists, and an investigator willing to stand beside a dumpster in Nebraska in January.

That combination, small police forces partnering with genealogy firms, has solved hundreds of cold cases across the United States in the last few years. It's also raised real questions. About privacy, when ordinary people's DNA uploads are used to find their relatives. About cost, when private companies decide which cases are worth their time. About fairness, when the families of victims in wealthy suburbs may be more likely to get these resources than families elsewhere.

And one of those questions came back to this very case.

Anderson appealed his conviction. One of his arguments went to the heart of how he was caught: that taking his trash and analysing the DNA on that can, without a warrant, violated his constitutional rights.

In August twenty twenty-six, the Colorado Court of Appeals ruled. [SRC: 12] By two judges to one, it rejected his argument. Neither the US Constitution nor Colorado's, the majority found, required police to get a warrant before taking the can from his trash and testing it to identify him. [SRC: 12] Courts had long allowed police to search discarded trash, and the fact that the evidence was DNA didn't, on its own, change that.

But even the judges who upheld the conviction sounded a warning. The majority opinion noted that DNA is different. "We constantly and involuntarily shed DNA everywhere we go," it said, "and its analysis may reveal a treasure trove of personal information about an individual beyond the person's identity." And, it added, "the amount of information available from DNA is only increasing with technological advances." [SRC: 12]

One of the judges in the majority wrote separately, saying he was concerned about the government collecting DNA for reasons other than identifying a suspect, and that the unfettered use, retention and cataloguing of DNA from discarded objects would be a problem. [SRC: 12] The third judge dissented. [SRC: 12]

So the Vanilla Coke can didn't just solve Sylvia's murder. It became a legal precedent, and a warning. Every one of us leaves our DNA on every cup, can and napkin we throw away. The question of what police may do with it is now being argued in courtrooms because of this case.

All of those are fair questions. None of them changes what happened in this case. Sylvia's killer was found because the evidence was kept, the science caught up, and people refused to give up.

[SLOW]

So, the question we started with. Who cut the phone lines in Denver's richest suburb, and how do you find a man who left almost nothing behind?

A jury has answered the first half. David Dwayne Anderson was convicted of her murder.

The second half has a more surprising answer. You find him through his relatives, people who never knew him and never knew Sylvia, who uploaded their DNA to learn about their own family history. And then you wait for him to throw away a can of soda.

And there's one more thing worth noticing. Twice, this case was "solved" by a confession or a theory: Ottis Toole in nineteen eighty-three, and, in the defence's telling, a former boyfriend who should have been the suspect. Both times, the answer that people found convincing was wrong, or at least unproven. The answer that held up in court came from the one witness that doesn't get tired, doesn't want attention, and can't be led: the DNA.

What's striking about this case is how ordinary the final piece was. Forty years of investigation came down to the most forgettable thing a person can do: finish a drink and toss the can.
