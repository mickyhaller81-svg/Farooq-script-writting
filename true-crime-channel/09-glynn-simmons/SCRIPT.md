## 6. FIRST RESPONSE (13:30–22:30)
[NORMAL]

[EDMOND]

The murder of a store clerk during a holiday-week robbery shook Edmond. Police needed to find the men responsible, and they had very little to go on. No obvious physical evidence pointing at a suspect. No security camera footage; this was nineteen seventy-four. What they had was the memory of the survivors.

And, above all, they had Belinda Brown. She had been face to face with the robbers. She had been shot by one of them. She had survived.

Investigators began organising lineups. [SRC: 1]

It's worth knowing how differently lineups are run today, because almost every modern safeguard was missing in nineteen seventy-five. Today, best practice, recommended by researchers and adopted by many police departments, says the officer running a lineup should not know who the suspect is, so they can't give unconscious cues. The witness should be told that the culprit may not be in the lineup at all. The witness's confidence should be recorded immediately, in their own words, before they hear anything else. And the same witness shouldn't be shown the same suspect again and again, because repeated exposure makes a face feel familiar, whether or not it was ever at the crime scene.

None of those protections were standard practice in Edmond in nineteen seventy-five.

[SLOW]

A live lineup, in the nineteen-seventies, worked like this. A group of people, one of whom police suspected, stood in a row behind glass or under lights. The witness looked at them and said whether they recognised anyone.

In this case, according to later accounts, police brought in people connected to a party, everyone they could find who had been there, and put them into lineups. [SRC: 1] Among those people was Glynn Simmons, who had returned to Oklahoma by then.

Think about what that approach means. Police didn't start with a suspect and test whether the witness recognised him. They gathered a group of young men connected by a social event, and showed them to the witness, lineup after lineup, until she picked someone out. The more lineups a witness views, and the more faces she sees, the greater the chance that she'll pick someone who simply looks familiar, or who resembles the person she remembers.

On February seventh and eighth, nineteen seventy-five, Belinda Brown viewed lineups. [SRC: 2]

And she identified two men. Glynn Simmons. And a twenty-one-year-old named Don Roberts. [SRC: 1]

On February eighth, nineteen seventy-five, both were arrested and charged with capital murder. [SRC: 1]

[NORMAL]

Remember what this case had. No physical evidence tying Simmons to the store. No confession. Seven witnesses placing him in another state. And one eyewitness: a young woman who had been shot in the head during the crime, and who, weeks later, had pointed at him in a lineup.

The trial came in the spring of nineteen seventy-five.

[SLOW]

The prosecution's case rested on Belinda Brown. She testified that she had identified Simmons and Roberts. And when she was asked about the lineups, she testified that she hadn't identified anyone else. [SRC: 2]

That's a crucial point. It means that, as far as the jury knew, her identification had been clean. She had looked at the lineups, seen two men, and recognised exactly those two. No hesitation. No other names.

The defence called its alibi witnesses. Seven of them. [SRC: 7] People from Harvey who said Glynn Simmons had been there, at home, on the night of the murder. Simmons himself testified that he'd been in Louisiana. [SRC: 7]

So the jury had a choice. Seven people from Louisiana, many of them friends or acquaintances of the defendant, saying one thing. And one young woman, a victim of the crime, who had nothing to gain, saying another.

It's well documented that juries tend to discount alibi witnesses who know the defendant. The assumption is understandable: friends and family might lie to protect someone they care about. But it creates a painful trap for innocent people. If you were somewhere ordinary on an ordinary night, the people most likely to remember you are exactly the people a jury is least likely to believe. Strangers don't notice you. Friends do, and friends are suspected of bias.

[NORMAL]

Don Roberts, the second man Belinda Brown had picked out, was tried alongside Simmons. Twenty-one years old. Like Simmons, he would spend decades insisting he had nothing to do with it. The two men's fates were bound together by the same identification, in the same lineups, recorded in the same police paperwork.

On June fifth, nineteen seventy-five, the jury convicted both men of capital murder. [SRC: 1]

In July nineteen seventy-five, Glynn Simmons and Don Roberts were sentenced to death. [SRC: 1]

Glynn Simmons was twenty-two years old.

What the jury didn't know, what the defence didn't know, what Glynn Simmons wouldn't know for a very long time, was that there was a document. A police report about those lineups.

## 7. THE MIDPOINT TURN (22:30–26:00)
[SLOW]

The report described the lineups Belinda Brown had viewed. [SRC: 2]

It recorded that, over the course of the process, she had viewed eight separate lineups. [SRC: 1] And it recorded that she had identified other people. [SRC: 2]

According to the report, she identified a man named Delbert Patterson, and another person listed as "subject number," with the number left blank. [SRC: 2] And it noted that, along the way, through the eight lineup procedures, she had identified four other individuals. [SRC: 2]

[PAUSE]

Now go back to the trial.

On the stand, Belinda Brown testified that she had not identified anyone other than Simmons and Roberts. [SRC: 2]

The police report said she had.

[NORMAL]

That report was never turned over to Glynn Simmons's defence for his trial. [SRC: 2]

Think about what that means. A jury was asked to choose between one eyewitness and seven alibi witnesses. The eyewitness's reliability was everything. And the single most important fact for judging that reliability, that she had picked out other people too, was in a police report that the defence never saw.

A good cross-examination doesn't need to call a witness a liar. It only needs to show the jury that her certainty has limits. Did you view other lineups? How many? Did you pick anyone else? Were you sure then? What made you sure about these two and not the others? With the report in hand, every one of those questions had a documented answer.

With that report, a defence lawyer could have stood up in front of the jury and asked: you've told us you picked only these two men. But this police report says you identified at least one other named man, and others. Which is it? How confident can you really be?

The jury never heard that question.

[SLOW]

There's a name for what went wrong here. In nineteen sixty-three, in a case called Brady versus Maryland, the US Supreme Court ruled that prosecutors have a constitutional duty to give the defence any evidence that's favourable to the accused and material to the case. Evidence that might help prove innocence, or that might undermine a key prosecution witness. Hiding it, whether deliberately or by mistake, violates the defendant's right to a fair trial.

These are called Brady violations. And a report showing that the only eyewitness had picked out other people in lineups is a textbook example of Brady material.

This one sat undisclosed while Glynn Simmons was tried, convicted and sentenced to death. And according to later accounts, it wasn't discovered for about twenty years. [SRC: 2]

In June nineteen ninety-six, a private investigator working to help Simmons obtained the case file. [SRC: 12] Inside it was the lineup report. [SRC: 12] Simmons's later lawyers would put its significance in the strongest possible terms: in their reading, the report showed that the eyewitness had identified other people in the lineups, not the two men she named in court. [SRC: 12] That's their characterisation. What isn't in dispute is the core fact a court would eventually accept: the report existed, it bore directly on the reliability of the only eyewitness, and the defence never had it at trial.

Twenty years. By the time anyone outside the police file saw that page, Glynn Simmons was in his forties.

Think about what nineteen ninety-six was like. The internet was just arriving in ordinary homes. A new generation had been born and grown into adulthood since his arrest. And a man in an Oklahoma prison was finally holding a piece of paper that, he believed, proved what he'd been saying since nineteen seventy-five.

## 8. COMPLICATIONS (26:00–37:00)
[NORMAL]

By nineteen seventy-five, Glynn Simmons was on death row for a murder in a state he says he wasn't in that night, convicted largely on an identification that a hidden report undermined. What he didn't have was any way to know that report existed.

[HALFWAY RESET ENDS]

So the question for the second half isn't just whether he was innocent. It's this: once the truth was on paper, why did it take nearly thirty more years to set him free?

[NORMAL]

In the mid-nineteen-seventies, the US Supreme Court was reshaping the death penalty. In nineteen seventy-two, in Furman versus Georgia, it had found that the death penalty, as it was then being applied, was arbitrary enough to be unconstitutional. States rushed to rewrite their laws. Some made death mandatory for certain crimes. But in nineteen seventy-six, the Court struck down mandatory death sentences too, ruling that juries had to be able to consider the individual circumstances of each defendant. In the aftermath, in nineteen seventy-seven, the death sentences of Glynn Simmons and Don Roberts were changed to life in prison. [SRC: 1]

He would not be executed. But he would not be going home either.

It's worth remembering, though, that for about two years, Glynn Simmons was a man under sentence of death, for a crime a court would later say he didn't commit. He is counted among the people in the United States who were sentenced to death and later exonerated. Each one of those cases raises the same question: what if the sentence had been carried out before the truth came out?

[SLOW]

Think about what life in prison meant for Glynn Simmons, starting at twenty-three.

Oklahoma would go on to have one of the highest incarceration rates in the United States, and at times in the world. Its prisons grew more crowded through the nineteen-eighties and nineties. A man serving life for murder was near the bottom of every list for transfers, programmes, and consideration. The system wasn't built to listen to someone who said he didn't belong there. It was built to hold him.

The nineteen-seventies became the nineteen-eighties. Then the nineties. His friends from Harvey grew up, married, had children, built careers. His family aged. Every year, the gap between the life he'd been living and the one inside the walls grew wider.

And every year, he maintained the same thing. That he had been in Louisiana that night. That he didn't kill Carolyn Sue Rogers.

When the lineup report finally came to light, around twenty years after the trial, you might think that would be the end of it. A key witness's testimony contradicted by the police's own paperwork. A clear Brady problem. But it's precisely here that the American appeals system is at its most unforgiving.

[NORMAL]

Appeals courts don't simply re-try cases. They look for legal errors, and they have strict rules about when new arguments can be raised. Deadlines. Procedural bars. Requirements that a claim be raised at the right moment, in the right form, in the right court. A prisoner without a lawyer, or with an overworked one, can lose a strong claim simply because it was filed too late or in the wrong way.

And even when courts consider new evidence, they often ask a harsh question: would it definitely have changed the verdict? Not "might it have," but "would it." That's a very high bar to clear decades after the fact.

And time was working against him in another way. Every year that passed, the alibi witnesses from Harvey got older. Some moved away. Some died. If a court ever did order a new trial, the evidence that had supported him in nineteen seventy-five would be thinner each time. Delay doesn't just keep an innocent person in prison. It erodes their ability to prove their innocence.

For years, Glynn Simmons's efforts to overturn his conviction failed. Even after the report surfaced in nineteen ninety-six, the courts did not set him free. The paper that should have changed everything became one more exhibit in one more filing, in a system that had already decided.

[SLOW]

There was another route: parole. Even a guilty person serving life can, after enough years, be released if a parole board decides they're no longer a danger.

But here's the trap that innocent prisoners face. Parole boards often want to see remorse. They want to hear the prisoner take responsibility for the crime. And a man who insists he didn't do it can look, to a parole board, like someone who hasn't accepted his guilt.

Glynn Simmons's parole was repeatedly denied, even at times when the Oklahoma Pardon and Parole Board supported releasing him. [SRC: 2] He stayed in prison through his forties, fifties, sixties.

How do you survive that? For Glynn Simmons, part of the answer was other people. Inside, he mentored younger inmates. [SRC: 13] Men who'd come into the system the way he had, young, scared, with decades ahead of them. He gave them advice, encouragement, an example. It was a way of keeping his mind engaged, and of finding a purpose in a place designed to strip purpose away. [SRC: 13]

It's one of the quiet patterns among long-term exonerees. Many of them say they survived by refusing to let the prison define them. By reading. By working. By helping others. By holding on to the one thing nobody inside could take: their own knowledge of what was true.

[NORMAL]

Meanwhile, his co-defendant's path went differently.

Don Roberts was paroled in two thousand and eight. [SRC: 1] He walked out of prison after more than three decades. But leaving on parole is not the same as being cleared. Roberts's conviction stood. It still does. [SRC: 11] He has maintained that he too is innocent, and his lawyers have sought to overturn his conviction. In twenty twenty-four, prosecutors declined to do so. [SRC: 11] That part of the story is still unresolved, and this video won't pretend to know how it ends.

[SLOW]

It's worth stepping back to look at what was really holding Glynn Simmons in prison all those years. Not new evidence of guilt. There wasn't any. What held him was the weight of a verdict. Once a jury has said "guilty," the whole system leans toward keeping it that way. Finality. Deference. Procedure. The assumption that the trial got it right.

It's also worth understanding why this case never had the kind of breakthrough that freed so many other wrongly convicted people in the nineteen-nineties and two-thousands. That era of exonerations was driven largely by DNA. Old rape kits, blood, hair, retested with new technology, proving that someone else had committed the crime. But Carolyn Sue Rogers was killed in a robbery with a gun. There was no biological evidence from the killers to test. No DNA that could point to the real culprits, or clear Glynn Simmons. His case had to be won the hard way: on paper, on testimony, on the argument that the trial itself had been unfair.

And underneath it all, an eyewitness identification. The single most persuasive kind of evidence a jury can hear, and, research now shows, one of the least reliable.

[NORMAL]

Over the last few decades, psychologists have studied eyewitness memory intensely. What they've found is unsettling. Memory isn't a recording. It's reconstructed every time we recall something, and it can be changed by what happens afterward: the way a lineup is run, the way a question is asked, what an officer says or doesn't say, whether a witness is told they picked "the right one."

Stress and violence make it worse. A witness who is terrified, or injured, or focused on a gun, often doesn't take in faces the way they believe they did. And confidence can grow over time. A witness who was unsure in a lineup can become certain by the time they reach a courtroom, especially after months of believing they've identified the right person.

According to the Innocence Project, mistaken eyewitness identification played a part in roughly seven in ten of the convictions in the United States later overturned through DNA evidence. That doesn't make witnesses liars. It makes them human.

Belinda Brown survived being shot in the head. She went into those lineups as a victim of the crime. Whatever happened in those eight lineups, she was not the one responsible for a report going undisclosed. That responsibility belonged to the system.

## 9. THE BREAK (37:00–43:00)
[NORMAL]

For decades, Glynn Simmons's case seemed stuck. In two thousand and nineteen, he hired new lawyers, Joe Norwood and John Coyle, private attorneys in Oklahoma who took on the case when his chances looked dim. [SRC: 12] They went back through everything. The trial transcripts. The lineup report. The alibi testimony. The decades of filings.

It's worth noting how often that happens in wrongful conviction cases. The breakthrough comes not from a new fact, but from a new person looking at old facts. A fresh lawyer. A new prosecutor. A journalist. Someone who doesn't carry the assumptions of the people who built the case in the first place.

Then, in Oklahoma County, something changed at the top.

Vicki Behenna became the Oklahoma County District Attorney. And her office looked at the Simmons case again.

Around the country, a growing number of prosecutors' offices have set up what are called conviction integrity units: teams inside the prosecutor's office whose job is to re-examine old convictions where there's credible evidence of a mistake. There are now dozens of them in the United States. It's a striking idea when you think about it. The same office that won the conviction takes responsibility for checking whether it should have.

[SLOW]

In April twenty twenty-three, Behenna did something prosecutors almost never do in a case like this. She filed a motion asking the court to vacate Glynn Simmons's conviction. [SRC: 2]

Think about how unusual that is. For forty-eight years, the State of Oklahoma had defended this conviction. Now the State's own chief prosecutor in that county was telling a judge that the trial had been unfair.

The reason, at the heart of it, was that police report. The lineup report that should have been given to the defence in nineteen seventy-five, and wasn't. [SRC: 2]

[NORMAL]

The case went to an evidentiary hearing before Oklahoma County District Judge Amy Palumbo. [SRC: 1]

Picture the courtroom. On one side, the defence: the man who had maintained his innocence for forty-eight years, now seventy, and his lawyers. On the other side, the State of Oklahoma, represented by a prosecutor's office that, for the first time, wasn't fighting him. Both sides were telling the judge the same thing: the trial in nineteen seventy-five was not fair.

In July twenty twenty-three, Judge Palumbo vacated Glynn Simmons's conviction and ordered a new trial. [SRC: 1] He was released on bond. [SRC: 2]

[SLOW]

After forty-eight years, one month and eighteen days, Glynn Simmons walked out of custody.

He was seventy years old. [SRC: 1]

In his first interviews after walking out, he talked about the simple strangeness of being outside, of making his own choices again after almost half a century of having every choice made for him. [SRC: 5]

He went in at twenty-two, a few months after Richard Nixon resigned the presidency. He came out in an era of smartphones, social media and electric cars. Most of the people who had testified for him in nineteen seventy-five were gone or old. His whole adult life had been spent inside.

[NORMAL]

But it wasn't over yet. "Vacated" means the conviction was erased and a new trial ordered. In theory, the State could still have tried him again.

On September eleventh, twenty twenty-three, District Attorney Behenna announced that her office would not retry him. [SRC: 1] On September nineteenth, Judge Palumbo dismissed the charges. [SRC: 2]

He was no longer convicted. He was no longer charged.

But he still wasn't officially innocent. And for Glynn Simmons, that distinction mattered.

## 10. RESOLUTION (43:00–49:00)
[NORMAL]

So, how was a man who says he was in Louisiana convicted of a murder in Oklahoma? And how did one report stay hidden for so long?

There was one more step left for Glynn Simmons. Under Oklahoma law, a person wrongly convicted can ask a court to declare them "actually innocent." Not just "not guilty," not just "the trial was unfair," but affirmatively innocent. It's a much higher bar. And it matters, legally and personally. It's what allows a person to seek compensation from the state. And it's what lets them say, with a court's backing, "I didn't do it."

[SLOW]

On December nineteenth, twenty twenty-three, Judge Amy Palumbo ruled. [SRC: 1]

She found there was "clear and convincing evidence" that Glynn Simmons did not commit the crime. [SRC: 1]

That phrase has a specific legal meaning. It's a higher standard than the one used in most civil cases, which only asks what's more likely than not. It's not quite as high as "beyond reasonable doubt," the standard for a criminal conviction. But it means the judge was firmly convinced. Not that the evidence against him was weak. That the evidence showed he didn't do it.

Glynn Simmons was declared actually innocent of the murder of Carolyn Sue Rogers. [SRC: 1]

He was seventy-one years old. He had served forty-eight years, one month and eighteen days for a crime he did not commit. According to the National Registry of Exonerations, it was the longest known wrongful imprisonment in the history of the United States. [SRC: 1]

[NORMAL]

Outside the courthouse, he spoke to reporters. And what he said wasn't anger. It was this:

"It's a lesson in resilience and tenacity. Don't let nobody tell you that it can't happen, because it really can." [SRC: 10]

And he said something else that went to the heart of what the ruling meant to him. "Validation and vindication," he said, "is finally happening." [SRC: 6]

He talked, too, about what he wanted to do with whatever time he had. Not revenge. Reform. "I got a passion for criminal justice," he said, "justice reform, and rethinking how we do criminal justice." [SRC: 6]

His story travelled around the world. News outlets on every continent reported it: an American man freed after nearly half a century for a crime he didn't commit. For many people, it was the first time they'd heard the phrase "longest wrongful imprisonment." For Glynn Simmons, the attention was a chance to talk about the people still inside.

Within days of his release, he had walked into a diversion and re-entry centre in Oklahoma County, where staff helped him start building a life from nothing: documents, transport, the basics. [SRC: 13] Later, he stood in front of a group of incarcerated Oklahomans finishing a job-training course and gave the keynote speech. [SRC: 13] A man who had spent forty-eight years inside, telling people about to come out that it was possible to start again.

[SLOW]

Then came the question of compensation. And here, the numbers tell their own story.

Oklahoma's law capped compensation for wrongful imprisonment at one hundred and seventy-five thousand dollars. [SRC: 7] Glynn Simmons received the maximum. [SRC: 7] One hundred and seventy-five thousand dollars, for forty-eight years. Divide it out, and it comes to about ten dollars for each day he spent in prison.

Ten dollars a day.

[NORMAL]

Glynn Simmons also sued. His federal civil rights lawsuit argued that local authorities had ignored clear opportunities to realise he hadn't committed the crime. [SRC: 12] Among those named was the estate of the former Edmond detective who had led the original investigation, Anthony David Garrett. [SRC: 12] Those were allegations in a lawsuit. They were never tested at trial.

In August twenty twenty-four, the City of Edmond agreed to pay him seven point one five million dollars, and in return he dropped his claims against the city and the detective's estate. [SRC: 8] Asked how he felt, he called it "bittersweet." [SRC: 14] It was far more than the state cap. Added together, one analysis calculated that his total compensation came to roughly four hundred and seventeen dollars for each day of his imprisonment. [SRC: 7] Less than a hundred and fifty thousand dollars for each year of a life.

And by then, Glynn Simmons was fighting another battle. Soon after his release, he was diagnosed with stage four liver cancer. [SRC: 9] Supporters raised more than three hundred and fifty thousand dollars through an online fundraiser to help pay for his treatment and the basics of starting a life at seventy: clothes, furniture, transport, food. [SRC: 9]

He had walked out of prison into a world he didn't know, with an illness that threatened to take away the freedom he'd only just been given.

Think about the cruelty of that timing. Forty-eight years waiting. Then freedom, at last, at seventy. And then, almost immediately, a diagnosis that meant chemotherapy, hospital appointments, and uncertainty about how much time he had left to enjoy it. Prison healthcare is notoriously limited, and long-term prisoners often leave with conditions that went undiagnosed or untreated for years. Nobody can say whether earlier care would have changed his illness. But the years taken from him were years in which his health, too, was in the state's hands.

## 11. AFTERMATH + MEANING (49:00–53:00)
[SLOW]

There are other people in this story who never got an ending.

Carolyn Sue Rogers was killed at her work on December thirtieth, nineteen seventy-four. For almost half a century, the official answer to who killed her was Glynn Simmons and Don Roberts. Now, for Simmons, a court has said that answer is wrong. The two men who walked into that liquor store have never been identified. It's very unlikely they ever will be.

Her family was told for decades that the case was solved. Now they live with the knowledge that, at least in part, it wasn't.

That's a different kind of grief, and it's one that wrongful conviction cases create again and again. A family builds its understanding of the worst thing that ever happened to it around a verdict. It goes to the trial. It hears the sentence. It tells itself that, at least, someone paid. And then, decades later, the verdict is taken away, and nothing is put in its place.

Belinda Brown survived being shot as a teenager. She lived with what happened in that store. And then she lived through decades in which her identification was the foundation of a conviction that has now been overturned. Whatever anyone thinks of eyewitness evidence, she was a victim of that robbery. The responsibility for what went wrong with the lineups, and the report, rests with the system that ran them and the system that failed to disclose them.

[NORMAL]

Don Roberts is still living with his conviction. He was paroled in two thousand and eight, but in the eyes of the law he remains guilty. [SRC: 11] He says he is innocent. In twenty twenty-four, prosecutors declined to overturn his conviction, and his lawyers have said they would keep fighting in court. [SRC: 11] Two men were convicted on the same identification. One has been declared innocent. The other has not. That contradiction is still sitting in Oklahoma's legal system today.

[SLOW]

And Glynn Simmons.

He went into prison at twenty-two. He came out at seventy. In between, he lost his entire adult life: the chance to build a career, to have a family of his own if he'd wanted one, to be there for his parents as they grew old. He fought a system that kept telling him no. And when he came out, with a court finally saying he was innocent, his first public message was about hope.

[NORMAL]

So, the question we started with. How was a man who says he was in Louisiana convicted of a murder in Oklahoma, and how did one report stay hidden for so long?

Here's the most honest answer the record allows.

He was convicted because a jury trusted one eyewitness over seven alibi witnesses. And it trusted her because it never saw the report showing she had picked out other people too. That report should have been handed over in nineteen seventy-five. It wasn't. When it surfaced, about twenty years later, the appeals system made it almost impossible to use. And the parole system punished him for refusing to confess to something he hadn't done.

What finally set him free wasn't new science. It was a prosecutor willing to look at an old file and say: we got this wrong.

There's a lesson here about time, too. Wrongful convictions are often described in terms of the moment they happen: the trial, the verdict. But the real damage is done slowly, year by year. Each year a report sits in a file. Each year an appeal is denied on a technicality. Each year a parole board asks for remorse. Forty-eight years is not one injustice. It's seventeen and a half thousand of them, one every morning.

And there's a lesson about who fixes these mistakes. It wasn't a new piece of science, or a dramatic confession by the real killers. It was people: a private investigator who got the file, lawyers who took the case when it looked hopeless, a district attorney willing to reverse her own office's position, and a judge willing to say the word "innocent."

What's striking about this case is how simple the fix would have been. One page, photocopied and handed to the defence in nineteen seventy-five. That's all. One page, and Glynn Simmons might have been home for the next New Year's Eve.

[COMMENT TRIGGER #2]
And for anyone who might one day sit on a jury, there's a lesson in it too. A confident witness is not the same as an accurate one. The most moving testimony in a courtroom can come from someone who is completely sincere and completely mistaken. And the evidence you're not shown can matter as much as the evidence you are.

Which leaves a question for you. Oklahoma's law capped Glynn Simmons's state compensation at one hundred and seventy-five thousand dollars for forty-eight years. What should a state owe someone it wrongly imprisoned for most of their life? I'd like to know what you think.
[/COMMENT TRIGGER]
