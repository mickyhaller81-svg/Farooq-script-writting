# TRUE CRIME STORIES — SCRIPT WRITING RULES
## For 50–55 minute faceless YouTube videos (US / English-speaking rich-country audience)

Version 1.1 — 2026-10-02 (v1.1: curiosity engine §5 rewritten, 5-minute trust CTA added to §4/§9b, word budget per block, halfway reset, comment prompts). Built from research on top long-form channels (EWU, JCS, Casefile, Coffeehouse Crime, Dr Insanity, MrBallen), YouTube's 2025–26 inauthentic-content and advertiser-friendly policies, the May-2026 $17.5M true-crime defamation verdict, and victim-centered ethics frameworks. Sources at the bottom.

These rules apply to EVERY script. A script that fails the §14 checklist does not go to voice.

---

## §0 — The five non-negotiables

1. **One case, one central question.** The whole 50 minutes answers one question ("How did a man with a perfect alibi end up convicted?"). No question = no script.
2. **Every fact traces to a source.** If you cannot name where a claim came from, cut it or label it as a theory.
3. **The victim is a person before they are a case.** They get a name, a life, and a voice before the crime is described.
4. **No graphic description of violence beyond what the viewer needs to understand the case.** Monetization and ethics both die on this line.
5. **Original angle every time.** YouTube's inauthentic-content policy terminates channels that produce templated, same-structure, "text read over slideshow" videos. Every script needs a human editorial point of view that another channel couldn't copy from the same Wikipedia page.

---

## §1 — Case selection

Pick a case only if it passes all of these:

- **Solved or legally resolved (preferred).** Convictions, exonerations, or officially closed cases. Unsolved cases are allowed only when the script's angle is the investigation itself, and all living persons of interest are handled under §7.
- **Enough documented material for 50 minutes without filler.** You need at least: a timeline, 2+ named investigators or witnesses, one piece of physical/digital evidence that turns the case, a courtroom or interrogation phase, and an aftermath. If the source pile gives you 25 minutes, make a 25-minute video or pick another case — never stretch.
- **A "turn".** Something that makes the viewer re-read the first half: a lie exposed, a second victim, an alibi that collapses, a wrong man freed, a detail everyone missed.
- **A searchable + a surprising half.** Mix well-known cases (search demand) with lesser-known cases (no competition, loyal audience). Lesser-known cases are where channels like Kendall Rae and Casefile built their identity.
- **No active family objection.** If the family has publicly asked creators not to cover the case, skip it.
- **No minors as perpetrators, no sexual violence against children as the central crime.** These get age-restricted / limited ads regardless of how carefully written.

Log the case in a Case Sheet before writing one sentence (see §2.3).

## §2 — Research and sourcing

### 2.1 Source hierarchy (use in this order)
1. Court documents: indictments, trial transcripts, sentencing memoranda, appeal rulings (PACER, state court portals, CourtListener).
2. Official records: police reports, autopsy/medical examiner summaries (facts only, no graphic detail), FBI/DOJ press releases.
3. On-the-record statements: named detectives, prosecutors, defense attorneys, family statements, victim impact statements.
4. Reputable journalism: AP, Reuters, local newspapers of record, long-form magazine pieces.
5. Books and documentaries by credited investigators or journalists.
6. **Never as a sole source:** Reddit, forums, TikTok, other YouTubers, Wikipedia (use it only as a map to the primary sources).

### 2.2 Verification rule
- Every date, name, age, place, charge, sentence, and dollar figure is confirmed by **two independent sources**, at least one from tiers 1–3.
- If sources conflict, say so in the script ("Reports differ on whether…"). Do not pick the more dramatic version silently.
- Anything that is a theory stays a theory: "Investigators believed", "Prosecutors argued", "One theory at the time was", "It has never been established whether".

### 2.3 Case Sheet (mandatory, saved beside the script)
```
CASE:            name, location, year
CENTRAL Q:       one sentence
THE TURN:        one sentence, at what minute it lands
VICTIM(S):       name, age, 3 humanizing facts, who speaks for them
LIVING PERSONS:  list everyone alive + their legal status (convicted / acquitted / never charged / witness / family)
TIMELINE:        dated, sourced
EVIDENCE SPINE:  the 1–3 pieces of evidence the story hangs on
SOURCES:         numbered list, tier marked
UNKNOWNS:        what is genuinely not established
```

## §3 — Length and pacing math

- Target runtime: **50–55 minutes of narration** (before intro/outro graphics).
- **Measure the voice first.** Render a 500-word test paragraph with the chosen ElevenLabs voice at the settings you'll use. Count words ÷ minutes = that voice's real WPM. Never assume.
- Word count = minutes × measured WPM. Reference: true-crime narration lands at 130–150 WPM, slower than news. At 140 WPM a 52-minute script is **~7,300 words**; at 130 WPM it is ~6,800; at 150 WPM ~7,800.
- Pacing is not uniform. Slow down (shorter sentences, more pauses) for the discovery, the confession, the verdict. Speed up (compress months into a sentence) for procedural stretches: "Eleven months passed. The file went cold."
- **Compression is a craft rule, not a cheat.** Three years of waiting can be one sentence; the 90 seconds of a 911 call can be two minutes of script.

### 3.1 Word budget per block (at 140 WPM, 52 min ≈ 7,300 words — scale to your measured WPM)

Write a **beat sheet** before prose: one line per beat, with the open loop it opens or pays marked `OPEN:` / `PAY:`. The beat sheet is approved against §5 before any narration is written.

## §4 — The 50–55 minute blueprint

Time blocks are targets, not walls. Adjust to the case, but every block must exist, and every block ends on an open loop (§5).

| Block | Time | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Cold open** | 0:00–1:00 | Drop into the single most gripping moment: the 911 call, the discovery, the one sentence in an interrogation, the knock on the door. Present tense allowed here. No names needed yet. End on the central question, stated or implied. |
| **2. The promise** | 1:00–1:30 | One or two lines that tell the viewer what this video will deliver and why it's different ("Most retellings stop at the arrest. The real story is what the lab found eighteen months later."). This is where the title/thumbnail promise is honored. |
| **3. The world before** | 1:30–5:00 | The victim as a full person. The place. The people around them. Ordinary details that will matter later (plant them here, pay them later). The viewer must like or understand someone before anything happens to them. **Must end on a strong open loop** so the CTA that follows sits on top of a question, not a lull. |
| **4. Trust + CTA** | ~4:00–6:30 (floating) | The one mid-video ask, 20–30 s. **Not a fixed timestamp** — the writer places it at the best open loop inside the 4:00–6:30 window (§9b placement rule). ≤ 60 words, sincere, tied to the research effort, then straight back into the story. |
| **5. The day** | 5:30–13:30 | Reconstruct the event from documented facts. Clear timestamps ("At 6:40 that evening…"). Distinguish what's known from what's inferred. Violence is stated, not described (§9). |
| **6. First response** | 13:30–22:30 | Discovery, 911, first officers, first 48 hours. Introduce investigators by name and role. First evidence. First wrong assumption. |
| **7. The midpoint turn** | 22:30–26:00 | The revelation that changes the shape of the case: a suspect emerges, an alibi cracks, a second incident connects. This is the retention spine. If the case has no midpoint turn, restructure so one lands here. |
| **8. Complications** | 26:00–37:00 | Open with the **halfway reset** (§5.6). Then suspects, dead ends, competing theories, the mistake, the pressure on the family, the detective who wouldn't let go. Multiple timelines run here — map them in the Case Sheet before writing. |
| **9. The break** | 37:00–43:00 | The evidence that solves it, the interrogation, the confession or the DNA hit. Slow the pace. Quote documents and recorded statements where they exist. |
| **10. Resolution** | 43:00–49:00 | Charges, trial, verdict, sentence. Include the defense's argument honestly. Victim impact statements if public. |
| **11. Aftermath + meaning** | 49:00–53:00 | What happened to the people. What the case changed (a law, a procedure, a cold-case unit). What remains unknown. Return to the victim by name. Deliver the thematic answer to the central question. |
| **12. Close** | 53:00–55:00 | Two or three calm lines, the closing CTA (§9b, ≤ 40 words), and a one-line tease of the next case if there is one. No sermon. |

### Alternative structures (use when they serve the case, never for novelty)
- **In medias res** (default above): open at the discovery/arrest, rewind.
- **Dual timeline**: original events intercut with the modern re-investigation (good for cold cases solved by genealogy).
- **Reverse chronology**: open at the exoneration, work back (wrongful-conviction stories).
- **Evidence-spine structure** (EWU style): one recording — bodycam, 911, interrogation — is the backbone; the story is "what this tape doesn't tell you yet".

## §5 — The curiosity engine (how 50 minutes actually holds)

Long videos win on total watch time, not percentage. A 52-minute video held at 40% beats a 20-minute video held at 60%. The job is to make the retention curve step, not slide — and the only thing that does that in a faceless, narration-only video is **curiosity that never fully switches off**. The viewer must always be waiting for something.

### 5.1 The Always-One-Open rule
At every second of the script, **at least one question must be open** in the viewer's mind. Before the script pays a loop, it opens the next one. Check this on the beat sheet: there is never a beat where every `OPEN:` has already been `PAY:`ed.

### 5.2 Three tiers of questions (all three run at once)
- **The spine question** (1 per video): the central question from §0. Opened in the cold open, paid in Aftermath. Restated — in new words, never verbatim — at roughly 5:00, 26:00 and 43:00.
- **Block questions** (1 per block, ~12 per video): each §4 block ends by opening one and the next block pays it 3–8 minutes later. Examples: "The neighbor's statement had one problem." / "What the lab found didn't match either story." / "He had an alibi — and it was a good one."
- **Micro-hooks** (every 60–90 seconds): a one-line tease, a reversal, a withheld detail, a sensory beat, a jump cut in time, a direct question to the viewer. For a faceless channel the writing has to do this; the editor can't save it.

### 5.3 Curiosity devices (use at least 6 different ones per script)
1. **The withheld name.** Don't name the perpetrator until the midpoint turn, even if the case is famous. Refer to "the man in the grey truck", "the second caller".
2. **The specific tease.** Numbers and objects, not adjectives: "Three words on a receipt would end the case" beats "a shocking discovery awaited". The tease must be literally true and literally paid.
3. **The contradiction.** Put two sourced facts side by side that cannot both be true, then move on: "He said he was home by nine. His phone said otherwise."
4. **The innocent detail.** Plant it in The World Before (the spare key, the dog that always barked, the brother's truck); pay it in The Break. Viewers who notice feel smart and stay.
5. **Dramatic irony.** Let the viewer know something a character doesn't: "She didn't know it, but the car had already been reported."
6. **The investigator's doubt.** A named detective "couldn't let one thing go." Say what the thing is 2–5 minutes later.
7. **The wrong road.** Walk the viewer down the investigation's actual wrong theory until it collapses. Real investigations meander; use that as suspense, never invent a theory.
8. **The cost of time.** "Every hour the search widened, the odds fell." Ticking clocks are curiosity with a deadline.
9. **The document reveal.** Quote one line from a transcript, letter, text message or 911 call — then explain its weight afterwards, not before.
10. **The reversal line.** A single short sentence that flips the previous paragraph: "Except that wasn't her car."
11. **The question to the viewer.** Twice per video max (see 5.7): "Put yourself in that interview room. What would you have said?"
12. **The flash-forward.** One sentence from the future: "Two years later, a jury would hear that same sentence read aloud."

### 5.4 Tension and release (MrBallen)
Tension builds through cause and effect; release with a short factual beat; build again. The rhythm inside any 5-minute stretch: build → micro-pay → bigger build → block question. Never 10 minutes of flat exposition, and never 10 minutes of unbroken tension either — viewers numb out.

### 5.5 Scene vs summary
Write key moments as scenes (specific place, time, sensory detail, what was said) and move between them in summary. Mandatory scenes: discovery, first interview, the lie, the break, the verdict. Specificity is tension: "a 2009 silver Honda Civic with a cracked tail light" holds attention; "a car" doesn't.

### 5.6 The halfway reset (open of block 8, ~26:00)
Two sentences, no more: where we are, and the one question that now matters more than before. "By now police had a name. What they didn't have was a single piece of evidence placing him in the house." This re-orients TV/background viewers who drifted and raises the stakes for the second half. This is the only recap allowed in the video.

### 5.7 Comment triggers (helps the algorithm, must stay tasteful)
Twice per video, once around 15:00–20:00 and once in Aftermath, ask the viewer one genuine question tied to the case — a judgment call, not a quiz: "Should the first detective have pushed harder that night?" / "Does the sentence fit what he did?" Never "comment below if you agree", never anything that invites speculation about living people (§7).

### 5.8 Hard timing rules
- **The 25–35 second cliff.** The cold open must land its first jolt inside 25 seconds and its second inside 60.
- **The thumbnail/title promise** is honored (partially) inside the first 90 seconds and fully by the midpoint turn. A viewer who got the title's answer at 48:00 feels cheated for 47 minutes.
- **Signpost every time jump.** "Three days later." "By the following spring." "Eleven months into the investigation."
- **Name the people.** Victims, detectives, witnesses, lawyers — by name and role. Pronoun soup ("he said that he saw him") loses viewers in audio.
- **Pay everything.** Every loop opened is paid on screen, in words. An unpaid loop is clickbait and kills the second view.

## §6 — Narration prose (written for a synthetic voice)

- **Write for the ear.** Sentences 8–20 words. One idea per sentence. Vary length deliberately: a run of short sentences for tension, a longer one for release.
- **Read every paragraph aloud** (or TTS-preview it). Anything you stumble on, rewrite.
- **Tense:** past tense for the narrative; present tense allowed in the cold open and in reconstructed scenes for immediacy, but never switch mid-paragraph.
- **No lists, no bullet-speak, no "firstly/secondly".** Narrate.
- **No filler phrases:** "it's important to note", "as we'll see", "little did they know", "chilling", "shocking", "horrific", "bone-chilling". If the fact is chilling, the fact does the work.
- **No melodrama adjectives on the victim.** "Beautiful young mother" is cliché; "She coached her daughter's under-10 soccer team on Saturdays" is a person.
- **Numbers, dates, names:** write out as the voice should say them ("nineteen ninety-seven", "Detective Maria Alvarez", "Route Sixty-Six"). Spell out acronyms on first use.
- **Pronunciation notes** for every unusual name or place in a `[PRON: ...]` note at the top of the script (stripped before TTS).
- **Quotes from records:** short, attributed, verbatim. "In his statement to police, he wrote: …"
- **The narrator's stance:** calm, measured, precise (Casefile model). Not a comedian, not a preacher, not a cop. The facts carry the weight; the narrator never performs outrage.
- **One point of view throughout.** The narrator does not become "we" and "I" randomly. Pick a stance (third-person documentary) and hold it.
- **Humor:** none. Not in true crime, not with a faceless narrator.

## §7 — Attribution and legal language (this is the $17.5M rule)

In May 2026 a jury awarded $17.5M against a YouTuber who suggested a missing teenager's case was a scam. True crime about living people carries real defamation exposure. Rules:

- **Convicted** people may be called what the verdict says ("convicted of first-degree murder"). Even then, state the charge and the court, not your adjective.
- **Charged but not convicted:** "accused", "charged with", "alleged", "prosecutors say". Always.
- **Acquitted:** state the acquittal clearly and early. Never imply they "got away with it".
- **Never charged / person of interest / cleared:** name them only if the public record did, and say explicitly that they were never charged or were cleared. If naming adds nothing, don't.
- **Family members of perpetrators:** no speculation about what they knew. Facts from the record only.
- **Motive:** always attributed ("Prosecutors argued the motive was…", "The defense claimed…"). Never stated as the narrator's own conclusion.
- **Opinion vs fact:** any narrator interpretation is framed as interpretation ("What's striking about this timeline is…"). A false statement of fact about an identifiable living person is the thing that gets sued. Opinion clearly framed as opinion is not.
- **Fraud/hoax/"staged" theories** about victims or families: banned. This is exactly what the $17.5M verdict was about.
- **Corrections:** if an error is found after publishing, pin a correction comment and fix the description. Keep a corrections log.

## §8 — Ethics (victim-centered)

- Use the victim's **name** throughout. Never "the body" or "the victim" for more than a sentence or two.
- Spend real time (block 3) on who they were. Use their own words if public (journals, posts quoted in court, family statements).
- Do not glorify the perpetrator: no "genius", "mastermind", "charismatic" without a sourced quote; no lingering on their childhood to excuse; no fan-service nicknames.
- No speculation about the victim's choices ("if she hadn't…").
- Acknowledge survivors and families in the aftermath block.
- Where a family has spoken publicly about how they want the case told, follow it.
- The video should leave the viewer knowing something true about justice, investigation, or human behavior — not just entertained by someone's worst day.

## §9 — Advertiser-friendly rules (monetization survival)

YouTube's 2025–26 guidelines allow educational/documentary treatment of violent crime with full ads. Graphic treatment gets limited or no ads, and true crime is reviewed with heightened scrutiny. Rules:

- **State, don't describe.** "She was stabbed multiple times" is a fact. A paragraph on wounds is a demonetization. Autopsy detail: cause of death in one clause, nothing more.
- **No sexual-violence detail.** If it's part of the case, name the charge ("sexual assault") once, in legal language, and move on.
- **Suicide:** stated factually, once, no method detail. Follow safe-messaging language.
- **Children:** victims who are minors are handled with maximum restraint; no detail of harm beyond the charge.
- **First 30 seconds, title, thumbnail, description, tags:** the cleanest part of the whole package. Classifiers weigh these hardest. No blood, no weapons as thumbnail hero, no "brutal/slaughter/butchered" in titles.
- **Profanity:** none in the first 30 seconds, title, or thumbnail. Quoted profanity from records later is tolerated but unnecessary — paraphrase.
- **Mid-roll ad breaks:** YouTube now auto-places mid-rolls at natural pauses. Write them in. Every block boundary in §4 ends on a complete sentence + a beat (the editor places a 1–2 s pause). Mark them `[AD BREAK OK]` in the script. Never let a break fall mid-scene.
- **Visuals brief for the editor** (in the script header): no real crime-scene photos, no autopsy images, no real victim photos used in a sensational way. Reconstructions, maps, documents, court sketches, blurred bodycam are fine.

## §9b — The two CTAs (exactly two, nowhere else)

### The early trust CTA (block 4, floating in the 4:00–6:30 window, ≤ 60 words)
Purpose: tell the viewer this is researched, real-fact storytelling, and ask once for like / comment / subscribe because it pushes the video to more people and keeps the channel going. Rules:
- **Placement is the writer's call, not a clock.** Target ~5:00, but the real rule is: put it at the **strongest open loop between 4:00 and 6:30**. In one video that's 4:10, in another 6:20. Pick the moment where the viewer has just been handed a question they want answered — then the ask sits on top of that question and nobody leaves. Never drop it on a lull, never cut a scene or a quote to hit a timestamp. If The World Before runs long, the CTA simply moves with it; adjust block 5's start accordingly.
- **Hard limits:** never before 3:30 (viewer hasn't earned trust yet), never after 7:00 (the moment for it has passed — if missed, skip it and rely on the closing CTA).
- **Three beats, in this order:** (1) effort + real facts, (2) the ask, (3) why it matters to the channel — then one bridge sentence straight back into the story.
- **Duration:** 20–30 seconds of speech, never more. "5:00–5:30" is the slot in the video, not the length of the ask.
- **Tone — the ask must never feel like an ask.** It is said the way you'd tell a friend something, not the way a channel asks for a favor. Rules:
  - **Delivery, not announcement.** Written as a continuation of the narrator's calm voice, same pace, same volume. No change of energy, no "Hey guys", no "quick interruption", no "before we continue" as a jingle. The first words are about the story or the research, never about YouTube.
  - **Respect the viewer's intelligence.** State what the research cost and why it matters; let them decide. Never tell them what they "need to" or "should" do.
  - **Gratitude before the ask.** Acknowledge that the viewer chose to spend an hour here. Thank first, then one quiet ask.
  - **Forbidden words/phrases:** "smash", "hit that", "don't forget", "make sure you", "it's free", "it costs you nothing", "I'd really appreciate", "it would mean the world", "if you haven't already", "like and subscribe" as a bare command, "the algorithm" said more than once, "notification bell", any exclamation mark, any question mark ("will you?").
  - **Allowed language:** "if this is worth your time", "if that matters to you", "it genuinely helps", "it's what keeps us doing this", "thank you for being here".
  - **One ask, one sentence.** Like, comment and subscribe can be mentioned together, but in one sentence, once.
  - **Exit gracefully.** The bridge sentence back into the story is required and must reference the open loop the viewer is waiting on, so the CTA ends by rewarding them for staying.
  - **Read-aloud test:** if it would make a polite listener wince, rewrite. If it sounds like every other channel, rewrite.
- **Never** interrupt a scene, a quote, or the cold open with it. Never repeat it later.
- **Written fresh every video** — different words each time (templated CTAs are an inauthentic-content signal and viewers skip them).

Reference template (rewrite, don't copy — ≈25 seconds):
> *"Everything you're hearing comes from court records, police files and reporting we checked line by line — it takes weeks to build one of these, and we're grateful you chose to spend this hour with us. If it feels worth your time, a like, a comment or a subscribe quietly tells YouTube to show it to more people, and it's what keeps us doing this. Thank you. Now — that phone call."*

Bad version (what the tone rules exist to prevent):
> *"Hey guys, quick break! If you're enjoying this, make sure you smash that like button, hit subscribe and ring the bell, it's free and it really helps the algorithm! Okay, back to the story!"*

### The closing CTA (block 12, ≤ 40 words)
After the aftermath, before the tease. Thank, ask once, name the next case in one line if there is one. Same tone rules.

### Everything else is banned
No CTA in the cold open, no CTA at the midpoint, no "subscribe" reminders in the middle of the investigation, no end-screen monologue longer than 40 words.

## §10 — Inauthentic-content defense (the 2026 kill rule)

In January 2026 YouTube terminated 16 channels (4.7B views) under the inauthentic-content policy. The policy does not mention AI; it targets templated, mass-produced, verbatim-read content. A faceless, AI-voiced channel survives only by being visibly authored. Rules:

1. **Unique angle per video**, written in the Case Sheet before research. Not "the X case" but "why the X case was solved by a parking receipt".
2. **Human editorial layer that is audible:** analysis of investigator decisions, what the case changed, what the records show that the headlines didn't, where the official story has gaps. At least 10% of runtime is this layer.
3. **Vary the structure** between videos (§4 alternatives). Never the same block-for-block skeleton ten uploads in a row.
4. **Never read a source verbatim.** Paraphrase, synthesize, attribute. Verbatim only for short quoted records.
5. **Primary-source detail other channels don't have.** One court document, one transcript line, one local-paper detail per video that the Wikipedia-readers missed.
6. **Disclosure:** AI narration alone doesn't require the "altered content" label. A **cloned voice of a real person always does and may also violate impersonation rules** — so don't clone real people. Use a Voice Library voice with commercial rights (paid plan) or a Voice Design voice. If any AI-generated realistic visuals are used, tick the altered-content box.
7. **Volume ≠ growth.** 1–2 deeply researched videos a week beat 7 shallow ones and are the difference between monetized and terminated.

## §11 — Title, thumbnail, description, chapters

- **Title:** ≤ 60 characters where possible. Specific + curiosity gap. Formulas that work in true crime: *"The [detail] That Solved [case]"*, *"He Had a Perfect Alibi. Then Police Checked [thing]."*, *"[Number] Years Later, a [object] Reopened the Case"*. No sensational violence words (§9). The title's promise must be paid inside the first 90 seconds and fully by the midpoint.
- **Thumbnail:** 2–3 elements max, ≤ 3–4 words of text, dark cinematic base with one color cast (cold blue-black for calculated crime, red-black for violent, green-black for rural, purple-black for mystery). Tension objects that lift CTR: empty chair, single light in darkness, case file, evidence board, a door. Never a real victim photo as the sensational hero. Follow the channel's locked thumbnail rules if any.
- **Description:** 2–3 sentence summary, then timestamped chapters (one per §4 block, named as hooks not labels: "27:14 — The alibi" not "27:14 — Part 7"), then a Sources section listing the primary sources used (this is both an authenticity signal and a legal shield).
- **Tags/keywords:** case name, location, year, "true crime", "documentary", investigator names. No violence words.

## §13 — Banned list (instant rewrite)

- Any uncited factual claim about a living person.
- "Hoax", "staged", "scam" theories about victims or families.
- Graphic wound/autopsy/sexual-violence description.
- "Chilling", "shocking", "horrific", "brutal", "bone-chilling", "little did they know", "it's important to note".
- The victim unnamed for more than two consecutive sentences.
- A block with no open loop at its end.
- Filler to reach runtime (repeated recaps, generic "crime is terrible" passages, padding about the town's weather).
- Same structure as the previous video.
- A cold open that starts with the victim's birth or "In the small town of…".
- Any CTA other than the two in §9b; a 5-minute CTA over 60 words or placed on a lull instead of an open loop; a CTA that reuses last video's wording.
- A beat where no question is open (§5.1).
- The perpetrator named before the midpoint turn (unless the case's structure is reverse-chronology or the title already names them).
- A tease that isn't literally paid ("shocking discovery" with no discovery).
- More than one recap (the halfway reset is the only one).
- Humor, sarcasm, or narrator outrage.

## §14 — Pre-voice checklist (all must pass)

- [ ] Case Sheet complete; central question and turn written in one sentence each.
- [ ] Every living person listed with legal status; attribution language checked against §7.
- [ ] Two-source rule applied to every date, name, charge, sentence.
- [ ] Word count = measured WPM × 50–55.
