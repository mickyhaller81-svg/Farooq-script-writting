## 3. THE WORLD BEFORE (1:30–5:00)

Her name was Lee Gunsalus Rotatori. She was thirty-two years old. [SRC: 1]

She grew up in Rochester, Minnesota, and if you had found one of her school notebooks, you would have found horses. Horses in the margins, horses on the covers, horses in the spaces between the homework. [SRC: 5] She drew them the way some kids hum: constantly, without thinking about it.

As an adult, living in Michigan, she finally got the real thing. A horse of her own.

There's another detail her family has shared that doesn't quite fit the soft picture. As a teenager, Lee was on the Rochester junior rifle club, alongside her younger brother, Greg. [SRC: 5] The artist who drew horses could also hold a rifle steady and hit what she aimed at.

Her sister, Ann, and her brother, Greg, describe her the same way. Mostly happy. Outgoing. Artistic. A woman with a lot of friends. [SRC: 5]

By the summer of nineteen eighty-two, Lee's life had a lot of moving parts. She had an eleven-year-old son from her first marriage, who lived with his father near Chicago. She still used her first husband's surname, Rotatori, because it was the name her career had been built on. [SRC: 5]

And she had just landed a job that meant a fresh start. Food service director at Jennie Edmundson Hospital, in Council Bluffs, Iowa. [SRC: 2] A city on the east bank of the Missouri River, looking across the water at Omaha.

The move was supposed to happen in stages. Lee would go first, settle into the job, and find a place to live. Until then, she'd stay in a motel. Her husband would stay behind in Michigan and join her once she was settled. [SRC: 4]

Hold on to that detail. Her husband. Staying behind.

The motel was the Best Western Frontier Motor Lodge, at twenty-two sixteen Twenty-Seventh Avenue. It sat right beside the junction of two interstates, I-twenty-nine and I-eighty. [SRC: 1] Hundreds of strangers passed that building every hour. Truckers. Families on road trips. Salesmen. People who would never be in Council Bluffs again.

Lee's room was on the ground floor. Room One-Oh-Six.

On Thursday, June twenty-fourth, Lee did something new employees do when they want a place to feel like home. She said yes to an invitation. A group from the hospital went boating, and Lee went with them. They stayed out on the water for a few hours, until the light began to go. [SRC: 6]

At dusk, the group broke up. Lee drove back toward the motel, and on the way she stopped at a McDonald's.

She ordered food for one person. [SRC: 6]

Then she went back to Room One-Oh-Six. Nobody at the front desk saw her walk in.

The staff at that McDonald's would turn out to be the last people known to have seen Lee Rotatori alive.

The last people known. Because someone else saw her after that.

## 5. THE DAY (5:30–13:30)
[SLOW]

Here is what investigators could establish about the night of June twenty-fourth into June twenty-fifth, nineteen eighty-two. And just as important, here is what they couldn't.

Lee was in her room after dark. She had eaten, or started to. She had changed for bed. [SRC: 4]

Picture the room the way it would have been in nineteen eighty-two. A metal key on a plastic fob. A chain on the door. A television bolted to the dresser, and heavy curtains over a window that looked out onto the parking lot. No security cameras in the corridor. No electronic lock recording who came and went. If you wanted to know who had been at that door, you had one source of information: people. And nobody had seen anything.

Remember the meal for one. It's a small detail, and it's the kind investigators notice. It suggests Lee wasn't expecting company that night. She'd spent the evening with a crowd of new colleagues, said goodnight at dusk, and come back to her room to eat alone and get some sleep before work.

Some time after midnight, someone else was in Room One-Oh-Six.

There was no sign of a break-in. The door hadn't been forced. The window hadn't been forced. [SRC: 6] Investigators would later say there was no sign of a struggle at all.

Sit with that for a second, because it's the first contradiction in this case. A woman who knew almost no one in this city. A motel room at a busy interstate junction. And yet whoever came into that room did not have to break anything to get there.

Either Lee opened the door. Or someone had a way in that didn't need her to.

Both of those possibilities lead somewhere uncomfortable. If she opened it, she either knew the person outside, or she was given a reason good enough to unlatch the chain after midnight in a strange city. A uniform, a name, a story. If she didn't open it, then someone had a key, or knew how to get one.

Investigators in nineteen eighty-two never established which it was. That question stays open to this day.

Lee Rotatori was killed by a single stab wound. [SRC: 1] There was also evidence that she had been sexually assaulted. [SRC: 2]

That is all this video will say about what happened to her body. The rest belongs to her family.

[NORMAL]

The next morning, Friday, June twenty-fifth, was meant to be Lee's first full working day after orientation at Jennie Edmundson Hospital. [SRC: 4] The new food service director didn't arrive.

Think about it from her new boss's side. You've just hired someone to run your hospital kitchens. She's been through orientation. She came out on the boat with the team last night. And now, on the morning she's supposed to start for real, her office is empty.

On its own, a new hire missing a morning isn't alarming. A flat tire. A bad night. A wrong turn in a city you don't know yet. But hours went by, and Lee didn't call. Her boss asked the motel to go and check on her. [SRC: 4]

At twelve twenty-seven in the afternoon, Council Bluffs police were sent to the Best Western Frontier Motor Lodge. A motel employee had found a woman's body in Room One-Oh-Six. [SRC: 1]

The officers who walked in that afternoon saw a room that didn't make sense.

Things were missing. Lee's purse was gone. So was some of her jewelry. [SRC: 6]

So, a robbery. That was the obvious first read. A stranger off the interstate, a ground-floor room, a woman alone, a purse taken.

Except the room hadn't been searched. [SRC: 6]

Drawers weren't pulled out. Belongings weren't thrown across the floor. Whoever had been there hadn't hunted through Lee's things looking for cash. They had taken what was in easy reach, and left.

And then there was the wound itself. One. Not a frenzy. Not a fight. A single, precise injury.

The crime scene team did what crime scene teams did in nineteen eighty-two. They photographed, measured, dusted for fingerprints, and collected physical evidence: bedding, swabs, anything that might carry a trace of whoever had been there. In nineteen eighty-two, the best those traces could tell you was a blood type. A blood type narrows a suspect pool to a large slice of the population. It doesn't name anyone.

DNA fingerprinting wouldn't be used to solve a crime anywhere in the world until the middle of the decade, in England. In Iowa, in the summer of nineteen eighty-two, it simply didn't exist.

But someone on that crime scene team bagged the evidence properly, labelled it, and sent it to storage. It's an unglamorous decision, made by a person whose name never appears in the headlines. Without it, there would be no story to tell. Hold on to that, too.

To a burglar, a woman waking up is a problem to be escaped. To a stranger with violent intentions, things usually go differently: chaos, noise, struggle. What police found in Room One-Oh-Six didn't look like either of those.

The scene raised questions that the evidence couldn't answer yet. How did someone get into the room without breaking in? Why take a purse and not search the room? Why one wound? And why would anyone want a newcomer, a woman almost nobody in Council Bluffs even knew yet, dead?

There was one reading that answered all four questions at once. Investigators would come back to it again and again over the next forty years.

It didn't look like a robbery. It didn't look like a random attack.

To at least one detective, it looked like a job. [SRC: 12]

## 6. FIRST RESPONSE (13:30–22:30)
[NORMAL]

"Looked like a job" is the kind of phrase that sounds definitive on television. In real life, in a police department in nineteen eighty-two, it was something much weaker. A feeling. A pattern. A theory with no name attached to it.

Because a job needs someone who wants it done. And Council Bluffs police could not find anyone who wanted Lee Rotatori dead.

Ten days after the murder, on July fourth, nineteen eighty-two, the Omaha World-Herald ran an update on the investigation. The detective sergeant on the case, Larry Williams, gave the paper two lines that tell you exactly where things stood. [SRC: 11]

The first was about the motel's location, right on top of that interstate junction.

"The killer," he said, "could have been five feet away, or a thousand miles away."

The second was even more honest.

"We don't yet even have a motive."

Think about what that first sentence means. A motel at a crossroads of two interstates is the perfect place to disappear from. By the time Lee's boss started wondering where she was, whoever had been in Room One-Oh-Six could have been in Kansas City. Or Chicago. Or back across the river in Omaha, eating breakfast.

Or still in Council Bluffs, reading about it in the paper.

[SLOW]

So detectives did what detectives always do first, in every case like this. They looked at the people closest to the victim.

And the person closest to Lee was her husband.

He hadn't come to Iowa yet. That was the plan, remember. Lee would go ahead, he would follow. In the summer of nineteen eighty-two, the two of them had a complicated history. They had married once, in nineteen seventy-eight. Divorced the following year. Then remarried, at the very end of December nineteen eighty-one. [SRC: 9] Six months before Room One-Oh-Six.

In any murder investigation, a husband in that position would get a long, hard look.

He got one. And he had an answer. On the night Lee was killed, he was in Michigan. Hundreds of miles from Council Bluffs. Investigators checked it. His alibi held. [SRC: 4]

He was ruled out.

That is the record, and this video will say it plainly: in nineteen eighty-two, Lee's husband was investigated and cleared. He was not in that motel room.

But there was something else in the file on Lee's husband. Something that made it thicker than a file on a grieving spouse usually is. It had nothing to do with anything he had done that summer. It was about something that happened more than twenty years earlier, in Chicago.

We'll come back to it. The detectives would too. It just took them thirty-nine years.

[NORMAL]

With the husband cleared, there was nowhere obvious left to go. The hospital offered a reward. Local businesses added to it. [SRC: 2] Officers canvassed the motel and the surrounding area. Nobody came forward with anything that led anywhere.

For Lee's family, the waiting started that summer and didn't stop. Her son was eleven. He would grow up, finish school, become an adult, all without knowing who had taken his mother or why. Her brother and sister would get older carrying the same question. The hospital filled her position. The motel rented out Room One-Oh-Six again.

That's how cold cases happen. Not with a decision to give up, but with a slow pile-up of other work. New murders, new victims, new families demanding answers today. Detectives retire. Their notes get passed on to people who never walked into the room.

The summer ended. Then the year. The file on Lee Rotatori went into a box, and the box went onto a shelf.

[SLOW]

And here's where the timelines start to cross. Because while Council Bluffs police were still asking who had been in Room One-Oh-Six, about four hundred and fifty miles to the southeast, in the farm country of southern Illinois, something else was happening that nobody in Iowa would hear about for nearly forty years.

Hold that thought. Iowa didn't know. Neither do you, yet.

[NORMAL]

Let's move forward. To two thousand and one.

By then, forensic science had changed in a way that would have sounded like science fiction in nineteen eighty-two. Investigators sent the physical evidence from Lee's case to the Iowa Division of Criminal Investigation, the state crime lab. [SRC: 2] And the technicians there found something.

A male DNA profile.

Someone had left a piece of himself in Room One-Oh-Six. A genetic signature that belonged to exactly one person on Earth.

The profile was entered into the state and national DNA databases, including CODIS, the FBI's Combined DNA Index System, which holds the profiles of people convicted of crimes. The national system had only been running for a few years. Every week, it grew. Every week, there was a new chance that the man from Room One-Oh-Six had been arrested somewhere for something, swabbed, and added.

No match. [SRC: 2]

That told investigators something. Whoever this man was, he had never been put into the system. Either he had never been convicted of a qualifying crime, or his profile had never been collected.

The lab didn't give up. Every so often, they ran the profile again, in case someone new had been added. Year after year, the answer came back the same.

No match. No match. No match.

A name was sitting inside that evidence box the whole time. Nobody had a way to read it.

In two thousand and eleven, the case landed on the desk of Council Bluffs police detective Steve Andrews. [SRC: 12] He was joined by Katie Pattee, who ran the department's crime lab. Two people, one box, and a murder that was already almost thirty years old.

For eight more years, Andrews and Pattee kept the file alive. They re-ran the profile. They re-read the old reports. They had something nobody in nineteen eighty-two had, a genetic signature of the man in that room, and they still couldn't do anything with it. It's a particular kind of frustration, holding the answer in your hands and not being able to read it.

Then, in April of twenty eighteen, something happened in California that changed cold-case work across the country. Investigators identified a suspect in the Golden State Killer case, decades after the crimes, not through a criminal database, but through a public genealogy website, by tracing his relatives. Suddenly, police departments everywhere were looking at their own evidence boxes in a different way.

A year later, in April of twenty nineteen, Council Bluffs tried it.

They sent Lee's DNA profile to Parabon NanoLabs, a private lab in Virginia that works with police on a technique called investigative genetic genealogy. [SRC: 3]

And less than a year later, in March of twenty twenty, Captain Todd Weddum of the Council Bluffs Police Department opened his inbox and found an email from someone he'd never heard of. [SRC: 3]

A college student. From Pennsylvania.

Offering to help.

## 7. THE MIDPOINT TURN (22:30–26:00)
[SLOW]

The student's name was Eric Schubert. And he wasn't just any college student with a hobby. He had a reputation, built quietly through work on other cold cases, for building family trees out of DNA faster and more accurately than people twice his age. Investigators would later call him a genealogy whiz kid. [SRC: 2]

Here's how what he did actually works.

CODIS only compares a DNA profile against people already in the criminal database. Genetic genealogy does something different. It compares the profile against people who have voluntarily uploaded their own DNA to public genealogy sites, usually to find relatives. Ordinary people. Retirees chasing their family history.

The killer was not in that database. But his distant cousins might be.

And if you find enough cousins, and you know how they're related to one another, you can build the family tree backwards, generation by generation, and then forward again, until all the branches meet at one person.

Working with Parabon, Schubert started building. Cousins. Then great-grandparents. Then their children. Then their children's children. Branch by branch, the tree narrowed toward a single family in southern Illinois.

It's slow, painstaking work. Obituaries. Marriage records. Census pages. Old newspaper announcements of births and weddings. Each cousin match is just a number, a measure of how much DNA two people share, and every number could fit more than one family relationship. A second cousin and a half first cousin can look almost the same. Get one branch wrong and you can spend weeks building a tree around the wrong family.

It took almost a year.

By February of twenty twenty-one, it had narrowed to two brothers. [SRC: 13]

Based on their ages in nineteen eighty-two, one brother fit and the other didn't. [SRC: 13]

For the first time in thirty-nine years, the man in Room One-Oh-Six had a name.

Thomas Oscar Freeman. [SRC: 2]

Born May tenth, nineteen forty-seven, in Herrin, Illinois. [SRC: 7] A truck driver, from the small town of West Frankfort. A man whose job was the interstate.

Thirty-five years old in the summer Lee died.

And detectives in Council Bluffs did what you do when you finally have a name after thirty-nine years. They went looking for Thomas Oscar Freeman.

They didn't find an old man in a recliner in southern Illinois. They didn't find a prison record. They didn't find an address.

They found a homicide file.

On October thirtieth, nineteen eighty-two, four months after Lee Rotatori died in Room One-Oh-Six, authorities in Union County, Illinois, found a man's remains buried in a shallow grave, just outside a small town called Cobden. [SRC: 7]

He had been shot. More than once. And he had been in that grave for about three months. [SRC: 7]

Do the math. Lee died at the end of June. Three months before the end of October puts his death at roughly the end of July.

The man police say killed Lee Rotatori was murdered himself only weeks after she was.

It was Thomas Oscar Freeman.

Whoever shot him and buried him has never been identified. [SRC: 1]

So the detectives in Council Bluffs had solved the question they'd been asking for thirty-nine years, only to find the answer had been dead for thirty-nine years too. And standing behind him was a brand-new question, darker than the first.

Who kills a killer, weeks after the crime, and makes sure he's never found?

## 8. COMPLICATIONS (26:00–37:00)
[NORMAL]

By the spring of twenty twenty-one, Council Bluffs finally had a name. What it didn't have was a living suspect, or any idea why a truck driver from southern Illinois had been inside Room One-Oh-Six.

[HALFWAY RESET ENDS]

So the question underneath this whole case has changed shape. It's no longer just who killed Lee Rotatori. It's what connects her death in Iowa to a shallow grave in Illinois, a few weeks later, and four hundred and fifty miles away.

Before detectives could chase that, they had a problem. Everything so far was a family tree. A strong one. But a tree is an inference, and an inference isn't proof. They needed to confirm that the DNA from Room One-Oh-Six was actually Thomas Freeman's.

Freeman had been dead for almost forty years. Testing him directly wasn't an option.

But DNA is inherited. Half of a person's DNA comes from each parent. So if Freeman had a child, that child would carry half of him.

He did. Detectives tracked down Freeman's daughter. [SRC: 7]

And this needs saying clearly, because it's easy to forget the people at the edges of stories like this: Freeman's daughter did nothing wrong. She was a member of the public, approached by police about the father she'd lost decades earlier, and asked to help. She agreed. She gave a sample voluntarily.

That sample went to the Iowa state crime lab. The result was a parent-and-child match with the DNA from Lee's case. [SRC: 13]

The tree was right. The man in Room One-Oh-Six was Thomas Oscar Freeman.

[SLOW]

It's worth pausing on how much depended on that moment. Genetic genealogy has its critics. Some people worry about police searching through the DNA of people who uploaded it to find long-lost relatives, not to help solve crimes. Most genealogy sites now ask users to opt in before their profiles can be used by law enforcement. In a case like this, though, the final step isn't a database at all. It's a person, deciding to say yes.

So who was he?

This is where the public record gets thin, and this video is going to stay inside it. Freeman was born in Herrin, Illinois. He lived in West Frankfort. He drove a truck. [SRC: 7] He was a father. He was thirty-five when he died.

That's it. There's no public record of a criminal past. No known connection to Lee. No known reason to have been in Council Bluffs in late June of nineteen eighty-two, other than what truck drivers always have: a route.

And a route is exactly what makes the first theory so tempting.

[NORMAL]

Here's how that first theory goes. A trucker on the interstate. A motel at an interstate junction. A woman alone in a ground-floor room. A crime of opportunity, by a stranger who drove on and was never seen again in Iowa.

It's a neat theory. It explains why nobody in Council Bluffs recognized anyone. It explains why the DNA never matched anyone local. It even explains Sergeant Williams's line: five feet away, or a thousand miles away.

But it doesn't explain the things that bothered investigators in nineteen eighty-two, and it doesn't explain what happened to Freeman afterward.

Go back to Room One-Oh-Six. No forced entry. No struggle. A room that wasn't searched. A single wound. Those details never looked like a random stranger, and the DNA match didn't change that.

And now there was a second problem. If Freeman was just a stranger who happened past a motel, then his own murder a few weeks later was a cosmic coincidence. One violent stranger, killed by another violent stranger, in an unrelated crime, at almost the same time.

Coincidences happen. But detectives are trained not to like them.

There was also the question of distance. West Frankfort, Freeman's home, is in the far south of Illinois. Council Bluffs is on the far western edge of Iowa. Between them lie Missouri, the Mississippi, and more than six hours of driving. A trucker could plausibly be anywhere, but there was nothing in the public record to show Freeman had a reason to be at that particular motel, on that particular night.

So either Thomas Freeman ended up in Room One-Oh-Six by chance. Or someone pointed him at it.

[SLOW]

So let's go to southern Illinois. Summer, nineteen eighty-two.

Cobden is a tiny town in Union County, in the far south of the state. Orchards. Rolling hills. Back roads. The kind of place where a person who wanted to make something disappear could find a quiet patch of ground.

Somewhere around the end of July, Thomas Freeman was shot multiple times. Someone dug a shallow grave and put him in it. [SRC: 7]

He lay there through August, September, and most of October. When his remains were found on October thirtieth, his death became a homicide case for Illinois investigators. [SRC: 7] A trucker from West Frankfort, shot and buried. No suspect. No obvious motive.

Lay the two calendars side by side, and the summer of nineteen eighty-two looks very different.

Thursday, June twenty-fourth. Lee goes boating with her new colleagues, buys a meal for one, and goes back to Room One-Oh-Six.

Friday, June twenty-fifth. After midnight, Thomas Freeman is in that room. By lunchtime, Council Bluffs police are standing in it.

July. In Iowa, detectives interview, canvass, and tell the newspaper they don't have a motive. Somewhere in that same month, around four hundred and fifty miles away, Thomas Freeman is still alive.

Late July. Freeman is shot and buried near Cobden.

August, September, October. In Iowa, Lee's case starts to go cold. In Illinois, nobody even knows yet that Freeman is dead.

October thirtieth. His remains are found.

Two families grieving, in two states, in the same autumn. Neither one had any idea the other existed.

Nobody in Union County knew that Freeman's DNA was sitting in an evidence box in Iowa. Nobody in Iowa knew that the man they were looking for was already in the ground in Illinois. Two police departments, two unsolved killings, a few weeks and a few hundred miles apart. And for almost forty years, nothing connected the two files except a man nobody knew to look for.

[NORMAL]

This is the moment to be precise about what genetic genealogy can and cannot do, because this case is almost a perfect demonstration of both.

What it can do is extraordinary. From a sample collected in nineteen eighty-two, entered into a database in two thousand and one, and matched to no one for eighteen years, it produced a name, a birthdate, a hometown, and a daughter willing to confirm it. That's real. It's something investigators in the nineteen-eighties couldn't have dreamed of.

But here's what it can't do. It can tell you whose DNA was in a room. It can't tell you why he was there. It can't tell you whether he came alone, or whether he was sent. It can't tell you who he talked to on the drive back, or who was waiting for him when he got home.

DNA answers who. It is silent on why.

And in this case, "why" was the question that mattered most, because the man who could have answered it had been dead for thirty-nine years.

[SLOW]

So detectives started doing the old-fashioned kind of work. Maps. Names. Old files. And something kept bothering them about the map.

Look at where Freeman was buried. Cobden, Illinois.

Now look about fifteen miles to the north. There's a college town called Carbondale, home to Southern Illinois University. [SRC: 10]

Why does Carbondale matter?

Because someone from the very first page of Lee's file had a connection to it.

Detectives went back to the oldest documents in the box. Past the DNA reports, past the genealogy charts, past the twenty-first century. All the way back to nineteen eighty-two, and to the one person who had been cleared on day one.

Her husband.

## 9. THE BREAK (37:00–43:00)
[SLOW]

Lee's husband was Gerald Stanley Nemke. Everyone called him Jerry. [SRC: 9]

And the reason his file was thicker than most is written into the public record, in the decisions of the Illinois Supreme Court.

On April twenty-ninth, nineteen sixty, in Chicago, Jerry Nemke had just turned seventeen. That evening he was with a sixteen-year-old girl named Marilyn Duncan, a friend of his. Two days later, Marilyn died. [SRC: 8]

Jerry Nemke was charged with her murder. A jury convicted him. He was sentenced to death. [SRC: 8]

In nineteen sixty-two, the Illinois Supreme Court reversed that conviction. The justices found that his preliminary hearing had been so restricted that it wasn't possible to properly judge whether his confession was reliable. [SRC: 8]

So he was tried again. And he was convicted again, and sentenced to prison. [SRC: 8]

Jerry Nemke spent roughly the next eighteen years in the Illinois prison system, including time at Menard Correctional Center, a maximum-security prison in southern Illinois. He was released in nineteen seventy-eight. [SRC: 10]

[PAUSE]

That same year, nineteen seventy-eight, on August fifteenth, in Madison, Wisconsin, he married Lee. [SRC: 9]

They divorced in nineteen seventy-nine. They remarried on December thirtieth, nineteen eighty-one. [SRC: 9]

Six months later, Lee was dead in Room One-Oh-Six, and her husband was hundreds of miles away in Michigan, with an alibi that held.

And here's the thing about an alibi. It's one of the most powerful tools in a murder investigation, and it answers exactly one question: where were you? It proves a person wasn't in the room. It says nothing at all about whether they arranged for someone else to be.

In nineteen eighty-two, with no DNA and no name for the man in Room One-Oh-Six, there was no second person to connect anyone to. The alibi was the end of the road. In twenty twenty-one, with a name in hand, detectives could finally ask the other question.

And according to authorities, Nemke and Freeman were not strangers. They had been acquainted before nineteen eighty-two. [SRC: 10]

There's one more thread, and it comes from investigators speaking in A&E's Cold Case Files, in an episode that aired in twenty twenty-five under the title "Contracted to Kill." [SRC: 12] As detectives described it there, during the years Jerry Nemke was in prison, he served time alongside another man. And that man, they said, was a friend and co-worker of Thomas Oscar Freeman.

A man in prison with the husband. The same man, a friend of the trucker. A college town fifteen miles from the trucker's grave.

You can see the shape that detectives saw.

[SLOW]

Here is the theory, as investigators have described it. Stated carefully, because it is a theory, and it has never been tested in a courtroom.

Detectives suspect that Lee's husband hired Thomas Freeman to kill her. [SRC: 12] That Freeman drove to Council Bluffs, to a motel at an interstate junction, where a woman alone in a ground-floor room would, perhaps, open the door. That the purse and the jewelry were taken to make it look like something it wasn't. That a single wound was a sign of someone who had come to do one thing.

And that, a few weeks later, Freeman wasn't paid the way he'd been promised. One summary of the investigators' account put it bluntly: he was paid in lead, not gold. [SRC: 12]

After Freeman was identified, investigators named Jerry Nemke as a person of interest in Thomas Freeman's death. [SRC: 10]

[PAUSE]

And now, the fairness this story requires. Because it matters, and because a theory this dark should be held to the facts.

Jerry Nemke had an alibi for the night Lee was killed. It was checked, and it held. He was never charged in Lee's death. He was never charged in Thomas Freeman's death. No court has ever heard the evidence against him in either case, because there has never been a case.

And he can't answer any of it. Jerry Nemke died in twenty nineteen. [SRC: 10]

Which means he died two years before the family tree came back with Thomas Freeman's name. Whatever he knew, or didn't know, about the man in Room One-Oh-Six, he took it with him. Before anyone could ask.

If detectives are right, this case had two killers. Only one of them has ever been named by evidence. And both of them are beyond the reach of any court.

## 10. RESOLUTION (43:00–49:00)
[NORMAL]

So, after all of that, what does it mean to say the murder of Lee Rotatori has been solved?

Who killed her has an answer now. Why does not, and may never.

On Friday, February twenty-fifth, twenty twenty-two, Council Bluffs police captain Todd Weddum announced in a press release that the department had cleared the cold-case homicide of Lee Rotatori. [SRC: 1] Thirty-nine years and eight months after Room One-Oh-Six.

He gave the credit where it was due. Detective Andrews. Katie Pattee and the crime lab. Parabon NanoLabs. And the college student from Pennsylvania who'd sent an email out of nowhere.

"It took this new technology," Weddum said, "to solve this case." [SRC: 3]

It's worth understanding what "cleared" means here, because it's not what most people picture when they hear a case is closed.

There was no arrest. There were no charges. There was no trial. There was no jury, no defense attorney, no cross-examination, no verdict. When police identify a suspect who has already died, they can close the case administratively. In police language it's often called "cleared by exceptional means." It means investigators are satisfied they know who did it, and there is no one left to prosecute.

The standard the FBI uses for that kind of clearance is strict. Police must know who the offender is. They must have enough evidence to support an arrest and a charge. They must know where that person is. And something outside their control, such as the offender's death, must make an arrest impossible. In this case, they knew exactly where Thomas Freeman was. He'd been in the ground since nineteen eighty-two.

That's a real conclusion, and in this case it rests on hard science. A crime-scene DNA profile. A family tree. A parent-and-child match confirmed by the state crime lab.

[SLOW]

But since there was never a trial, there was never a defense either. So let's give the other side the hearing it never had.

On the question of who was in Room One-Oh-Six, the DNA is strong evidence. A defense lawyer couldn't easily argue it away.

On the bigger question, whether Lee was killed because someone paid for it, the defense would have a great deal to say. Jerry Nemke's alibi was checked in nineteen eighty-two and it held. Knowing someone isn't the same as hiring them. Going to college fifteen miles from a crime scene isn't evidence of a crime. Sharing a prison with someone who later became a trucker's friend is a connection, not a contract. And a conviction from nineteen sixty, however serious, is not proof of anything that happened in nineteen eighty-two. In a courtroom, a judge might not even let a jury hear about it.

Every one of those points is fair. Taken together, they're why this remains a suspicion, and has never become a verdict.

[NORMAL]

After Freeman was identified, the Council Bluffs Police Department began working with the Illinois State Police, looking at whether Freeman's murder was connected to what he had done in Iowa. [SRC: 10]

The Illinois case is still open. As of twenty twenty-five, no one has been charged with the murder of Thomas Oscar Freeman. [SRC: 1]

That's a strange sentence to sit with. Thomas Freeman is the man police say killed Lee Rotatori. He is also, officially, a murder victim himself, with an open file, and the Illinois State Police are still trying to find out who killed him. His daughter lost a father in nineteen eighty-two, and then, almost forty years later, learned what police say he had done weeks before he died. Both of those things are true at once.

Detective Andrews summed up Iowa's position in an interview with a southern Illinois television station. On their side of the river, Council Bluffs had officially closed its case. [SRC: 14] What happened in Illinois belongs to Illinois.

Think about where that leaves the people who loved Lee.

For forty years, they had no answer at all. Then, in twenty twenty-two, they got one. A name. A face, from an old photograph. The certainty of science. And right behind it came a second set of questions, about her own husband, that might never be answered.

What do you do with half an answer?

## 11. AFTERMATH + MEANING (49:00–53:00)
[SLOW]

Lee's younger sister, Ann Chinn, lives in Rochester, Minnesota. That's where Lee is buried. Her brother, Greg Gunsalus, splits his time between St. Louis and Las Vegas. [SRC: 5]

When the news came, they didn't talk about DNA, or Thomas Freeman, or theories. They talked about their sister. The girl who drew horses. The one who was mostly happy and outgoing. The artist with lots of friends. [SRC: 5]

That's worth holding on to, because it's very easy for a case like this to swallow the person at its center. Lee's story became a story about genetic genealogy, a trucker, a shallow grave, and a husband's record. She is in danger of becoming the least interesting person in her own murder case.

She shouldn't be.

Lee Rotatori was thirty-two years old. She had a son who was eleven. She had just taken the biggest job of her career, in a city where she knew almost no one, and on her last evening she said yes when her new co-workers asked her to come out on the boat. She stayed until the light went. She picked up dinner for one. She was going to start work in the morning.

Everything that happened after that was done to her. None of it was about who she was.

Her son is the person in this story we know the least about, and that's how it should be. He was eleven in nineteen eighty-two. By the time the DNA came back, he was a man in his fifties, older than his mother ever got to be. Whatever the answer meant to him, it's his to keep. But it's worth remembering that every year this file sat on a shelf, someone was growing up without her.

And it's worth remembering the people who refused to let the file stay shut. A crime scene technician in nineteen eighty-two who bagged the evidence properly. A lab analyst in two thousand and one. A detective and a crime lab manager who carried the case for a decade. A college student who sent an email to a police captain he'd never met.

[NORMAL]

So, the question this video started with. Who killed Lee Rotatori, and why did solving it open a second murder instead of closing the first?

Here's the most honest answer the record allows.

The DNA says Thomas Oscar Freeman was in that room, and police say he killed her. Science got that far, across almost forty years, from a sample that had been in a box since nineteen eighty-two. That part of the case is as solved as it can be.

But a killing in a motel room led to a killing in an Illinois field. A field fifteen miles from where the victim's husband had gone to college. And that's where the science runs out. DNA can carry a name across forty years. It can't carry a reason. In this case, the reason, if there was one, may have died with the only two men who knew it.

What's striking about this case is how close the answer came, and how late. Freeman's name came back in twenty twenty-one. Jerry Nemke died in twenty nineteen. Two years. If Lee's DNA had reached a genealogist a little sooner, detectives could have knocked on a door and asked a living man a question.
