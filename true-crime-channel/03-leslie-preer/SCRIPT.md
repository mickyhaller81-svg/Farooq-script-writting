## 1. COLD OPEN (0:00–1:00)
[SLOW]

June eighteenth, twenty twenty-four. A small interview room at a police station in Maryland. A camera high in the corner.

At the table sits a man in his forties. Calm. Polite. No criminal record. A job, an apartment in Washington, D.C., a life that, by every outward sign, is completely ordinary.

Across from him, two detectives. One of them leans forward and says, gently, almost kindly:

"Well, honey, your DNA was in the crime scene." [SRC: 10]

The man doesn't shout. He doesn't storm out. He looks at the table and says:

"I don't know. I don't know. I don't know."

The crime scene they're talking about is twenty-three years old. The woman who died there was the mother of someone he once loved.

And the most remarkable thing about this room isn't what the DNA says. It's that police had been given this man's name, in writing, more than twenty-two years earlier.

## 3. THE WORLD BEFORE (1:30–5:00)
[NORMAL]

Her name was Leslie Ann Preer. She was born Leslie Jennings, in nineteen fifty-two, in North Kingstown, Rhode Island. [SRC: 1]

She grew up the way military kids grow up. Her family was large, and it moved, across the country, from base to base, school to school. [SRC: 1] Children raised like that learn something early: how to walk into a room full of strangers and make it feel like home. How to be the new person, again and again.

Leslie went on to study journalism at the University of Florida. [SRC: 1] A journalist's instinct is a particular kind of curiosity: you want to know people's stories, and you want to tell them properly.

In nineteen eighty-two, she settled in Chevy Chase, Maryland, with her husband, Carl Preer, who everyone called Sandy, and their daughter, Lauren. [SRC: 1]

Chevy Chase sits right on the north-west border of Washington, D.C. Big trees. Old houses with deep porches. Lawyers, diplomats, journalists, government officials. It's the kind of suburb where people leave their back doors unlocked during the day, because nothing ever happens there.

The Preers lived on Drummond Avenue. [SRC: 1]

Leslie had a job, and she had something she did for no money at all. At a local library, she volunteered teaching English as a second language. [SRC: 1] Think about who sits in those classes. New arrivals. People who had just moved somewhere strange, didn't know the language, and needed someone patient to help them belong. The military kid who'd been the new person in a dozen schools spent her free time making other new people feel at home.

The family had a dog. His name was Boomer. [SRC: 7] Remember Boomer.

Picture the house in the mid-nineteen-nineties. A teenage daughter. Friends from school coming and going. A dog who greets everybody at the door. From what her family has said since, Leslie was the kind of mother who made her daughter's friends feel welcome.

And as Lauren grew up, the Preer house became the kind of house teenagers like to hang out in. When Lauren was fifteen, she started dating a boy from the same neighbourhood. [SRC: 14] He was polite, funny, a bit mischievous. They stayed together for about five years. [SRC: 6]

Leslie liked him. A lot. He came on family outings. He came on family vacations. [SRC: 6] For a few years, he was almost part of the household. He knew the house. He knew the dog. He knew the family's routines. Which door was usually unlocked. What time people left in the morning. Who would be home, and when.

Those are the things you learn about a family without trying, when they let you in. They're also, as this story will show, the things that matter most when a door has no sign of forced entry.

Then, the way teenage relationships do, it ended. Lauren and the boy went their separate ways. By the spring of two thousand and one, they hadn't spoken in a long time. [SRC: 6]

On the morning of Wednesday, May second, two thousand and one, Leslie Preer was home on Drummond Avenue. It was, as one later documentary would title it, a perfect spring morning.

She was supposed to go to work that day. She never arrived.

## 5. THE DAY (5:30–13:30)
[NORMAL]

That morning, Sandy Preer was out of the house. He would later tell police he'd spent it running errands. [SRC: 8] Leslie was home alone, getting ready for the day. Showering before work. One of the most ordinary, private, unguarded moments in anyone's routine. The back door, in a neighbourhood where people didn't worry much about back doors, was unlocked.

Nobody on Drummond Avenue reported seeing anyone come or go. On a weekday morning in a commuter suburb, most of the street was already at work. Driveways empty. Kids at school. The kind of quiet that makes a neighbourhood feel safe, and that also means nobody is watching.

When Leslie didn't show up for work, it was noticed. She was reliable. People who are always on time get missed quickly.

Her employer was concerned. So was her husband. Sandy Preer and someone from her workplace went to the house on Drummond Avenue to check on her. [SRC: 1]

Montgomery County police officers were called. And inside the house, they found Leslie.

[SLOW]

She was in the shower. [SRC: 7] She had been killed in a violent assault. [SRC: 1]

That is as much as this video will describe about what happened to her body. What matters for understanding the case is what the officers saw around her.

There was blood in the front hall, the foyer. [SRC: 7] Whatever had happened had not started or stayed in one room.

The back door was unlocked. [SRC: 7]

And there was no sign of forced entry. [SRC: 7] No broken glass. No splintered frame. No jimmied lock. Whoever had been in the house that morning had either walked in through a door that was open, or been let in.

[NORMAL]

And then there was the dog.

Boomer was in the house. He was frightened, terrified even, but he hadn't been hurt. [SRC: 7]

On its own, that could mean anything. Dogs hide. Dogs run. But think about it for a moment as a detective would. A family dog, in his own home, with a stranger breaking in and attacking the woman he lived with. Many dogs would bark the street awake. Some would fight. And a person who had come to hurt someone might well hurt the dog too, to keep it quiet.

Boomer was frightened, but untouched.

It's one of those small details that doesn't prove anything, and yet sits in your mind. It's possible the person in the house that morning was someone Boomer didn't treat as a stranger.

[SLOW]

There was one more thing. And it would turn out to be the most important evidence in the entire case.

Under Leslie's fingernails, investigators found skin and blood that weren't hers. [SRC: 6]

Leslie fought back. In the middle of the attack, she scratched the person who was hurting her, hard enough to take some of him with her. It's a detail that tells you something about who Leslie was in the last minutes of her life. And it meant that, somewhere under a microscope, she had left investigators the identity of the man who killed her.

In two thousand and one, though, that evidence couldn't name him. It could exclude people. It could confirm a suspect. But it could only match someone if police already knew who to compare it with.

Here's how DNA worked in a police lab at the time. Analysts looked at a small set of specific locations on the DNA, places where people tend to differ. The result was a kind of genetic barcode. Compare two barcodes, and you could say with enormous confidence whether they came from the same person.

The national database, CODIS, held barcodes from people who had been convicted of certain crimes. If the killer had ever been convicted of one of those crimes and swabbed, the database could find him. If he hadn't, the barcode was just a lock without a key. You needed to bring the key to it: a suspect, and a sample from that suspect.

So from the very first day, the evidence in this case was waiting for one thing. A name.

[NORMAL]

So, an unforced entry. An unlocked back door. A dog who wasn't harmed. A struggle, and DNA under her nails. Put together, those facts pointed investigators in one direction.

Someone who knew the house. Someone who knew the family. Someone Leslie, and maybe Boomer, recognized.

And in a murder investigation, when the evidence says "someone close to her," the first person police look at is always the same.

## 6. FIRST RESPONSE (13:30–22:30)
[NORMAL]

Sandy Preer became the focus of the investigation almost immediately. [SRC: 3]

From the detectives' point of view, it isn't hard to see why. A violent killing inside the family home. No forced entry. A husband who had a key, who knew the house, who the dog obviously knew. Statistically, when a married woman is killed at home, the person responsible is most often her partner. Police know that number by heart.

And as detectives looked more closely at Sandy, some things seemed to them to fit.

He gave investigators a very detailed account of his morning. He said he'd been running errands. A trip to the dump. Stops at three different stores, looking for a battery charger. [SRC: 8] Years later, detectives reviewing the old file would note that an account that detailed can look, to suspicious eyes, like an attempt to build an alibi. [SRC: 8]

It can. It can also look exactly like what an innocent man says when he's asked to remember every minute of the worst morning of his life. Detailed answers are what police ask for. And then, sometimes, detail itself becomes suspicious.

According to later accounts, Sandy also took a polygraph test, and the results were not in his favour. [SRC: 8]

It's worth saying here what most courts in the United States say about polygraphs. They're generally not admissible as evidence, because they measure stress, not truth. A man whose wife has just been killed in their home, who knows he's under suspicion, is going to be stressed. That's not a sign of guilt. That's a sign of being a human being in an unbearable situation.

[SLOW]

Sandy Preer was never arrested. He was never charged with anything. [SRC: 3]

But in a neighbourhood like Chevy Chase, and in a case like this, you don't need to be charged to be convicted in people's minds. The family would later describe how Sandy lived the rest of his life under a cloud of suspicion. [SRC: 3]

Lauren was about twenty years old that spring. Old enough to understand everything that was happening, and young enough that her mother should have been there for decades more. For graduations, weddings, grandchildren, ordinary phone calls on ordinary Sunday afternoons.

Lauren never believed her father was involved. [SRC: 8] She had lost her mother. Now she watched people look at her father differently, whisper about him, wonder. She held on to what she knew, against what the neighbourhood suspected.

[NORMAL]

Months passed. The investigation ground on. Interviews. Re-interviews. Leads that went nowhere.

And then, in January of two thousand and two, about nine months after Leslie died, a tip came in. [SRC: 5]

It came from a neighbour. The tip suggested that police should look at someone from the Preer family's past. Not Sandy. Someone younger. Someone who, the tipster thought, might have been involved. [SRC: 5]

The tip was written down. It was placed in the case file.

And there, for reasons that have never been fully explained in public, it stayed.

It helps to understand what a tip is in a big homicide investigation. In the months after a killing that makes the news, police can receive dozens or hundreds of them. Some are sincere and useless. Some are malicious, neighbours settling scores. Some are from people who just want to feel involved. Each one gets logged. Each one gets a decision: follow up now, follow up later, or file. And the decision is made through the lens of whatever the investigation already believes.

In January two thousand and two, this investigation believed in one man. And this tip was about someone else.

We'll come back to what that tip said, and whose name was on it. For now, just hold this: by January of two thousand and two, the answer to this case was physically inside the Montgomery County Police Department's file.

[SLOW]

Then, at some point in the years that followed, the science caught up enough to test the DNA from under Leslie's fingernails against Sandy Preer.

It wasn't his. [SRC: 3]

The male DNA from the struggle did not match Leslie's husband. The one piece of evidence that came straight from the attacker said, as clearly as science can, that the man who fought with Leslie Preer that morning was not Sandy.

You might think that would end the suspicion. In the official sense, it did. But suspicion in a community isn't official. It doesn't read lab reports. And without another name, without a real answer, the cloud over Sandy Preer never fully lifted.

The DNA profile was entered into the national criminal database. No match. Whoever had been in the house on Drummond Avenue had never been arrested and swabbed for anything that would put him in the system.

The case went cold.

[NORMAL]

Years became a decade. Lauren grew into adulthood carrying her mother's absence and her father's shadow. New families moved into Chevy Chase who had never heard Leslie Preer's name.

And in twenty seventeen, sixteen years after his wife was killed, Sandy Preer died. [SRC: 3]

He died still under that cloud. Lauren would later say she believed it was grief that, in the end, took him. [SRC: 8]

He was never cleared in public. Not because the evidence pointed at him. It didn't. But because nobody could yet say who it pointed at instead.

That would take five more years, two new detectives, and a laboratory in Texas.

## 7. THE MIDPOINT TURN (22:30–26:00)
[SLOW]

In twenty twenty-two, the Montgomery County Police Department's cold case unit picked up Leslie Preer's file. Two detectives took it on: Tara Augustin and Alyson Dupouy. [SRC: 6]

They read it from the beginning. Every interview, every report, every tip. Hundreds of pages from two thousand and one and two thousand and two, written by detectives who had since retired. When you inherit a cold case, that first read is everything. You're trying to see the case the way nobody has seen it for twenty years: whole.

In September of twenty twenty-two, they went to court. Maryland is one of only a few states with a law that specifically regulates police use of genetic genealogy, and it requires a judge's authorization before investigators can search those databases. [SRC: 15] The judge signed off. And they sent the DNA from under Leslie's fingernails to Othram, a forensic genetics lab in The Woodlands, Texas. [SRC: 5]

Othram specializes in exactly this: taking old, degraded, small DNA samples, and building them into the kind of rich genetic profile that can be compared with public genealogy databases. The lab produced an enhanced profile of the man who had fought with Leslie. [SRC: 6]

The difference between this and the old police barcode is scale. The police profile looked at a couple of dozen locations on the DNA. A genealogy profile looks at hundreds of thousands. That's what makes it possible to find not just an identical match, but a distant one. A third cousin. A fourth cousin. Someone who shares only a sliver of DNA with the killer, but enough to say: you are related to him, somewhere back in your family tree.

The catch is that a sample twenty-one years old, taken from under a victim's fingernails, is tiny and damaged. Getting hundreds of thousands of readable data points out of it is technically hard. That's the problem Othram exists to solve.

That profile was uploaded to FamilyTreeDNA, one of the genealogy databases that allows law enforcement searches. [SRC: 15] And it came back with a list of people who shared some of the killer's DNA.

Then the family-tree work began.

[NORMAL]

Genetic genealogy doesn't hand you a name. It hands you relatives. Distant cousins, usually, people who share a small slice of DNA with the person you're looking for. Then you build their family trees, backwards and forwards, until the branches cross at the one person who fits.

In this case, the closest matches led somewhere investigators may not have expected. Across the Atlantic. To a family line in Romania. [SRC: 6]

That's a hard place to build a tree. Records from Eastern Europe can be patchy, in another language, scattered across archives that survived wars and regime changes. Spellings shift. Villages change names. Month after month, the detectives worked the Romanian line, trying to find the branch that crossed the ocean and ended up in Maryland.

It took a long time. The DNA went to Othram in September twenty twenty-two. A year passed. Then most of another.

[SLOW]

By June of twenty twenty-four, Detective Tara Augustin was combing through publicly available records and articles connected to that Romanian family line. [SRC: 13] Names. Obituaries. Old newspaper mentions. Looking for any American branch.

And on one of those pages, she came across a surname.

Gligor.

[PAUSE]

It stopped her. Because she had seen that name before. Not in a genealogy database. Not on a Romanian archive page.

In the case file. [SRC: 13]

She and Detective Dupouy had read the old Preer file from cover to cover when they took the case. And one of the documents in it was a tip from January two thousand and two, nine months after the murder. A neighbour had told police they should look at Lauren Preer's former boyfriend. [SRC: 5, 13]

His name was Eugene Gligor.

The boy who had dated Lauren from the age of fifteen. The boy who had grown up in the same neighbourhood. [SRC: 14] The boy who had come on the family vacations. The boy Leslie had liked so much. The boy who knew the house, and knew the dog.

Think about that from Detective Augustin's side of the desk. A genealogical trail running from Maryland to Romania and back again, through records in two languages, across more than a century of family history. And at the end of it, a name that had been typed into her own department's file twenty-two years earlier.

For more than two decades, the man whose DNA was under Leslie's fingernails had been named in the police's own paperwork.

Which raised the question every detective on that case now had to answer: could they prove it?

## 8. COMPLICATIONS (26:00–37:00)
[NORMAL]

By June of twenty twenty-four, the cold case team had a name, and a tip from two thousand and two that matched it. What they didn't have was proof. A family tree is a powerful lead. It isn't evidence a jury can convict on.

[HALFWAY RESET ENDS]

So now the question that matters is this. How do you prove that a forty-four-year-old man, with no record and no reason to suspect anything, was the person in that house twenty-three years ago, without tipping him off?

First, the detectives needed to understand who Eugene Gligor was, then and now.

[NORMAL]

In two thousand and one, Gligor was about twenty years old. [SRC: 5] He and Lauren had been together from about fifteen, for roughly five years. [SRC: 6] By the time of the murder, they hadn't been in contact for some time. [SRC: 6]

People who knew him as a teenager described him as mischievous. And according to court documents, he had a history of substance abuse in his younger years. [SRC: 9]

But the man he became looked nothing like a suspect. In the twenty-three years after Leslie's death, Eugene Gligor stayed in the Washington area. He worked at a real estate firm. Later, he worked from home for a company that ran video surveillance systems. [SRC: 9] He had no criminal record at all. [SRC: 9] Court documents described him, by the time of his arrest, as "zen." [SRC: 9]

A calm man. A professional. Someone you'd pass on the street in D.C. without a second look.

It's tempting to read that calm as something sinister, a mask. This video won't do that. People change over twenty-three years. A man can be genuinely different at forty-four from who he was at twenty. That's exactly the argument his lawyers would later make. But it's also true that, whatever kind of man he had become, he had lived that entire life knowing what happened in the house on Drummond Avenue, and never said a word.

The man whose job was watching surveillance cameras was about to find out what it's like to be watched.

[SLOW]

There was also the question of who could know. In cases like this, investigators typically tell almost no one, sometimes not even the victim's family, because a single phone call or an overheard conversation can reach the suspect. Lauren Preer had grown up with this man. They had friends in common. Whatever the detectives now suspected, it had to stay inside a very small room.

The detectives needed his DNA. And they needed it in a way that would hold up in court, without asking for it, because asking would warn him.

This is a technique used in cold cases all over the country, and it's legally well established. When a person throws something away in a public place, they give up their expectation of privacy in it. A coffee cup. A cigarette butt. A napkin. Police can collect it, and test it.

So they watched him.

The opportunity came almost immediately. On June ninth, twenty twenty-four, Eugene Gligor passed through Washington Dulles International Airport. [SRC: 5] Augustin and Dupouy had enlisted help from customs officials at the airport. [SRC: 13] Between the officers on the ground and the airport's own cameras, he was being watched.

For two years, since the DNA went to Texas, the detectives had been doing the slow work. Building and rebuilding the tree. Ruling out branches. Checking dates of birth, addresses, marriages. Now, after twenty-one months of that, everything came down to a few minutes in an airport terminal.

They watched as he drank from a plastic water bottle.

They watched as he finished it.

They watched as he dropped it into a trash bin. [SRC: 6]

And as soon as he walked away, they retrieved it. [SRC: 5]

[NORMAL]

Pause on the strangeness of that scene. In one of the busiest airports in the country, among thousands of people and thousands of discarded bottles, a cold case that had outlived the victim's husband came down to one specific bottle in one specific bin. Twenty-three years of waiting, and the next step was a few seconds of footage of a man throwing away his trash.

The bottle went to the lab.

[SLOW]

While they waited, it's worth asking the question the whole case keeps circling. How did a name in a tip in January two thousand and two sit in a file for twenty years?

The public record doesn't give a full answer, and this video won't invent one. But it's possible to understand how it happens without assuming bad faith.

In two thousand and two, the investigation was focused on Sandy. When investigators have a strong theory, new information gets weighed against it. A tip about an ex-boyfriend who hadn't been around for a while might have seemed less urgent than the man already at the centre of the case.

And in two thousand and two, even if detectives had looked at Gligor, what could they have done? They could have interviewed him. They could have asked for a DNA sample. If he'd said no, they'd have needed probable cause for a warrant. If he'd said yes, though, the case might have been solved within weeks.

That's the painful part. The DNA under Leslie's fingernails existed in two thousand and one. The name existed in two thousand and two. Each was waiting for the other. Nobody put them in the same room.

[NORMAL]

What it cost is easy to count. Sandy Preer lived sixteen years under suspicion and died without being cleared in public. Lauren spent more than twenty years not knowing who killed her mother, and watching people wonder about her father. Leslie's wider family, the big military family she grew up in, spent two decades with a question instead of an answer.

And for those same twenty-two years, the man named in that tip went to work, paid his rent, and lived a quiet life a few miles away.

Within about a week of the bottle being collected, the lab called back. [SRC: 6]

## 9. THE BREAK (37:00–43:00)
[SLOW]

The DNA on the water bottle matched the DNA from under Leslie Preer's fingernails. [SRC: 6]

Eugene Gligor was the man Leslie had fought with on the morning of May second, two thousand and one.

On June eighteenth, twenty twenty-four, Montgomery County detectives arrested him outside his apartment in Washington, D.C. [SRC: 9] He was charged with first-degree murder. [SRC: 9]

For Lauren, the news arrived like a second blow, twenty-three years after the first. Police told her who they had arrested. That evening, speaking to a local television station, she tried to put it into words. "It's been a hell of a day," she said. "He was my ex-boyfriend." [SRC: 14]

And then they sat him down in that interview room. The one from the beginning of this video. The camera in the corner. Two detectives across the table.

The interrogation lasted twenty-four minutes. [SRC: 10] In twenty twenty-five, the Washington Post obtained the footage and published it. [SRC: 10] It's one of the more extraordinary interrogation recordings in recent cold case history, not because of a confession, but because there isn't one.

[NORMAL]

The first thing you notice is the tone. There's no table-slamming. No shouting. The detectives are calm, warm, almost conversational. That's deliberate. Modern interview training in many American police departments has moved away from confrontation toward rapport. The idea is simple. People who feel cornered dig in. People who feel understood sometimes talk. You give a suspect room to explain, and you let the evidence do the pressing.

And in this case, the evidence was overwhelming. The detectives didn't need a confession to make an arrest. They had already made it. What the interview could offer was something else: the chance for Gligor to explain. An accident. A fight that went wrong. Anything.

The detectives explain the case to him. The genealogy. The family tree. The match. And one of them tells him, in that gentle, almost maternal tone:

"Well, honey, your DNA was in the crime scene."

And Gligor says:

"I don't know. I don't know. I don't know." [SRC: 10]

He tells them: "I'm really confused, and I'm really at a loss. I don't have any recollection of being involved with any of this." [SRC: 10]

It's not a confession. But listen to it carefully, and it's not a denial either. He doesn't say "that's impossible." He doesn't say "I was somewhere else." He doesn't say "I haven't been in that house in years." He says he doesn't remember.

One of the detectives picks up on exactly that. They point out to him that a person who is truly innocent usually says something very simple. They say they didn't do it. [SRC: 10]

[SLOW]

And Gligor answers:

"Oh, I didn't do it. I definitely didn't do it." [SRC: 10]

Watch the order of events there. First, "I don't know." Then, "I don't remember." Then, only after a detective points out what an innocent person would say, the denial. "I definitely didn't do it."

For twenty-four minutes, he gives no ground. [SRC: 10] "I don't know," "I don't remember," over and over. He never explains how his DNA could have been under Leslie's fingernails. He never offers an innocent reason he might have been at the house that day.

There's a reason "I don't remember" is such a common answer in rooms like this one. It isn't a lie that can be disproven. Nobody can prove what's in someone else's memory. It keeps every door open: if the evidence is weak, he never admitted anything; if the evidence is strong, he never denied it either. Whether Gligor's lack of memory was genuine, as his lawyers would later argue, or a way of saying nothing, only he knows. But either way, those twenty-four minutes gave the Preer family nothing. No explanation. No reason. Not even a denial that sounded like one.

The detectives didn't need him to. They had the DNA. They had the tip from two thousand and two. They had the history: five years as Lauren's boyfriend, the vacations, the knowledge of the house.

The only question left was whether Eugene Gligor would make the Preer family sit through a trial.

## 10. RESOLUTION (43:00–49:00)
[NORMAL]

So: who did Boomer let into the house that spring morning, and why did it take twenty-three years to read a name that was already in the file?

The first half of that question now had an answer. The second half was about to be argued in a courtroom in Rockville.

When Lauren Preer learned who had been arrested, she spoke to reporters. She said she had dated him when they were both fifteen. [SRC: 4] The man accused of killing her mother wasn't a stranger. He was her first love.

And then she revealed something that made the whole story even harder to absorb.

About a year before the arrest, she had run into Eugene Gligor. By chance, at a restaurant in Washington. [SRC: 14] Two people who had dated as teenagers, crossing paths as adults. The kind of encounter that happens all the time in a city. A hello. Small talk.

"He didn't seem weird," she said afterward. And then: "How you can look somebody in the eye, knowing that he committed this crime, and act like nothing happened, is pretty unreal." [SRC: 14]

Think about that encounter from both sides of the table. On one side, a woman who had lost her mother, watched her father die under suspicion, and still had no answer. On the other, according to his own later guilty plea, the man who had the answer. Making small talk.

In court, she would later call him something else.

[NORMAL]

In May of twenty twenty-five, Eugene Gligor pleaded guilty. Not to first-degree murder, but to second-degree murder. [SRC: 11]

The difference matters in law. First-degree murder in Maryland generally requires premeditation: a decision to kill, made in advance. Second-degree murder is an intentional killing without that advance planning.

Montgomery County State's Attorney John McCarthy was candid about what the prosecution did not have. No motive had been established, he said. It would be up to the defendant himself to explain why he did it. And there was no evidence the killing had been premeditated. [SRC: 12]

So twenty-three years later, the State of Maryland knew who killed Leslie Preer. It did not know why.

A guilty plea has a cost and a benefit, for everyone involved. For the defendant, second-degree murder carries a maximum of thirty years in Maryland, instead of the possibility of life. For the prosecution, it means a guaranteed conviction, without the risk of a jury, and without a defence attorney attacking the genealogy, the chain of custody of a water bottle, or the old evidence.

And for the family, it means something that's easy to overlook. No trial. No weeks of testimony. No defence lawyer suggesting, once again, that maybe the husband did it. After twenty-four years, Lauren would not have to sit in a courtroom and hear her father accused one more time.

[SLOW]

The sentencing hearing came on August twenty-eighth, twenty twenty-five, in the Montgomery County Circuit Court in Rockville. [SRC: 3]

The courtroom was full. More than sixty people came: family, friends, people who had loved Leslie. [SRC: 3]

Seven members of Leslie's family stood up to speak about what her death had done to them. [SRC: 4]

Victim impact statements are one of the few moments in an American courtroom where the person who died gets to be more than evidence. For a few minutes, the room hears about birthdays and phone calls and holidays. About a laugh, a habit, the way someone made coffee. About who wasn't there at a wedding, and who never met a grandchild. Seven people, each carrying twenty-four years of it, said it out loud with Eugene Gligor in the room.

Lauren was one of them. Through tears, she faced the man she had once loved and called him "a wolf in sheep's clothing." [SRC: 4]

Later, when prosecutors showed photographs from the scene, she cried out in the courtroom. [SRC: 4] Twenty-four years had not made any of it easier to see.

[NORMAL]

Then it was the defence's turn, and in fairness, their argument deserves to be heard.

Gligor's lawyers said the killing followed a night of heavy drinking and cocaine use. [SRC: 12] They said there was no motive, because there was no reason. They called it a tragic aberration in an otherwise law-abiding life. Twenty-three years with no record, a steady job, a quiet existence. The man in the courtroom, they argued, was not the twenty-year-old who had been in that house.

When Gligor was given the chance to speak, he told the court he did not remember the killing, because he had blacked out. But he said he was sorry. [SRC: 12]

[SLOW]

Judge David Lease handed down the maximum sentence of thirty years, with all but twenty-two years suspended. [SRC: 3] That means Eugene Gligor will serve twenty-two years in prison, followed by five years of supervised probation. The judge also ordered him to undergo mental health evaluation and treatment, and to have no contact with the Preer family. [SRC: 3]

As the sentence was read, the courtroom erupted. Cheers. Shouts. [SRC: 3] Twenty-four years of waiting, released in a single moment.

Afterward, State's Attorney McCarthy spoke to reporters. He called it a "history-making case." [SRC: 15] It was one of the first times, in Maryland and in the United States, that this kind of genetic genealogy had been used not just to generate a lead, but as part of the evidence used to prove a case. [SRC: 15]

That's worth understanding. For years, genetic genealogy has been treated as a way to point detectives in the right direction. The proof was supposed to come afterward, from a traditional DNA match. In the Preer case, the genealogical work itself, the family tree built through Romania, was part of the case the State was ready to put in front of a court. Other prosecutors around the country will be studying it.

## 11. AFTERMATH + MEANING (49:00–53:00)
[SLOW]

There's one person who wasn't in that courtroom, and should be remembered here.

Sandy Preer. The husband who was investigated first, who gave police every detail of his morning, who reportedly failed a test that measures stress and not truth. The man who, for sixteen years, lived as the person everyone quietly wondered about.

The DNA had never been his. The man it belonged to was named in a tip in January two thousand and two. And Sandy died in twenty seventeen, five years before anyone opened the file and read the name properly. He never got to hear a courtroom say, in effect, that it wasn't him.

If there's a second victim in this case, it's him.

[NORMAL]

And Leslie.

Leslie Preer was a military-family kid who learned to make every new place home, and then spent her free time helping other newcomers do the same. A journalism student who cared about people's stories. A mother who welcomed her daughter's boyfriend into the family, and took him on vacation with them.

On her last morning, in her own home, she fought back. She took a piece of the man who attacked her and held on to it. That evidence sat in a lab for twenty-one years, waiting for someone to pair it with a name the police already had.

In a very real sense, Leslie Preer solved her own murder. She just had to wait for everyone else to catch up.

And Lauren. It's difficult to imagine a harder set of facts to carry. Her mother was killed by her first love. Her father spent the rest of his life suspected of it. She ran into the man responsible at a restaurant and made small talk, not knowing. And when the answer finally came, it came with the knowledge that the name had been in a police file since she was a young woman.

She has spoken publicly about her mother many times since the arrest, to local stations, to national programmes. And what comes through, every time, isn't the case. It's Leslie. The mother who took her daughter's boyfriend on vacation because that's the kind of person she was.

And Boomer, the dog who was so frightened that morning, but unhurt. We'll never know exactly what he saw. But the most likely explanation for why a family dog didn't treat the person in the house as a stranger is the simplest one. He wasn't a stranger.

[NORMAL]

So, the question we started with. Who did Boomer let into the house that morning, and why did it take twenty-three years?

He let in someone he knew. A young man who'd been part of the family for five years, and who Leslie herself had liked.

And it took twenty-three years not because the case was unsolvable, but because it was unread. The physical evidence was there from the first day. The name was there from the ninth month. What was missing was someone looking at both at once.

There's a lesson in that for every police department with a shelf of cold cases. The tools change. New science arrives every few years. But the most valuable thing in an old file is often already in it: a name, a tip, a statement nobody had time to chase. Genetic genealogy didn't create the answer in the Preer case. It told two detectives which page of their own file to turn back to.

What's striking about this case is that the breakthrough didn't come from a new witness, or a confession, or luck. It came from two detectives doing the most basic thing in police work, reading the whole file from the beginning, with a tool that finally told them which page mattered.
