## 1. COLD OPEN (0:00–1:00)

In Japan, a prisoner on death row is not told the date of his execution in advance. He finds out on the morning itself. [SRC: 3] A few hours' notice, sometimes less. Footsteps in the corridor. A door opening that isn't usually opened. Guards who stop outside your cell instead of walking past it.

So every morning, for decades, the men on Japan's death row have listened.

Iwao Hakamada listened for forty-six years.

Every morning, the footsteps might stop at his door. Every morning, for forty-six years, they walked past.

On September twenty-sixth, twenty twenty-four, a court in Shizuoka finally ruled on the case that had put him there. The man it was ruling on was eighty-eight years old. He wasn't even in the courtroom. By then, his mind could no longer fully follow what was happening to him.

And the court didn't just say he was innocent. It said who had made him look guilty.

## 2. THE PROMISE (1:00–1:30)

The evidence that sent Iwao Hakamada to death row didn't exist when he was arrested. It appeared fourteen months after the murders, in a vat of fermenting soybean paste, in a factory the police had already searched. This is the story of how that evidence got there, and of the sister who spent more than half a century waiting for someone to admit it.

## 3. THE WORLD BEFORE (1:30–5:00)
[NORMAL]

[LETTER]
*"Of course I don't have any connection to this case. Mom, please be confident and come visit me here in jail with your head held high."*
— Iwao Hakamada, letter to his mother, 1967 [SRC: 1]
[/LETTER]

Iwao Hakamada was born in nineteen thirty-six in Shizuoka Prefecture, on Japan's Pacific coast, with Mount Fuji looming over the landscape to the north. [SRC: 10]

He was the youngest of six children. And the sibling he was closest to was his older sister, Hideko, three years older. [SRC: 5] Remember her. She is the one constant in everything that follows.

As a young man, Iwao found something he was good at. He boxed.

Between nineteen fifty-nine and nineteen sixty-one, he fought twenty-nine professional bouts as a featherweight. He was ranked as high as sixth in Japan in his weight class. His record was sixteen wins, eleven losses, and two draws. [SRC: 10] Not a champion. A solid professional. A man who got into the ring and took his punches, and walked out on his own feet. Every one of his losses came on points. He was never knocked out. [SRC: 10]

That's worth holding on to. Iwao Hakamada was a man who did not go down easily.

Boxing in Japan in the late nineteen-fifties was a hard, working-class sport. Fighters trained in small gyms, fought often for little money, and took real damage. Twenty-nine professional fights in about three years is a heavy schedule. It speaks to discipline, and to a man who needed the purse money. When his career ended, he didn't have a degree or connections to fall back on. He had his hands, and a willingness to work.

After his boxing career ended, he needed work. He found it at a company that made miso, the fermented soybean paste that is at the heart of Japanese cooking. The Kogane Miso company, in the port city of Shimizu. [SRC: 1]

Miso is made in large tanks. Soybeans, salt, and a fermenting culture, packed in and left to mature, sometimes for many months, until the paste turns a deep, rich brown. It's slow, quiet work. Stirring, packing, carrying, cleaning.

Iwao lived in the company dormitory right beside the factory. [SRC: 1] Just across the railway tracks was the home of one of the company's bosses, the managing director, Fujio Hashimoto, who lived there with his wife and their two teenage children. [SRC: 1]

A former boxer, now a factory hand, living in a dormitory a short walk from his employer's family home. A quiet, ordinary life.

It's worth picturing Japan in nineteen sixty-six. Two years earlier, Tokyo had hosted the Olympics, and the bullet train had begun running along this very coast, between Tokyo and Osaka. The country was in the middle of an extraordinary economic boom. Factories were busy. Small cities like Shimizu were growing. Millions of ordinary working people like Iwao Hakamada were building new lives in company dormitories, working long hours, sending money home.

It was also a country whose criminal justice system had been rebuilt after the war, but which still relied, heavily, on one thing above all others to solve its crimes. We'll come to that.

On the night of June thirtieth, nineteen sixty-six, that house across the tracks caught fire.

And the next fifty-eight years of Iwao Hakamada's life began.

## 5. THE DAY (5:30–13:30)
[SLOW]

In the early hours of June thirtieth, nineteen sixty-six, the Hashimoto home in Shimizu was in flames.

People came running, as people do. According to Iwao Hakamada, he was one of them. He said he helped fight the fire. [SRC: 1]

When it was put out, the people inside the house were found.

Fujio Hashimoto, the managing director. His wife, who was thirty-nine. Their seventeen-year-old daughter. Their fourteen-year-old son. [SRC: 1]

All four had been stabbed to death. The house had been set alight afterward. [SRC: 1]

That's all this video will say about how they died. Four members of one family, including two children, killed in their own home. They deserve to be remembered for more than what was done to them, and their murders have never been solved. Whoever killed them has never been identified or held to account. That's a second injustice inside this story, and it should never be forgotten while we talk about the first.

[NORMAL]

Now think about the investigation from the police's point of view in the summer of nineteen sixty-six.

A wealthy family, wiped out. A fire meant to cover it. Enormous public pressure. In a small city, a crime like that is the only story anyone talks about. The police needed an answer, and they needed it fast.

And very quickly, their attention turned to the factory across the tracks. To the men who worked there and lived in the dormitory. Men who knew the family, knew the house, knew the routines.

And to one of them in particular.

Iwao Hakamada was an outsider in some ways. A former professional boxer, which in the Japan of the nineteen-sixties carried a certain reputation, rightly or wrongly, as a rough world. He lived alone in the dormitory. [SRC: 1] To investigators looking for a man capable of violence, a former fighter living a few hundred metres from the scene fit a picture.

It was a picture. Not evidence.

[SLOW]

There's something important to understand about criminal justice in Japan, both then and, to a large degree, now. It helps explain everything that happened next.

Japan's conviction rate is extraordinarily high. Of cases that go to trial, more than ninety-nine percent end in a conviction. [SRC: 1] Supporters of the system say that's because prosecutors are careful, and only bring cases they're sure of. Critics say something else. They say it's because the system relies heavily on one kind of evidence above all others.

The confession.

In Japan, suspects can be held for long periods before they're formally charged. They can be questioned for many hours a day. For most of the twentieth century, they were questioned without a lawyer present, and without the interrogation being recorded. Critics, inside Japan and outside, have a name for this system. They call it "hostage justice": you are held until you say what the interrogators want to hear. [SRC: 1]

That is the system that was waiting for Iwao Hakamada.

On August eighteenth, nineteen sixty-six, forty-nine days after the fire, police arrested him. [SRC: 1]

## 6. FIRST RESPONSE (13:30–22:30)
[SLOW]

What happened in the interrogation room over the next three weeks has been described by his lawyers, by human rights organisations, and, eventually, by a Japanese court.

According to his lawyers, Iwao Hakamada was interrogated for a total of around two hundred and sixty-four hours, across twenty-three days. Some sessions lasted as long as sixteen hours. [SRC: 1] He has said he was beaten. [SRC: 9]

The number twenty-three isn't random. Under Japanese law, police can hold a suspect for up to twenty-three days before deciding whether to charge them: an initial seventy-two hours, then two ten-day extensions approved by a judge. Throughout that time, the suspect can be questioned. And if the investigators then arrest the suspect on a different charge, the clock can start again.

Two hundred and sixty-four hours. That's eleven full days of questioning, compressed into three weeks. Day after day after day, in a small room, with investigators who had already decided who was responsible.

For nineteen days, Iwao Hakamada denied it.

On the twentieth day, he signed a confession. [SRC: 1]

[NORMAL]

It's worth pausing here, because people who have never been interrogated often find false confessions hard to believe. "I would never confess to something I didn't do." Most of us believe that about ourselves.

But research on false confessions, across many countries, shows a clear pattern. Long interrogations. Sleep deprivation. Isolation. Repeated insistence that the evidence is overwhelming. A suspect told that confessing is the only way to make it stop. Under those conditions, ordinary people confess to things they didn't do. Not because they've been persuaded they're guilty, but because they're exhausted, frightened, and desperate for the questioning to end. Many believe they'll be able to explain the truth later, in court, to a judge who will see through it.

Iwao Hakamada tried exactly that.

[SLOW]

At his trial, he took back the confession. He said it had been forced out of him. He said he was innocent. [SRC: 3]

And from his cell, he wrote to his mother. The letter that opened this story. "Of course I don't have any connection to this case." And another line, written while he waited for the verdict: "I am innocent, and now I am calmly waiting for my trial to conclude. I am in a warm room and I do not have any complaints." [SRC: 1]

Calm. Confident. A man who believed the truth would protect him.

[NORMAL]

But even inside the prosecution, there was a problem. A retracted confession is weaker than a physical piece of evidence. The trial went on through nineteen sixty-six and into nineteen sixty-seven, and the prosecution's case needed something solid. Something that put the murders in Hakamada's hands, not just in his signed statement.

There was clothing to consider. According to accounts of the case, the prosecution's original story was that Hakamada had committed the murders wearing his pyjamas. [SRC: 1] The pyjamas were part of the story the confession told.

And then, in the summer of nineteen sixty-seven, more than a year after the fire, with the trial already under way, investigators announced a new discovery.

Something had been found at the miso factory.

## 7. THE MIDPOINT TURN (22:30–26:00)
[SLOW]

In August nineteen sixty-seven, about fourteen months after the murders, five items of clothing were found at the bottom of a tank of miso at the factory where Iwao Hakamada had worked. [SRC: 3]

A pair of trousers. A shirt. Other garments. All stained with what was said to be blood.

The prosecution now had a new theory. It wasn't the pyjamas after all. The killer, they said, had worn these clothes during the murders, then hidden them in the miso tank. And they belonged, the prosecution said, to Iwao Hakamada. [SRC: 1]

Think about the logic, too. If the killer had worn these clothes and wanted to get rid of them, why push them into a tank of miso at his own workplace, where colleagues would eventually dig through it? And if Hakamada had done that, why hadn't the prosecution mentioned these clothes for more than a year, while building a case around his pyjamas?

Think about the timing. The factory had been searched by police after the murders. The tank was a place the workers used. Yet these clothes were only "found" more than a year later, after Hakamada had already retracted his confession, while the trial was going on.

[NORMAL]

And then there were the trousers.

When the trousers were tried on Iwao Hakamada, they didn't fit. They were too small. He couldn't get them on. [SRC: 10]

Most people would think that settled it. The prosecution didn't. They argued that the trousers must have shrunk. Soaked in miso for a year, then dried. [SRC: 10]

They also pointed to a tag on the trousers marked with the letter "B". That, they said, indicated a size that would have fitted Hakamada. [SRC: 10]

It didn't. According to later accounts of the case, the "B" on the tag referred to the colour of the fabric. Black. Not the size at all. [SRC: 10]

[SLOW]

There was something else about the trial that, looking back, is almost impossible to believe.

The prosecution had presented forty-five documents as signed confessions by Iwao Hakamada. The judges examined them. And they threw out forty-four of the forty-five, finding they had not been given freely. [SRC: 17] Forty-four out of forty-five. The court itself had recognised that the interrogation process had produced statements it couldn't trust.

It accepted one.

None of this stopped the case. In nineteen sixty-eight, the Shizuoka District Court found Iwao Hakamada guilty of murdering the Hashimoto family. Three judges heard the case.

They sentenced him to death. [SRC: 3]

And one of those three judges, the most junior on the panel, a young man named Norimichi Kumamoto, went home that day carrying something he wouldn't speak about publicly for nearly forty years.

He didn't believe Hakamada had done it. [SRC: 10]

As he would later explain, he felt there was almost nothing in the case except the confession, and that the confession had been taken under intense interrogation. He put that view to the other two judges. They disagreed. [SRC: 17] In a three-judge panel, the majority decides. And Japanese judges, then, did not publish dissents in cases like this. He was bound to silence.

So the junior judge was required to help write a judgment he didn't believe in. A death sentence for a man he thought was innocent.

## 8. COMPLICATIONS (26:00–37:00)
[SLOW]

[LETTER]
*"As I feel fear spreading across my body like a black wave, I have fallen into a condition of despair… Oh God, what a difficult thing it is to live. There is no sin in me."*
— Iwao Hakamada, 1974 [SRC: 1]
[/LETTER]

By nineteen sixty-eight, Iwao Hakamada was a condemned man, sentenced on a confession he had retracted and clothes that didn't fit him. What he didn't know was that he would spend the rest of the twentieth century, and a good part of the twenty-first, waiting to find out whether anyone would ever say so.

[HALFWAY RESET ENDS]

So the question now isn't just whether he was innocent. It's how a man survives knowing he might be killed, on any given morning, for something he didn't do, and how long a system can go on refusing to look.

[NORMAL]

His appeals went up through the courts. They failed. In nineteen eighty, Japan's Supreme Court upheld the death sentence. It was now final. [SRC: 3]

Under Japanese law, the only way left was a request for a retrial: an extraordinary procedure, rarely granted, which requires clear new evidence. And so began the longest part of this story. Petitions filed. Petitions refused. Years spent waiting for courts to decide, then appealing their decisions, then waiting again.

It's important to know that Japan had been here before. In nineteen eighty-three, a man named Sakae Menda became the first person in Japan's history to be released from death row through a retrial. [SRC: 12] He had been convicted of a double murder in the nineteen-forties and sentenced to death. In the retrial, the court confirmed his alibi, found that he had falsely confessed, and found that the prosecution had failed to disclose evidence that would have helped him. He walked free after more than thirty-four years. [SRC: 12]

Within a few years, three more men were freed from Japan's death row in the same way. Four death-row exonerations in a single decade. [SRC: 12] Several of them involved the same ingredients: long interrogations, confessions later retracted, and evidence that didn't hold up.

In other words, by the mid-nineteen-eighties, Japan's justice system had been shown, four times, that it could sentence an innocent man to death. Iwao Hakamada's case was sitting in the same system, with the same warning signs. A retracted confession after hundreds of hours of questioning. Physical evidence that appeared late and didn't fit. And still, for another thirty years, the system did not look again.

Meanwhile, Iwao Hakamada lived on death row at the Tokyo Detention House.

[SLOW]

Death row in Japan is a particular kind of place. Prisoners are often held in single cells, with very limited contact with other inmates. Much of Hakamada's time was spent in what amounted to solitary confinement. [SRC: 5] And every morning came that moment, the one this story opened with. The footsteps. The question of whether today was the day.

That's the thing that's hardest to imagine. Not a single terrible event, but the same fear, repeated, every morning, for years. Then decades.

Japan carries out executions by hanging. The process is kept highly secret. Prisoners learn their time has come only hours before. Their families are typically told after it's over. [SRC: 3] Human rights organisations have criticised this system for years, precisely because of what it does to people like Hakamada: it turns every single morning of a sentence into a possible last morning.

Over the decades he spent on death row, Hakamada would have known that other men on the same corridors were taken. Some of them he may have known. Each time, the footsteps stopped at someone else's door. Each time, the message was clear: it could be you next.

His letters trace what it did to him. In nineteen sixty-seven, he had written to his mother with calm confidence. By nineteen seventy-four, the tone had changed completely. "As I feel fear spreading across my body like a black wave," he wrote, "I have fallen into a condition of despair, with a feeling of rage that wants to curse everything about this transitory world." [SRC: 1] And: "I don't know how many more years this awfulness of authority will continue. There is no sin in me." [SRC: 1]

There is no sin in me. He wrote that while waiting to be hanged.

Read the two letters side by side, seven years apart, and you can watch something being lost. In nineteen sixty-seven, he's a man reassuring his mother. He's in a warm room, he has no complaints, the truth will come out, hold your head high. By nineteen seventy-four, he's a man talking to God, asking whether his life will be nothing but suffering until the very end.

Nothing about his case had changed in those seven years, except that the courts kept saying no. The evidence was the same. The clothes were the same. The confession was the same. What changed was him.

His letters were later collected and published, in nineteen ninety-two. [SRC: 1] And then, gradually, the letters stopped making sense.

[NORMAL]

Over the long years in confinement, Iwao Hakamada's mind began to come apart. He developed what his supporters and family describe as serious mental illness. He began to live, as his sister would put it, in his own world. [SRC: 5]

He began refusing visits. Even from Hideko.

His sister had been coming to the Tokyo Detention House for years. Every month, she made the journey to see her brother. And now, more and more often, he wouldn't come out. When he did talk, the words didn't connect to reality. In an interview outside the prison, Hideko said she hadn't seen him for almost three years. And she said something very simple, and very true.

"If you put someone in jail for forty-seven years, it's too much to expect them to stay sane." [SRC: 11]

[SLOW]

Hideko never stopped. She wasn't a lawyer. She wasn't an activist by training. But she became the face of her brother's case. She spoke to reporters, to supporters, to human rights groups, to anyone who would listen. For decades, an ordinary woman in Shizuoka carried the single demand that her brother's case be heard again.

She wasn't entirely alone. Another group took up her brother's cause, one you might not expect: Japanese professional boxers.

The Japan Pro Boxing Association formed a committee to support Iwao Hakamada. [SRC: 15] Boxers, trainers, gym owners and former champions spent around two decades campaigning for a man most of them had never seen fight. They considered him one of their own. [SRC: 15] The committee's head, Shosei Nitta, who ran a boxing gym in Kawasaki, said it was simply natural to support a fellow boxer in trouble. [SRC: 15]

And the boxers made a pointed argument. They said prejudice against boxers had played a part in the case from the beginning. That in nineteen sixty-six, the press had reported Hakamada's guilt as fact before any trial, and that the idea of a former fighter as a natural killer had shaped how investigators, reporters and the public saw him. [SRC: 15] Innocent until proven guilty, they said, had been abandoned the moment he was arrested.

Over the years, they rallied outside courts. They held events. Former champions stood in front of cameras and said: this man is innocent. In a country where challenging the courts publicly was rare, a group of people who make their living taking punches kept stepping into the ring for him.

And then, in March two thousand and seven, someone joined Hideko from the most unexpected place imaginable.

[NORMAL]

Norimichi Kumamoto, the junior judge from nineteen sixty-eight, by then long retired, went public. He said he had believed Hakamada was innocent at the time of the trial. [SRC: 10] He said he had been outvoted by the two other judges, and that the rules of the court had kept him silent about it. And he joined the call for a retrial. [SRC: 10]

"Since I had convicted him," he said, "I felt extremely guilty that I had to sentence an innocent man. I still do, to this day." [SRC: 10]

A judge from the original panel, saying publicly that the man he helped sentence to death was innocent. In almost any country, that would be a bombshell. And it was. But even then, the system didn't move quickly.

What finally moved it was the one kind of evidence nobody could argue with.

## 9. THE BREAK (37:00–43:00)
[SLOW]

By the two thousand and tens, DNA testing had advanced to the point where the blood on the miso-tank clothes, more than forty years old, could be examined in a way nobody in nineteen sixty-seven could have imagined.

The tests were done. And the results did not connect Iwao Hakamada to that blood. [SRC: 3]

On March twenty-seventh, twenty fourteen, the Shizuoka District Court granted Hakamada a retrial. [SRC: 3] And it did something else, something almost unheard of in a case like this. It ordered him released. Immediately.

The presiding judge, Hiroaki Murayama, explained why. The possibility of his innocence, the judge said, had become clear "to a respectable degree." And then: "It is unbearably unjust to prolong detention of the defendant any further." [SRC: 4]

The court said there was reason to believe evidence against him may have been fabricated. [SRC: 3]

[NORMAL]

Iwao Hakamada walked out of the Tokyo Detention House at the age of seventy-eight. He had been in custody for forty-eight years. More than forty-five of those years under a sentence of death. [SRC: 3]

Hideko was there to take him home.

Try to picture that moment from his side. He went into custody in nineteen sixty-six, at thirty. The world he walked out into in twenty fourteen had mobile phones, the internet, a different currency of prices, buildings that hadn't existed, streets he wouldn't recognise. His parents were long gone. He was an old man now, and the sister waiting for him at the gate was over eighty.

For Hideko, it was the day she had been working toward for forty-eight years. For Iwao, in his state of mind, it's not clear he fully understood what had happened.

He went to live with her in Hamamatsu. And a few weeks later, in April twenty fourteen, the World Boxing Council presented him with an honorary championship belt. [SRC: 10] The former featherweight who had never been knocked out finally got a title.

But freedom, for a man who had spent nearly half a century in a tiny cell, didn't look like freedom looks to the rest of us.

For the first two months after his release, he didn't try to go outside at all. He paced inside the apartment, back and forth, as if the walls of his old cell were still around him. [SRC: 13]

Then he began to walk. Every day, in any weather, he walked around the streets of Hamamatsu, sometimes for as long as six hours. A volunteer would follow a few steps behind, to make sure he didn't get hurt and could find his way home. [SRC: 13] His supporters said he believed he was out "patrolling," a guardian keeping watch over the neighbourhood. [SRC: 13]

Doctors described his condition as a form of prison psychosis, the result of decades of confinement under a death sentence. [SRC: 13] He followed the same routine, day after day. The same vending machine. The same drink. The same place to sit. The same restaurant at the end of the day. [SRC: 13] If something interrupted his pattern, he sometimes attributed it to germs, or to unseen forces. [SRC: 13]

Hideko took care of him through all of it. A woman in her eighties, looking after a brother in his late seventies, in a small apartment, while the legal fight over whether he would be hanged carried on in courtrooms he couldn't follow.

[SLOW]

But the fight wasn't over. The prosecution appealed.

In June twenty eighteen, the Tokyo High Court overturned the decision to grant a retrial. [SRC: 3] It didn't send him back to prison, given his age and health, but legally, he was once again a condemned man, technically on death row, living in his sister's house.

The High Court's reasoning centred on the DNA. Prosecutors had attacked the methods used in the testing, and the court agreed that the results were not credible enough. They fell short, it said, of being indisputable evidence. [SRC: 14] On the colour of the bloodstains, the prosecution said its own experiments showed the reddish colour was credible. [SRC: 14]

Think about what that meant for the man walking the streets of Hamamatsu every day. He was free to walk. But under the law, he was still sentenced to death. At any point, the legal machinery could, in theory, turn back the other way. He was over eighty years old, and he was living on borrowed time in more than one sense.

His lawyers appealed to the Supreme Court. In twenty twenty, the Supreme Court sent the case back to the Tokyo High Court and told it to look again. [SRC: 3]

And this time, the debate centred on one very specific scientific question. A question about colour.

[NORMAL]

When the clothes were found in the miso tank in nineteen sixty-seven, the bloodstains on them were described as reddish. [SRC: 3]

But what happens to blood when it's soaked in miso for more than a year?

Miso is salty, acidic, and dark. Experiments were carried out, soaking bloodstained fabric in miso over long periods. The findings put forward by the defence were clear. Over time, blood soaked in miso turns dark. Black, or close to it. It doesn't stay red. [SRC: 3]

This is the kind of evidence that makes a case. Not an expert's interpretation, not a witness's memory, but chemistry. The reaction between blood and the salt and acidity of fermenting soybeans happens the same way every time. You can test it in a lab. You can photograph the results week by week. You can show a judge exactly how the colour changes.

So if the clothes had really been in that tank since June nineteen sixty-six, the stains should have been almost black by the time they were found. The fact that they were still reddish suggested something else. That the clothes hadn't been in the miso for anywhere near that long.

That they'd been put there much more recently. Shortly before they were "found."

On March thirteenth, twenty twenty-three, the Tokyo High Court granted the retrial. [SRC: 3] Prosecutors decided not to appeal that decision. After fifty-seven years, there would be a new trial.

When she heard the news, Hideko, then ninety, said: "I was waiting for this day for fifty-seven years, and it has come." [SRC: 5]

## 10. RESOLUTION (43:00–49:00)
[NORMAL]

So how does a country that convicts more than ninety-nine percent of the people it puts on trial finally admit it got something this wrong?

Not easily. Even now.

The retrial began in the Shizuoka District Court in late twenty twenty-three, and ran through a series of hearings into twenty twenty-four. And here's the part of the story that surprises almost everyone who hears it.

At the final hearing, in May twenty twenty-four, after the DNA, after the miso experiments, after a former judge had said publicly that the man was innocent, after forty-six years on death row and a decade of living with prison psychosis in his sister's home, the prosecutors stood up and made their closing demand.

They asked for the death penalty. Again. [SRC: 16]

For an eighty-eight-year-old man living with the effects of decades of psychological damage, who could no longer follow the proceedings. Human rights groups criticised the move sharply, accusing prosecutors of trying to drag the case out even further. [SRC: 16]

[PAUSE]

The verdict was set for September.

It came on September twenty-sixth, twenty twenty-four, in the Shizuoka District Court. The same court that had sentenced Iwao Hakamada to death fifty-six years earlier.

He wasn't there. Because of his mental state, he had been excused from attending. His sister, Hideko, by then ninety-one, sat in his place. [SRC: 3]

[SLOW]

The presiding judge was Koshi Kunii. And the court's ruling went further than almost anyone had dared to hope.

It found Iwao Hakamada not guilty.

It found that the five items of clothing from the miso tank had been fabricated by investigators: processed and placed in the tank long after the crime. [SRC: 2] On the central scientific question, the judge said: "The court cannot accept the fact that the blood stain would remain reddish if it had been soaked in miso for more than a year." [SRC: 3]

And on the confession, the court found that the interrogation records had been obtained through inhumane questioning, and could not be relied on. [SRC: 3]

Fabricated evidence. An inhumane interrogation. Words a Japanese court had almost never used about its own police and prosecutors.

It's hard to overstate how unusual that is. Courts in many countries overturn convictions by saying the evidence was insufficient, or unreliable, or that new information has emerged. That leaves room for everyone to save face. This court went much further. It didn't just say the evidence against Hakamada was weak. It said the evidence had been manufactured, and it said by whom: the investigating authorities.

When the verdict was read, Hideko stood and bowed deeply to the court. [SRC: 3]

[NORMAL]

Then the prosecutors had a decision to make. They had fourteen days to appeal.

In October twenty twenty-four, Japan's Prosecutor-General, Naomi Unemoto, announced they would not. [SRC: 8] But the statement she issued was not a simple apology. She expressed "strong dissatisfaction" with the ruling, especially its finding that investigators had fabricated evidence. [SRC: 8] She said, however, that they would accept it, and she apologised for the decades of legal uncertainty Hakamada had endured. [SRC: 8]

Read that carefully. The prosecution service didn't accept that its people had planted evidence. It accepted the result.

And there's a question the ruling leaves hanging. If the clothes were fabricated by investigators, who exactly did it? The court's finding was about the authorities as a whole. No individual officer or prosecutor has been publicly named as responsible, and no one has been charged with planting the evidence. [SRC: 2] Most of the people involved in the original investigation in nineteen sixty-six and nineteen sixty-seven are almost certainly no longer alive. The fabrication has been declared. The fabricators remain anonymous.

The police went further. Later that month, the chief of the Shizuoka Prefectural Police, Takayoshi Tsuda, went in person to the Hakamada home. He apologised directly, expressing deep regret for what he called the "unspeakable mental distress" the wrongful conviction had caused. [SRC: 7] A senior prosecutor also visited and bowed in apology. [SRC: 7]

[SLOW]

Iwao Hakamada was now, formally and finally, an innocent man.

When he was able to speak about it, he said: "Finally, I have won full and complete victory. I couldn't wait any longer. Thank you very much." [SRC: 4]

In March twenty twenty-five, a court awarded him compensation: two hundred and seventeen million yen, about one point four million US dollars. [SRC: 6] It was calculated at twelve thousand five hundred yen for each day he had been imprisoned. The maximum the law allowed. [SRC: 6]

Twelve thousand five hundred yen a day. Around eighty US dollars. For each of more than seventeen thousand days.

It's worth understanding what that figure represents. Japan's criminal compensation law sets a daily rate for people wrongly imprisoned, with a ceiling. The court awarded the highest rate the law permitted. [SRC: 6] In other words, this was not a court being stingy. It was a court giving everything the law allowed, and the law allowing about eighty dollars for a day spent waiting to be hanged.

## 11. AFTERMATH + MEANING (49:00–53:00)
[SLOW]

There are four people in this story who never got justice at all.

Fujio Hashimoto. His wife. Their daughter. Their son. Killed on June thirtieth, nineteen sixty-six, in their home in Shimizu. For fifty-eight years, the official answer to who killed them was Iwao Hakamada. Now that answer is gone. No one else has ever been charged. Given how much time has passed, it's very likely no one ever will be.

A father who ran a business. A mother of thirty-nine. A seventeen-year-old girl and a fourteen-year-old boy, with their whole lives in front of them, in a booming country, in the summer of nineteen sixty-six. Any relatives who loved them were told, for more than half a century, that the man responsible was on death row. Then, in twenty twenty-four, they were told that he wasn't, and that nobody knows who was.

That's the hidden cost of a wrongful conviction. It doesn't only take the life of the innocent person. It ends the search for the guilty one. Every year spent defending the wrong answer is a year nobody spends looking for the right one.

[NORMAL]

The Hakamada case has become a turning point in a debate Japan has been having with itself for years. About long interrogations without lawyers. About how hard it is to get a retrial. About the fact that prosecutors can appeal a decision to grant a retrial, dragging cases on for decades. [SRC: 1] Lawmakers and legal experts have pushed for reforms to make the retrial system faster and fairer. [SRC: 1]

The phrase "hostage justice" has become well known around the world in recent years, after high-profile cases involving foreign business executives held in Japan drew international attention to the country's long detentions and interrogations without lawyers present. But for many Japanese legal reformers, the Hakamada case is the clearest, most painful example of where that system can lead. A man held for weeks, questioned for hundreds of hours, and then kept under a death sentence for longer than most people live.

Some things have already changed. Since twenty nineteen, Japanese law has required police and prosecutors to record interrogations on video in the most serious cases, those tried before panels that include citizen judges. Campaigners say it doesn't go nearly far enough: it covers only a small share of cases, and suspects still have no right to a lawyer in the room. But it's a step that would have made the two hundred and sixty-four hours of nineteen sixty-six visible to a court.

Iwao Hakamada also became, according to Guinness World Records, the longest-serving death row inmate in the world. [SRC: 3] It's not a record anyone should ever hold.

[SLOW]

And Hideko.

She was born in nineteen thirty-three. She was in her thirties when her brother was arrested. She was ninety-one when he was acquitted. She spent more than half a century on one demand, and never once, as far as anyone has reported, gave up.

After the acquittal, she was asked how she felt. Her answer wasn't triumph. It was this: "This is the beginning." [SRC: 5] She meant that her brother's case should change things for others still waiting.

There are still men on Japan's death row today who say they are innocent, and who have spent decades asking for retrials. Some have lawyers and supporters fighting for them. Most do not have a Hideko. Her point was simple: the system that took fifty-eight years to free her brother is still the system they're waiting on. If it only works when an extraordinary sister, a dissenting judge, a boxing federation and a chemistry experiment all line up at once, then for most people, it doesn't work at all.

That's why, at ninety-one, standing outside the court that had finally cleared her brother, she didn't talk about the end of something. She talked about a beginning.

[NORMAL]

So, the question we started with. How does an innocent man spend forty-six years waiting to be hanged? And what does it take for a country to admit it was wrong?

Here's the most honest answer the record allows.

He spends it in a small cell, listening for footsteps, writing letters that go from confidence to despair to silence. He survives it, physically, by being the kind of man who doesn't go down easily. And he doesn't survive it whole.

And what it takes for the system to admit it was wrong is everything at once. DNA. A judge from the original panel breaking his silence. A scientific experiment with blood and soybean paste. Lawyers working for decades. And a sister who never, ever stopped.

What's striking about this case is that the evidence against him was never strong. A retracted confession. Clothes that didn't fit. A label that meant black, not medium. The problem wasn't that the truth was hidden. It's that, for more than half a century, the people with the power to see it chose not to.
