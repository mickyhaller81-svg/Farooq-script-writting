Phoenix, Arizona. Nineteen ninety-three. A courtroom.

The prosecution is about to call a witness unlike any witness that has ever been called in an American murder trial.

The witness has never spoken. It has never seen a police lineup. It doesn't know the defendant's name. It has been standing in the same patch of Arizona desert for years, and it can't be cross-examined.

It's a tree.

The defence says this is absurd. That no court in the country has ever let a plant's DNA put a man at a crime scene. That this isn't evidence, it's an experiment.

They're right that it's never been done. In a few days, a judge will decide whether it can be.

And a man's freedom, and justice for a thirty-year-old mother of two, depends on the answer.

## 3. THE WORLD BEFORE (1:30–5:00)
[NORMAL]

Her name was Denise Johnson.

She was born on January fourth, nineteen sixty-two. [SRC: 3] In the spring of nineteen ninety-two, she was thirty years old, and she was raising two children on her own. [SRC: 4]

That's almost all the public record tells us about Denise's life. There were no long newspaper profiles, no interviews with her friends, no documentary about who she was. That silence is common for victims whose cases become famous for the science rather than for the person. So we'll be honest about it. We don't know her favourite song, or what she did for work, or what her children called her. We know she was thirty. We know she was a mother. We know she was trying to get somewhere on the day she died.

And we know that what happened to her mattered enough to the people who investigated it that they did something nobody had ever done before.

[NORMAL]

To understand that, you need to understand the place where Denise was found. And the tree that would testify.

In the early nineteen-nineties, Phoenix was one of the fastest-growing cities in America. New subdivisions spread out across the valley every year. But drive far enough in almost any direction, and the city ended abruptly, and the desert began.

West of Phoenix, the city gives way quickly to the Sonoran Desert. Flat, wide land. Creosote bushes. Saguaro cacti standing like sentries. Dry washes that flood for a few hours a year and are dust the rest of the time. And scattered across all of it, a tree with a remarkable trick.

The palo verde.

In Spanish, the name means "green stick." And it's exactly that. The palo verde's bark is green. [SRC: 5] In a desert where leaves are a liability, because they lose precious water, the palo verde drops its tiny leaves for much of the year and does its photosynthesis through its trunk and branches instead. It's one of the most recognisable trees in the American Southwest. It's Arizona's state tree.

In spring, palo verdes explode into yellow flowers, so many that whole hillsides turn gold. And after the flowers come the seeds, in long, flat pods, like small green beans. [SRC: 1] The pods dry, split, and fall. They catch in clothing. They blow into gutters. And they drop into anything parked beneath the branches.

Including the bed of a pickup truck.

[SLOW]

West of the city, in an empty stretch of western Maricopa County, there's an area known as the Caterpillar Proving Grounds. [SRC: 1] It's a remote site, the kind of place where heavy machinery has been tested, and where people on dirt bikes and off-road vehicles go to ride because there's nobody around for miles.

In that area, there's a cluster of palo verde trees.

Keep that cluster in mind. One of the trees in it has a fresh scar on a low branch, the kind of mark you'd get if something heavy had scraped against it. A vehicle, maybe. Reversing too close.

That scar is about to become the most important piece of bark in Arizona legal history.

The Caterpillar Proving Grounds took their name from the heavy-equipment company that tested bulldozers and earth-movers out in the Arizona desert. It's rough, open land, scored with tracks. The kind of place where nobody notices one more set of tyre marks. And, on a weekday or a quiet weekend, the kind of place where nobody might see what happens there for hours.

On Saturday, May second, nineteen ninety-two, Denise Johnson needed a ride.

That's worth pausing on, because it's the kind of detail that, in some retellings, gets turned into a judgement. It shouldn't be. In the early nineteen-nineties, plenty of people without a car asked for rides, or accepted them. It was ordinary. Needing to get somewhere, and trusting someone to take you, isn't a mistake. The only person responsible for what happened next is the person who did it.

She never got where she was going.

## 5. THE DAY (5:30–13:30)
[SLOW]

[COURTROOM]
In the nineteen ninety-three trial, the story of how Denise Johnson was found began with a man on a dirt bike.
[/COURTROOM]

On the morning of Sunday, May third, nineteen ninety-two, a man named Tim Faulkner was riding his dirt bike in that remote area of western Maricopa County, the Caterpillar Proving Grounds. [SRC: 1]

It's the kind of morning that thousands of people in Arizona spend exactly the same way. Early, before the heat. Open desert. The sound of an engine and nothing else.

And then he saw something that made him stop.

Near the cluster of palo verde trees, lying in the brush, was the body of a woman. [SRC: 1]

[NORMAL]

For Tim Faulkner, a Sunday morning ride became a moment he would carry for the rest of his life. People who find a body, especially by chance, in a place they went to for fun, often describe never quite seeing that place the same way again. He did the right thing. He got help.

Faulkner raised the alarm. Deputies from the Maricopa County Sheriff's Office came out to the site.

The woman was identified as Denise Johnson. She had been strangled. [SRC: 3] She had died the day before, on Saturday, May second. [SRC: 3]

That's all this video will say about what happened to her. A thirty-year-old woman, a mother, left in the brush in the desert.

[SLOW]

Now, put yourself in the place of the detectives arriving at that scene.

A remote desert site, miles from anywhere. No houses, no cameras, no neighbours. The ground was dirt and gravel, crisscrossed by tyre tracks from years of off-road vehicles. Any one of them could belong to anyone. In the desert, the weather and the wind work against evidence. Footprints blur. Fibres blow away. Heat breaks things down.

Desert crime scenes have their own rules. Investigators photograph everything before touching anything, because once a scene is walked on, it's changed forever. They look for tyre impressions in softer patches of ground, for drag marks, for anything dropped or torn. They look at the vegetation, too, because broken branches and crushed plants can show where a vehicle or a person moved. In nineteen ninety-two, nobody imagined that the vegetation itself might carry DNA evidence. But the habit of looking at it carefully would turn out to be everything.

How do you find out who brought her here?

[NORMAL]

The answer started with two things.

The first was lying on the ground near Denise's body. A pager. [SRC: 4]

If you're under a certain age, you might need this explained. In the early nineteen-nineties, before mobile phones were common, people carried pagers, also called beepers. Small devices that clipped onto a belt. When someone wanted to reach you, they called a number, and your pager would beep and display a phone number to call back. Every pager was registered to someone.

Detectives traced this one. Paging companies kept subscriber records, the name and address of whoever paid for each device. It was registered to a man in the Phoenix area. [SRC: 4]

In nineteen ninety-two, a pager was a little like a mobile phone number today. It was personal. It was tied to a name. Losing it at the scene of a crime was, for investigators, almost like finding a business card.

And when they looked at him, they found he had a son.

[SLOW]

The second thing came from a witness.

Someone had seen a vehicle in the area around the time Denise was killed. A pickup truck, leaving. [SRC: 4] The witness described it. And the description matched a truck connected to that same family. [SRC: 4]

Witness evidence about vehicles is notoriously tricky. People remember colours and shapes, but not licence plates. At a distance, in bright sun, in an area full of off-road traffic, a pickup truck is a pickup truck. Detectives knew that a defence lawyer would ask the witness how far away they were, how long they looked, and how many other trucks of that type were in Maricopa County. Probably thousands.

So, a pager near the body. A truck seen leaving the area. Both leading to the same man.

On paper, that sounds like a solved case. But every experienced detective knows how much weaker it is than it looks. A pager can be lost. It can be stolen. It can be dropped by someone else. And a witness who sees a truck at a distance, in the desert, can be honest and still be wrong. There are a lot of pickup trucks in Arizona.

What the detectives needed was something that put that specific truck, not just a similar one, at that specific spot.

They were about to find it in the most unlikely place.

## 6. FIRST RESPONSE (13:30–22:30)
[NORMAL]

[COURTROOM]
At trial, the jury heard what the driver had told investigators. His account would turn out to contain the contradiction that broke the case.
[/COURTROOM]

When detectives found the young man the pager and the truck led to, they asked him about May second.

And he didn't deny meeting Denise Johnson.

He told them a story. According to accounts of his statement, he said he'd been on his way home from work when he stopped to make a phone call. A woman approached him and asked for a ride to the interstate. He agreed. [SRC: 6]

He said that during the drive, things became sexual, and that they stopped and had consensual sex in the cab of his truck. [SRC: 6]

And then, he said, when he was letting her out, she tried to take things from his dashboard. His wallet. He caught her. There was a struggle. He got his wallet back. [SRC: 6]

And then, he said, she was gone, and he drove away. [SRC: 6]

[SLOW]

Listen carefully to what's in that story, because it's built to explain things.

If his DNA or fibres were on her, the consensual encounter explained it.

If she had his pager, maybe she'd taken it in the struggle over the dashboard, and dropped it later.

And when the detectives looked at his face, they could see scratches. [SRC: 6] Scratches that, in his story, came from a scuffle over a wallet.

Detectives have a name for this kind of account. It's sometimes called an explaining-away statement. A suspect who knows, or suspects, what evidence the police might have, builds a version of events that gives every piece of it an innocent explanation. The encounter explains the physical contact. The struggle explains the scratches. The theft explains the pager.

The weakness of an account like that is that it has to explain everything. Every piece of evidence it can't explain becomes a crack. And the more detailed the story, the more places a single fact can break it.

It's a story that accounts for almost every piece of evidence. Except one.

He said he'd never been to the place where Denise was found. [SRC: 2]

That was the line he held. Yes, he picked her up. Yes, there was a struggle. But he let her out somewhere else, and whatever happened to her after that, happened after he'd driven away. He had never been in that cluster of palo verde trees at the Caterpillar Proving Grounds.

[NORMAL]

That's the contradiction at the centre of this whole case. If he was telling the truth, he had a sexual encounter with a stranger, a fight over a wallet, and then he left. Someone else, later, took her into the desert and killed her. If he was lying, then he was there, at that spot, when she died.

Everything depended on one question. Was his truck ever at that spot in the desert?

[SLOW]

The lead detective on the case was Charlie Norton of the Maricopa County Sheriff's Office. [SRC: 1]

And back at the scene, something had caught his attention. One of the palo verde trees near where Denise was found had a fresh abrasion on a low branch. [SRC: 1] A scrape. The kind of mark you'd get if something big, like the side or back of a truck, had pushed against it.

A fresh scar on a tree, right where the body was found.

It's the kind of detail most people would never notice. A scrape on a branch in a desert full of scrubby, battered trees. But good crime-scene investigators train themselves to ask of everything they see: is this new? Does it belong to the scene, or to the crime? The scrape was fresh. The bark was freshly broken. Something had hit that tree recently.

And when investigators searched the young man's pickup, they found something in the truck bed.

Two seed pods. From a palo verde tree. [SRC: 1]

[NORMAL]

Now, think about how that would have felt to Detective Norton. Two palo verde pods in the truck. A palo verde tree at the scene with a fresh scrape on it. It's exactly what you'd expect to find if the truck had backed into the tree and knocked a few pods loose into its bed.

But here's the problem. There are palo verde trees everywhere in the Phoenix area. In parks. In car parks. In front yards. Along roads. Any pickup truck that had parked under any palo verde tree in Maricopa County might have pods in the back.

Finding palo verde pods in a truck in Phoenix is like finding sand on a beach. On its own, it proves nothing.

Unless you could prove that those two pods came from that one tree. The tree with the scar.

And nobody, anywhere, had ever done that.

## 7. THE MIDPOINT TURN (22:30–26:00)
[SLOW]

The young man's name was Mark Alan Bogan. [SRC: 1]

And Detective Norton had a question for science that, as far as anyone knew, no detective had ever asked before.

Can you identify an individual tree the way you can identify an individual person?

[NORMAL]

By nineteen ninety-two, DNA fingerprinting of humans was well established, though it was still young. The technique had been invented in England in nineteen eighty-four by a geneticist named Alec Jeffreys. In nineteen eighty-seven and eighty-eight, it was used to catch a killer in the English Midlands, the first murder ever solved with DNA. In the United States, the first conviction using DNA evidence came in Florida in nineteen eighty-seven. By the early nineties, courts across the country had accepted human DNA. Everyone's DNA is unique, except identical twins, and labs could compare a sample from a crime scene with a sample from a suspect.

But trees? Many plants reproduce by cloning themselves, or are so genetically similar to their neighbours that telling them apart might be impossible. Nobody had checked whether palo verde trees were different enough from one another to be told apart by their DNA.

Norton went looking for someone who might know. He called the University of Arizona, and he reached Dr. Timothy Helentjaris, a professor of molecular genetics. [SRC: 1]

Helentjaris wasn't a forensic scientist. He was an academic geneticist, working in a university lab. As far as the record shows, he'd never been involved in a criminal case.

And a sheriff's detective was asking him whether he could match two seed pods to a single tree in the desert.

[SLOW]

Helentjaris decided to find out.

The first question was whether it was even possible: did individual palo verde trees have different DNA? He began collecting samples. And he found that palo verdes have a high degree of genetic variation from one tree to the next. [SRC: 2] Each one had its own pattern.

There's a biological reason that makes sense. Palo verde flowers are pollinated by insects, mostly bees, carrying pollen from one tree to another. That means most palo verde seeds are the offspring of two different parent trees, mixing their genes the way human parents do. Over generations, that produces a population in which, like people, almost every individual is genetically distinct. Plants that clone themselves, or mostly pollinate themselves, can't be told apart that way. The palo verde can.

That meant it might work.

The method he used was called R-A-P-D: randomly amplified polymorphic DNA. [SRC: 1] It's a technique that copies small, random sections of a plant's DNA and compares the patterns those sections make. Two samples from the same tree produce the same pattern. Samples from different trees produce different ones.

Then he designed a test that would hold up to scrutiny.

The design mattered as much as the chemistry. If he only compared the pods with the scarred tree, a defence lawyer could say: maybe all palo verde trees in the area look the same at this level, so of course they match. To answer that, he needed to show that other trees, both near the scene and far away, looked different. Only then would a match mean something. So he went out and sampled trees from the crime scene cluster, and from other locations scattered around the county. A reference population. The plant equivalent of the people in a police lineup who aren't the suspect.

He analysed the DNA of the two seed pods from Bogan's truck. He analysed the DNA of the tree with the scar. And he analysed the DNA of other palo verde trees, from the crime scene, and from sites all around Maricopa County. [SRC: 1] In all, twenty-nine reference trees, examined using forty-seven different DNA markers. [SRC: 7]

What does a result like that actually look like? In a RAPD test, the copied pieces of DNA are run through a gel, a slab of jelly-like material, using an electric current. Smaller pieces travel further, larger ones less far. Stained and photographed, each sample appears as a column of bands, like a barcode printed sideways. Two samples from the same tree produce the same pattern of bands. Different trees produce different patterns: a band here that isn't there, a gap where another tree has a line.

And crucially, he did it blind. [SRC: 1] He didn't know which sample was which while he ran the tests. Like a police lineup in which the officer showing the photographs doesn't know which one is the suspect, so they can't influence the result, even unconsciously.

[PAUSE]

The results came back.

Every one of the trees had a different DNA profile. [SRC: 7]

And the two seed pods from the bed of Mark Bogan's pickup truck matched exactly one tree. [SRC: 7]

The tree with the scar.

Not a tree from a park in Phoenix. Not one from a car park, or a front yard, or a roadside. The one tree, in the remote desert, standing over the place where Denise Johnson's body was found. The tree that had been scraped by something big.

Mark Bogan had said he'd never been there.

The tree said otherwise.

Twenty-nine trees, each with its own genetic signature. Two seed pods from the back of a truck. And only one possible match: the tree with the fresh wound, at the place where Denise was found. If the pods had matched a tree in a Phoenix car park, the case would have been weaker. If they'd matched nothing, it would have helped the defence. They matched the one tree that mattered.

## 8. COMPLICATIONS (26:00–37:00)
[NORMAL]

[COURTROOM]
And so we're back in that Phoenix courtroom in nineteen ninety-three.
[/COURTROOM]

By the time the case reached court, prosecutors had a scientific result no one had ever produced before: two seed pods matched by DNA to a single tree. What they didn't have was any guarantee that a judge would let a jury hear it.

[HALFWAY RESET ENDS]

So now the question isn't whether the tree "knows" what happened. It's whether the law will let a tree speak at all.

[NORMAL]

Courts don't automatically accept new science. And for good reason. History is full of confident experts whose methods turned out to be worthless. Before a jury is allowed to hear scientific evidence, a judge has to decide whether the science is reliable enough to be trusted.

In Arizona in nineteen ninety-three, the test for that was called the Frye standard. [SRC: 1] It came from a nineteen twenty-three federal case in Washington, D.C. A man named James Frye, accused of murder, wanted to introduce the results of an early lie-detector test based on changes in his blood pressure. The court refused. The rule was simple: a scientific technique can be used in court only if it's generally accepted in the relevant scientific community. Not just one expert's opinion. The field as a whole.

And that same year, nineteen ninety-three, the US Supreme Court was rewriting the rules for federal courts. In June, in a case called Daubert versus Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, it set a new standard, asking judges to act as gatekeepers and look directly at a method's reliability: whether it can be tested, whether it's been peer-reviewed, what its error rate is. Arizona's state courts still used Frye at the time. But the whole country's legal system was thinking hard, in exactly that moment, about when science is good enough for a courtroom.

Into that debate walked a geneticist with two seed pods.

[SLOW]

Before the trial, both the prosecution and the defence asked for a Frye hearing: a special hearing, without the jury, to decide whether the plant DNA evidence could be used. [SRC: 1]

It lasted four days. [SRC: 1]

Three experts testified about the RAPD technique and whether it was accepted in the scientific world. [SRC: 1]

Picture the hearing. No jury in the box. Just a judge, the lawyers, and a series of scientists explaining gel images and genetic markers to a courtroom more used to fingerprints and eyewitnesses. Hours of testimony about how RAPD works, what its weaknesses are, and whether scientists outside this one lab would trust it. It's the kind of hearing that almost never makes the news, and it's where the real decision about new science gets made.

The defence had serious arguments, and they deserve to be heard.

They could point out that this had never been done before in any criminal case. That the technique was being used for a purpose it wasn't designed for. That there was no large database of palo verde DNA, so nobody could say how common or rare a particular tree's profile was across the whole state. With human DNA, experts can tell a jury that a profile might be shared by one person in many millions. With palo verde trees, no one had ever counted.

And RAPD itself had critics. It was known to be sensitive to small changes in laboratory conditions. Results could vary between labs. For research purposes, that might be fine. For sending someone to prison for life, the defence argued, it wasn't good enough.

[NORMAL]

The prosecution's reply was the experiment itself. RAPD was an established tool in plant genetics. It had been used in scientific papers. And the way Helentjaris had applied it, with blind testing against twenty-nine other trees, every one of which had come out different, was designed precisely to answer the question of whether the match could be a coincidence.

[SLOW]

It's worth stepping back here, because this wasn't actually the first time a tree had testified in a famous American trial.

In nineteen thirty-five, in New Jersey, a man named Bruno Richard Hauptmann went on trial for the kidnapping and murder of the infant son of the aviator Charles Lindbergh. It was called the trial of the century. And one of the key pieces of evidence was a homemade wooden ladder left at the scene.

A government wood scientist named Arthur Koehler, from the Forest Products Laboratory in Madison, Wisconsin, studied that ladder for months. He identified the types of wood. He traced the marks left by the planing machine at the mill. And he testified that one of the ladder's rails had come from a floorboard in Hauptmann's own attic, matching the grain, the nail holes, the cut.

That was botany as evidence: wood as a witness. But it was based on the eye of an expert, looking at grain and tool marks. What Helentjaris was doing was something different. Not reading the wood, but reading the tree's DNA.

Nearly sixty years after Hauptmann, the question was the same one. Can a piece of a tree tell a jury where a man has been?

## 9. THE BREAK (37:00–43:00)
[NORMAL]

After four days of testimony, the judge ruled.

The trial court found that RAPD technology was generally accepted in the scientific community. [SRC: 1]

The tree could testify.

[SLOW]

At trial, Dr. Helentjaris took the stand and explained to the jury what he'd done. The pods. The scarred tree. The twenty-nine others. The blind test. And the result: the pods in Mark Bogan's truck had come from the tree at the crime scene. [SRC: 1]

There's an irony in calling it a lineup. Human lineups, where a witness picks a suspect out of a row of faces, are one of the least reliable forms of evidence in criminal justice. Memory is fragile. Witnesses are influenced by stress, by suggestion, by who the officer seems to expect them to pick. Hundreds of wrongful convictions in the United States have been traced back to mistaken eyewitness identifications.

The tree lineup had none of those problems. The tree had no memory to fade, no stress, no bias, no officer to please. And the test was run blind. In that sense, the tree was a better witness than most human beings.

Think about how that must have sounded to twelve ordinary people in Phoenix in nineteen ninety-three. Most of them had probably heard of DNA from the news. Very few would have imagined that a tree had DNA you could fingerprint. And yet here was a university professor, calmly explaining that he'd run what amounted to a lineup of trees, and the trees had picked out one of their own.

There's something about a scientist from a university, rather than a police lab, that can be especially persuasive to a jury. Helentjaris had no stake in the outcome. He wasn't a career prosecution witness. He'd been asked a question and had gone to find the answer. For jurors trying to decide whom to trust, that kind of independence matters.

The defence pushed back. That's their job, and it's important. They could argue that a scientific first should never be used to convict someone. They could remind the jury that the pods only showed the truck had been under that tree at some point, not when, and not that Mark Bogan had killed anyone there.

[NORMAL]

But think about how the pieces now fitted together.

Mark Bogan admitted picking Denise up. He admitted a physical struggle with her. He had scratches on his face. A pager connected to his family was found near her body. A witness saw a truck like his near the scene. And now, the tree at the exact spot where she was found had left two of its seed pods in the back of his truck. The tree with a fresh scrape, as if something large had backed into it.

His story depended entirely on one claim: that he had never been there.

The seed pods said he had.

[SLOW]

In closing arguments, the two sides would have framed it very differently. For the prosecution, the tree was the final piece of a picture that already pointed at Mark Bogan, the piece that broke his alibi. For the defence, it was an untested scientific novelty that should never decide a man's life, and everything else in the case had an innocent explanation in his account.

The jury had to decide which picture was true.

Once that one claim fell, the rest of his story looked very different. If he was at that spot, then his account of letting her out somewhere else was untrue. And if that part was untrue, the jury had to ask what else was.

The pager near her body was no longer something she might have taken and dropped somewhere along a road. It was something lost at the place where she died. The scratches on his face were no longer from a fight over a wallet in town. And the scrape on the tree, at the height of a truck, at the spot where she was found, became a picture of a truck manoeuvring in a hurry in the desert.

## 10. RESOLUTION (43:00–49:00)
[NORMAL]

So, the question we started with. How do you make a tree testify, and will a court let it?

The jury reached its verdict.

Mark Alan Bogan was found guilty of first-degree murder. [SRC: 1] He was sentenced to life in prison. [SRC: 4]

It was the first conviction in an American murder trial that relied on plant DNA. [SRC: 2]

For Denise's family, the verdict meant that the man responsible would not walk free. For the investigators, it meant that a hunch about a scraped branch, and a phone call to a university, had held up through a four-day scientific hearing, a trial, and a jury's scrutiny. And for the legal world, it meant that a line had been crossed. From that day on, no defence lawyer in America could say that plant DNA had never been accepted in a murder trial.

[SLOW]

The case made news far beyond Arizona. In May nineteen ninety-three, the journal Science, one of the most prestigious scientific publications in the world, ran a story about it. Its headline was "Botanical Witness for the Prosecution." [SRC: 2] It described the first time that DNA evidence from something other than a human had been admitted in a criminal trial. [SRC: 2]

A small trial in Arizona, with no celebrity defendant and no national television coverage, had suddenly become a scientific landmark. Legal scholars began writing about it. Forensic textbooks started including it. In the years that followed, when lawyers in other states wanted to introduce non-human DNA evidence, Bogan was one of the first cases they cited.

For forensic scientists, it opened a door. If you could fingerprint a palo verde tree, what else could you fingerprint? Grass. Pollen. Soil microbes. Algae. Animal hair. The idea that the natural world at a crime scene could be read genetically, and could place a person or a vehicle somewhere specific, began to spread.

[NORMAL]

Bogan appealed. His main argument was the one his lawyers had made from the start: that the judge should never have allowed the plant DNA evidence. [SRC: 1]

In nineteen ninety-five, the Arizona Court of Appeals rejected that argument. [SRC: 1] It found that the trial court had properly allowed Dr. Helentjaris to testify about the genetic match between the seed pods and the scraped palo verde tree. [SRC: 1] The conviction stood.

The case became State versus Bogan, and it's still cited in textbooks and courses on forensic science. A tree had testified, and an appeals court had confirmed it was allowed to.

[SLOW]

It's worth being clear about the limits of what the tree proved, because that's part of being fair to everyone in the story.

The seed pods showed that Mark Bogan's truck had been under that particular tree. They didn't, on their own, show what happened there, or when, or who did what. The conviction rested on all the evidence together: his own admissions, the scratches, the pager, the witness, the pods. What the tree did was destroy the one part of his account that kept all the rest from fitting together.

In court, Mark Bogan had argued, through his lawyers, that the science was untested and that he should not be convicted on it. A jury and an appeals court decided otherwise. That's how the system is supposed to work. New science gets tested by the defence, examined by a judge, weighed by a jury, and reviewed on appeal. Each step happened here.

[AD BREAK OK]

## 11. AFTERMATH + MEANING (49:00–53:00)
[NORMAL]

In the decades since, forensic botany has grown into a recognised field. [SRC: 5] Investigators now routinely think about the plants at a crime scene. Pollen on a suspect's clothing can show which region they've been in. Seeds in a shoe tread can point to a specific type of habitat. Microscopic algae called diatoms, found in the lungs of a drowning victim, can show whether they drowned in a particular body of water. Even the soil on a tyre can be matched to a location.

And non-human DNA has followed the same path. In the mid-nineteen-nineties, investigators in Canada used DNA from a cat's hair, found on a jacket, to link a suspect to a murder. Dog DNA, horse DNA, and even the DNA of bacteria and fungi have since been used in investigations. Every one of those cases, in one way or another, walks through the door the palo verde opened.

It's also a field that has learned to be careful. The same national scientific review in two thousand and nine that criticised many forensic disciplines for overstating their certainty applies here too. A plant match is only as good as the reference samples, the lab's controls, and the honesty of the expert about what the result can and can't show. The palo verde case worked because the method was tested blind, against a real reference population, and challenged in court. Without those safeguards, the same kind of evidence could mislead.

The specific technique Helentjaris used, RAPD, has largely been replaced in forensic work by more reliable and reproducible genetic markers. Science moves on. But the principle he proved, that individual plants can be genetically identified, and that a court will accept it if the method is sound and carefully tested, is still the foundation.

[SLOW]

But let's come back to where this should end. Not with the tree. With Denise.

Denise Johnson was thirty. She was raising two children. On Saturday, May second, nineteen ninety-two, she needed a ride. And the man who gave her one took her into the desert.

Her children were young in nineteen ninety-two. Today they'd be adults, probably in their thirties or forties, maybe with children of their own. Grandchildren Denise never met.

Her children grew up without her. Whatever their lives became, their mother wasn't there for it. And the case that should be remembered as hers is remembered, almost everywhere, as the case of the palo verde tree.

That's the strange fate of victims in landmark forensic cases. The science becomes famous. The person becomes a footnote. Search for her name and you'll find pages about RAPD markers and seed pods, and almost nothing about who she was.

There's a lesson in that for anyone who tells true-crime stories, too, including this channel. It's easy to be fascinated by the method: the pods, the markers, the courtroom drama. It's harder, and more important, to remember that every landmark case starts with someone's worst day. The palo verde case is in the textbooks because a woman named Denise didn't come home.

So, for a moment, set the science aside. A mother of two died in the Arizona desert. Detectives, a sheriff's office, and a professor who had never worked a crime decided that she deserved an answer, even if getting it meant doing something no one had ever done. That's what the tree really testifies to.

[NORMAL]

So, the question this story started with. How do you make a tree testify?

You notice a scar on a branch that everyone else would walk past. You find two seed pods that most people would brush out of a truck bed without a thought. You find a scientist willing to try something new, and you make him prove it the hard way, blind, against twenty-nine other trees. And then you put the method in front of the toughest audience there is: a defence lawyer, a judge, a jury, and an appeals court.

What's striking about this case is how ordinary every piece of it was. A dirt-bike ride. A pager. A scraped branch. Two bean-like pods. None of it was remarkable on its own. The remarkable thing was the decision to take it seriously.

[COMMENT TRIGGER #2]
Which leaves a question for you. This was the first time plant DNA was used to help convict someone, and it was used in a murder case where the stakes were life in prison. On one hand, waiting for "safer" cases would mean someone else's killer going free in the meantime. On the other, a first-of-its-kind method has no track record. Should a brand-new kind of forensic evidence ever be used for the first time in a case that serious? I'd like to know what you think.
