## 5. THE DAY (5:30–13:30)
[SLOW]

Minneapolis in the early nineteen-nineties was a city going through one of the most violent periods in its history. Homicides were climbing year after year. By the middle of the decade, the national press would give the city a grim nickname, Murderapolis, after a record number of killings. Detectives were overwhelmed. Every new case competed for time with a dozen others.

That's the city in which Jeanie's case landed.

The first sign that something was wrong came through the ceiling.

Neighbours in Jeanie's building noticed water leaking from her apartment. And the water was red. [SRC: 1]

Think about those neighbours. People living their ordinary lives in an apartment building, noticing a stain spreading, and slowly realising what it might be. They did the right thing. They called for help.

On June thirteenth, nineteen ninety-three, Minneapolis police arrived at the building. [SRC: 1]

Inside, they found Jeanie in her bathroom, in the shower. The water had been running. [SRC: 1]

She had been stabbed to death. [SRC: 1]

That's all this video will say about what was done to her. The number of wounds has been reported many times, in many places. It won't be repeated here. What matters is that she was killed in an act of extraordinary violence, in her own home, and that she deserved far better than what came next.

[NORMAL]

The officers who walked into that apartment found a scene that one of them, a retired sergeant named Robert Krebs, would still remember vividly almost thirty years later, when he testified about it in court. [SRC: 4]

Sergeant Krebs would tell the jury something small and human about that day. In the apartment, he saw a photograph of the woman who lived there. And she looked familiar to him. "She looked familiar to me," he said. [SRC: 10] In a big city, police officers come to know faces from their beats. Jeanie wasn't a stranger to the neighbourhood. She was someone people had seen, and known, and walked past.

Krebs and another retired sergeant, David Palmer, also described parts of the apartment that seemed undisturbed. The kitchen looked orderly, apart from a few dirty dishes in the sink, and there was no blood there. [SRC: 10] Whatever happened had happened in other rooms.

And among everything they saw, there was one detail that would turn out to be the key to the entire case.

Footprints.

[SLOW]

In the bedroom, on the floor, there were footprints made in blood. [SRC: 1]

And they were bare. Not shoeprints. Not sock prints. The prints of a person's naked feet. [SRC: 1]

Here's why that mattered so much. Jeanie was wearing socks. [SRC: 1] So the bare footprints couldn't be hers. And they were made in her blood, which meant they were left after she had been attacked. [SRC: 1]

Whoever made those footprints had been in that apartment, barefoot, while Jeanie was bleeding, or immediately after.

Investigators understood very quickly what that meant. The footprints were the killer's.

It's a piece of reasoning so simple it's almost elegant. Socks on the victim. Bare feet on the floor. Blood under the bare feet. Only one kind of person could have made those prints: someone who was there when the blood was fresh. In a case that would later turn into a fierce argument about DNA and timing, that simple chain of logic, laid down on the first day, would end up being the prosecution's strongest answer.

[NORMAL]

A footprint is a lot like a fingerprint. And bare footprints at a murder scene are rare. Most killers wear shoes. Socks blur the detail of the sole. For a killer to walk barefoot through a scene, in blood, and leave clear prints behind, is unusual enough that investigators who've worked hundreds of homicides may never see it.

Why would someone be barefoot? Nobody outside that apartment knows for certain. But it's worth noticing what it suggests. A person who was barefoot in Jeanie's apartment was a person who had been there long enough, or comfortably enough, to take their shoes off. The soles of the human foot have ridges, the same kind of friction ridges you have on your fingertips. And like fingerprints, those patterns are believed to be unique to each person. If you can get a clear enough print, and a clear enough comparison, you can say whose foot made it.

But in nineteen ninety-three, there was nobody to compare them with.

[SLOW]

There was other evidence too. Investigators collected items from the apartment: a comforter, a towel from the bathroom with blood on it, a washcloth, a T-shirt. They took swabs from the sink. [SRC: 9]

Those items carried traces of male DNA. [SRC: 9]

And there was something else, a detail that would matter decades later. Not all of the male DNA in that apartment came from the same man. There was more than one male profile. [SRC: 8]

Remember that. It's the question this story will end on.

[NORMAL]

So on the day she was found, police had bloody, bare footprints, and DNA that would one day be readable. What they didn't have was a name. Or, it would turn out, much of anyone's attention.

## 6. FIRST RESPONSE (13:30–22:30)
[NORMAL]

In June nineteen ninety-three, the murder of Jeanie Childs got very little press coverage. [SRC: 2] A few brief reports. No major appeals. No front-page campaign. No public outcry.

Compare that to how a city reacts when a suburban mother, a college student or a child is killed. Rewards. Billboards. Vigils. Politicians at press conferences. Television crews outside the house for days.

None of that happened for Jeanie.

[SLOW]

It's worth being honest about why. Victims who are poor, who struggle with addiction, who do sex work, who are seen as living on the margins, are often treated very differently, by the press, by the public, and sometimes by the system. Their cases get less coverage. Less pressure to solve them. Fewer resources. There's an ugly, unspoken assumption that somehow their deaths matter less, or that they were in danger anyway.

Researchers and advocates have documented this pattern for decades, in city after city. Murders of sex workers are among the least likely to be solved. And the people who commit them know it. Predators deliberately target people they believe nobody will look for.

One of the most notorious serial killers in American history, the Green River Killer in Washington State, said exactly that after his arrest. He told investigators he chose women involved in sex work because he believed he could kill them without anyone reporting them missing quickly, and without the police looking hard. He was able to kill for years before he was caught. That's what society's indifference looks like from the other side: a hunting ground.

That doesn't mean the detectives in nineteen ninety-three didn't care. The officers who worked the scene remembered it for the rest of their careers. But the case didn't get the kind of public energy that pushes an investigation forward. And without a suspect, without a match, without witnesses, it slowly stopped moving.

[NORMAL]

[BETTY]
For Betty Eakman, the years after June nineteen ninety-three were, as she would later put it, a kind of hell. "It's put my life through so much hell," she said. [SRC: 3] She kept loving her daughter, and kept waiting.
[/BETTY]

Think about what Betty's work meant in that context. A hospice worker spends her days helping families say goodbye. Making sure nobody dies alone, frightened, without dignity. And her own daughter had died in exactly the opposite way, and Betty never got to say goodbye at all.

The months became years. The detectives who had worked the case moved on. The evidence went into storage: the towel, the washcloth, the comforter, the T-shirt. Photographs of the bloody footprints went into a file.

That detail matters more than it seems. For twenty-five years, someone had to keep that evidence properly stored, labelled, and protected from contamination. Evidence rooms flood. Files get lost. Departments clear out old boxes to make space. In some cities, evidence from old cases has been destroyed by mistake or by policy. In Jeanie's case, it survived. Without that, nothing that follows would have been possible.

Through the late nineteen-nineties and the two-thousands, DNA technology advanced. Profiles could be developed from smaller and older samples. National databases grew. Every so often, an unsolved case got a hit.

Jeanie's didn't. Whoever left his DNA in her apartment had never been convicted of anything that put him in the system.

And a footprint? There's no national database of footprints. You can't search a bare footprint against millions of people the way you can search a fingerprint. A footprint is only useful once you already have a suspect.

So the case sat. For years. For decades.

Jeanie's little sister, Cindy, grew up, married, had children. Those children grew up too, never knowing their Aunt Jeanne. [SRC: 3]

Every year on Jeanie's birthday, in April, and every June, on the anniversary, the family remembered. Every year, they wondered whether the phone would ring. For twenty-five years, it didn't.

Then, in twenty eighteen, a quarter of a century after Jeanie was found, the Minneapolis Police Department opened the file again.

## 7. THE MIDPOINT TURN (22:30–26:00)
[SLOW]

In twenty eighteen, Minneapolis police began working with the FBI to review the case. [SRC: 1]

The world of cold cases had just been transformed. In April of that year, investigators in California had identified a suspect in the Golden State Killer case using genetic genealogy, tracing the killer through relatives who had uploaded their DNA to a public website. Police departments across the country were taking another look at their oldest unsolved cases, wondering if the same approach could work for them.

The FBI was beginning to build its own expertise in this kind of work, and partnering with local departments on violent cold cases. For a city police department with a twenty-five-year-old murder, that partnership meant resources and experience it might not have had on its own.

Jeanie's case was a perfect candidate. They had good-quality DNA from the scene. They had no suspect. And they had a family who had never stopped asking.

[NORMAL]

Investigators sent DNA from the crime scene to a private laboratory, DNA Solutions, to develop a new kind of profile, called an SNP profile. [SRC: 1] Instead of the couple of dozen markers used in criminal databases, an SNP profile looks at hundreds of thousands of points across the genome. It's the kind of data genealogy websites use to find your cousins.

Police arranged for that profile to be uploaded to several genealogy services: GEDmatch, Ancestry, and MyHeritage. [SRC: 1]

And on MyHeritage, they got a hit. [SRC: 6]

Not the killer himself. A relative. Someone whose DNA overlap suggested they were a first cousin of the person who had been in Jeanie's apartment. [SRC: 6]

[SLOW]

It's worth noting that the genealogy databases have different rules about police use, and those rules have shifted over the years. Some allow it if users opt in. Some prohibit it. The question of which databases police may use, and how, has become a live debate in its own right. In this case, the courts that later reviewed the investigation focused on a narrower question, the napkin, and upheld how it was handled.

A first cousin is a close match in genealogy terms. You share a set of grandparents. From a first cousin, a genealogist can work backward to the shared grandparents, and then forward through all of their grandchildren, looking for the one who fits.

The forensic genealogist working the case built that tree. And she narrowed it down to two brothers, living in Minnesota. [SRC: 8]

One of them was a man named Jerry Westrom. [SRC: 8]

[PAUSE]

When investigators identified him, Jerry Arnold Westrom was fifty-two years old. [SRC: 9] He lived in Isanti, a small city north of Minneapolis. He was a businessman, with a family. And he was, as the press would later put it, a hockey dad. His daughter played, and he went to her games. [SRC: 9]

He had no violent criminal record.

In nineteen ninety-three, he would have been in his mid-twenties.

[NORMAL]

The genealogy pointed at him. But a family tree isn't proof. It narrows a search; it doesn't close a case. The detectives needed a direct sample of Jerry Westrom's DNA, to compare with the DNA from Jeanie's apartment.

And they needed to get it without him knowing.

## 8. COMPLICATIONS (26:00–37:00)
[NORMAL]

By early twenty nineteen, Minneapolis police had a name from a family tree, a man with no record and a respectable life, and bare footprints from twenty-five years earlier. What they didn't have was his DNA.

[HALFWAY RESET ENDS]

So now the question was simple, and delicate. How do you get a sample from a man who doesn't know he's a suspect, in a way a court will accept?

[SLOW]

The answer was a hockey game.

In January twenty nineteen, investigators followed Jerry Westrom to an ice rink, where he'd gone to watch his daughter play. [SRC: 9]

Picture the scene. A Minnesota hockey rink in January. Cold air, bright lights, the scrape of skates. Parents in team jackets in the stands, cheering. A concession stand selling coffee, popcorn, hot dogs.

Jerry Westrom bought a hot dog. [SRC: 9]

He ate it. He wiped his mouth with a paper napkin. And he threw the napkin away. [SRC: 9]

When he'd gone, investigators retrieved it from the trash. [SRC: 9]

There's a terrible contrast in that scene. A father watching his child play, in one of the most wholesome settings imaginable. Kids in helmets, parents cheering, the smell of popcorn. And twenty-five years earlier, according to the verdict a jury would later reach, the same man had been barefoot in Jeanie's apartment. People are capable of living entire lives on top of a secret. Most of the people around them never suspect a thing.

[NORMAL]

Investigators who do this kind of work describe it as one of the most nerve-racking parts of a case. You can't approach the person. You can't let them notice you. You can't touch anything until it's been thrown away, and once it's in a trash can, you have to be sure the item you retrieve is the right one, and that nobody else's DNA has been mixed in. One wrong napkin, one contaminated sample, and a defence lawyer will tear the evidence apart.

Think about how ordinary that moment was. Thousands of parents, at thousands of rinks, do exactly the same thing every winter weekend. A hot dog, a napkin, the trash can. Nobody gives it a thought.

But a napkin used to wipe your mouth carries saliva. And saliva carries cells. And cells carry DNA.

The napkin went to the lab.

[SLOW]

The DNA from the napkin matched the DNA from Jeanie Childs's apartment. [SRC: 9]

The towel. The washcloth. The comforter. The T-shirt. The sink. The traces of a man left in that apartment in June nineteen ninety-three belonged to the man who had just eaten a hot dog at his daughter's hockey game.

[NORMAL]

In February twenty nineteen, Jerry Westrom was arrested. [SRC: 9]

The news went off like a bomb in Isanti. A businessman. A family man. A familiar face at the rink. Charged with a twenty-five-year-old murder in Minneapolis.

Imagine the scene at his home, and at the rink, when word spread. Parents who'd sat beside him in the stands. Business contacts. Neighbours. People who had known him for years as one kind of man, suddenly reading that police believed he was another. For his own family, it must have been devastating in ways that have nothing to do with guilt or innocence. This video won't speculate about what they knew. The record says nothing to suggest they knew anything at all.

Detectives sat down with him. Sergeant Chris Karakostas of the Minneapolis police was one of those who questioned him. [SRC: 8] They asked him about Jeanie Childs. About the apartment. About June nineteen ninety-three.

Westrom denied knowing her. He denied knowing what had happened in that apartment. [SRC: 8]

That denial would matter at trial. If he had admitted being in Jeanie's apartment at some earlier time, for some other reason, the DNA would have been easier to explain. By denying he knew her at all, he left the jury with a simple contradiction: his genetic material was all over her apartment, on a bloody towel, and he said he'd never known her.

[SLOW]

But the investigators now had a second way to test his account. Remember the footprints.

With Westrom identified, police could finally do what they couldn't do in nineteen ninety-three: compare the bloody, bare footprints from Jeanie's bedroom with the feet of a specific person. They took known footprint impressions from Jerry Westrom. [SRC: 8]

And those comparisons would become the centre of the trial.

[NORMAL]

It's worth pausing on the legal question this case raised, because it went all the way to the Supreme Court of the United States.

Jerry Westrom's lawyers argued that what the police had done with that napkin was a search. That analysing a person's DNA, the most personal information a human being has, without a warrant, violated his constitutional right to privacy. [SRC: 6]

The police argued the opposite. He threw the napkin away in a public place. Once you abandon something, you don't have a reasonable expectation of privacy in it.

It's a genuinely hard question. Every one of us sheds DNA constantly. On every cup, every fork, every tissue, every door handle. If police can collect and analyse anything we leave behind, then in theory they can collect anyone's DNA, anywhere, any time, without a warrant. Supporters of the practice say it's no different from police picking up a fingerprint from a glass. Critics say DNA is different, because it can reveal far more than identity: health, ancestry, family relationships.

If you watched our episode on Sylvia Quayle in Colorado, this question will sound familiar. In that case, a Vanilla Coke can taken from a dumpster in Nebraska raised exactly the same issue, and a Colorado appeals court wrestled with it in twenty twenty-six. Two different states. Two different discarded objects. The same question about the DNA all of us leave behind every day.

Courts would have to decide. But first, there would be a trial.

## 9. THE BREAK (37:00–43:00)
[NORMAL]

In August twenty twenty-two, Jerry Westrom went on trial in Hennepin County District Court for the murder of Jeanie Childs. [SRC: 4]

Twenty-nine years after she was found.

[SLOW]

The prosecution's case rested on two pillars. The DNA, and the footprints.

The DNA put Westrom in the apartment. His genetic material was on the bloody towel, the washcloth, the comforter, the T-shirt, the sink. [SRC: 9]

Westrom was represented by an experienced Minneapolis defence attorney, Steve Meshbesher. [SRC: 10] And the defence had an answer to the DNA, and it's important to hear it fairly.

Westrom's lawyers argued that DNA alone couldn't establish when he had been in the apartment. [SRC: 8] They pointed out that Jeanie worked as a sex worker, and that other men's DNA was also present in the apartment. [SRC: 8] DNA, they argued, could show he had been there at some point. It couldn't show he had been there when she was killed.

That's a serious argument. DNA doesn't carry a timestamp. Someone can leave DNA in a room days before, or after, something happens there.

The defence went further. Before the trial, Westrom's lawyers had wanted to put several alternative suspects in front of the jury. The judge allowed one. [SRC: 10] The defence pointed to a man who, according to trial reporting, lived in the apartment with Jeanie and was involved in her work, and noted that some of his hairs had been found in her hand. [SRC: 10]

That man was never charged with any crime in connection with Jeanie's death. This video won't name him. But the argument was put to the jury, and the jury weighed it.

[NORMAL]

Which is why the footprints mattered so much.

The footprints were made in blood. Jeanie's blood. They could only have been left while she was bleeding, or immediately afterwards. If the footprints were Jerry Westrom's, then he wasn't just in that apartment at some point. He was there at the time of the killing.

[SLOW]

So the trial became, in large part, a battle over feet.

It's worth understanding why footprint evidence is contested. Friction ridge analysis of fingerprints is long established in courts, though even it has been criticised in recent years for overstating certainty. Footprints are used far less often. Fewer examiners have deep experience with them. The prints at a crime scene are often partial, smudged, or distorted by movement, blood, and the surface they were made on. And there's an ongoing debate between methods that focus on fine ridge detail, the way fingerprint examiners work, and methods that focus on the overall size and shape of a foot. That debate played out, live, in front of the jury.

For the prosecution, Mark Ulrick, the forensic science supervisor for the Minneapolis Police Department, testified that he had compared the bloody footprints with Westrom's known footprints using friction ridge analysis, the same kind of detailed ridge comparison used for fingerprints. He testified that Westrom was the source of three of the bloody footprints at the scene. [SRC: 8]

The defence brought its own experts.

A forensic podiatrist, Dr. Michael Nirenberg, used a different technique, known as the Reel method, which compares the overall shape and dimensions of a foot rather than the fine ridge detail, to dispute the footprint findings. [SRC: 8]

And another forensic scientist, Alicia McCarthy, reviewed the prints. Here's the remarkable part. She agreed with the prosecution about one small area: a section just below the left big toe, labelled E-two. She agreed that it had been made by Jerry Westrom. [SRC: 8] But she disagreed with Ulrick's conclusions about the other prints. [SRC: 8]

[NORMAL]

Picture what the jurors were weighing. Photographs of bare, bloody footprints on a bedroom floor from nineteen ninety-three. Enlargements of ridge detail. Charts comparing those ridges with impressions taken from the defendant's feet in twenty nineteen. Experts disagreeing about how many of the prints could be tied to him, and how confidently.

Think about what that meant for the jury. Even an expert brought in to challenge the prosecution's footprint analysis had agreed that at least part of one bloody footprint, made in Jeanie's blood, came from Jerry Westrom's foot.

The prosecution put it to the jury directly. The DNA might, on its own, be explained by an earlier visit. But the bloody footprints placed Westrom in that apartment while Jeanie was bleeding from the attack. [SRC: 8]

[SLOW]

In closing arguments, the two sides painted very different pictures. The defence asked the jury to see an apartment full of DNA from different people, footprint experts who disagreed, and an alternative suspect with hairs in Jeanie's hand. Reasonable doubt, they argued, was everywhere. The prosecution asked the jury to focus on one image: a bare foot, in Jeanie's blood, and the ridges on it matching the man who said he'd never known her.

Jerry Westrom did not testify in his own defence. [SRC: 8]

That's his constitutional right, and a jury is told it can't hold it against him. The jury did hear his voice, though, through the recording of his twenty nineteen police interview, in which he denied knowing Jeanie Childs or knowing what had happened in the apartment. [SRC: 8]

The judge would have reminded the jurors of the standard. Proof beyond a reasonable doubt. Not proof beyond all doubt; that's impossible in any human case. Not "probably guilty." A doubt based on reason, the kind that would make a sensible person hesitate in the most important decisions of their own life. If they had that kind of doubt, they had to acquit.

Then the jury went out.

## 10. RESOLUTION (43:00–49:00)
[NORMAL]

So: who left those bare, bloody footprints in Jeanie Childs's apartment?

The jury deliberated for about two hours. [SRC: 9]

Two hours, after a trial about a twenty-nine-year-old murder, conflicting expert testimony, and a defence built on doubt.

A quick verdict doesn't mean a careless one. It usually means that, once the jurors sat down together, they found they mostly agreed. They had the DNA. They had a footprint that even a defence expert had tied, in part, to Westrom. They had his flat denial that he'd ever known her. And they had no innocent explanation for how his bare foot ended up in her blood.

They found Jerry Westrom guilty of first-degree premeditated murder and second-degree intentional murder. [SRC: 5]

[SLOW]

The sentencing came on September ninth, twenty twenty-two. [SRC: 3]

Jeanie's family spoke.

[SRC: 3] She said her heart had been broken for twenty-nine years. [SRC: 3]

Think about that detail: twenty-nine years. Betty had spent nearly three decades not knowing who had done it, then three more years waiting through an arrest, delays and a trial. She had been working in a hospice when she learned of her daughter's death. Now she was standing in a courtroom, finally able to say it out loud to the man the jury said was responsible.

Jeanie's niece, Melissa Beaulieu, spoke about the long reach of a loss like this. "The effect something like this creates can be vicious," she said. "They affect you for an entire lifetime." [SRC: 3]

And a statement from Jeanie's sister, Cindy, the little sister Jeanie had doted on, was read into the record. "My children have grown up never knowing their Aunt Jeanne," it said. "I am so sorry we never got to say goodbye." [SRC: 3]

[NORMAL]

Then the judge passed sentence.

Life in prison. [SRC: 3]

For first-degree premeditated murder, the sentence in Minnesota is mandatory: life. The judge had no discretion about that. What mattered was the parole rule. Because the murder was committed in nineteen ninety-three, the sentence was governed by the law at that time. Under it, Westrom will not be eligible for parole until he has served thirty years. [SRC: 3] He was fifty-six at sentencing. Thirty years would take him well into his eighties.

[SLOW]

Westrom appealed. His lawyers took the napkin argument, the question of whether analysing his discarded DNA without a warrant was an unconstitutional search, to the Minnesota Supreme Court.

On May eighth, twenty twenty-four, the court ruled. [SRC: 6] The court drew a careful line. Its ruling focused on what the police actually did with the napkin: the kind of DNA analysis that could only compare his identity with the DNA from the scene. It held that the analysis of the napkin, which could only match his DNA to the DNA found at the crime scene, was not a search, and that Westrom had no reasonable expectation of privacy in that identifying information. [SRC: 6] The court affirmed his conviction for first-degree premeditated murder. [SRC: 6] It vacated the second-degree conviction for a technical reason, because a person can't stand convicted twice for the same killing, but he remains in prison for life. [SRC: 5]

His lawyers then asked the US Supreme Court to take the case, arguing that society should recognise a reasonable expectation of privacy in the DNA we shed. [SRC: 7] On October twenty-first, twenty twenty-four, the Supreme Court declined to hear it. [SRC: 7]

When the US Supreme Court declines to hear a case, it doesn't mean the justices agree with the lower court. It just means they've chosen not to take it up. Thousands of petitions are turned away every year. But for Westrom, it meant the end of his direct appeals. The question of whether we have a right to privacy in the DNA we leave behind will have to be answered in some other case, on some other day.

The conviction stands.

## 11. AFTERMATH + MEANING (49:00–53:00)
[SLOW]

There's one more piece of this story, and it's the one that doesn't fit neatly.

Remember the other male DNA in Jeanie's apartment? It wasn't Jerry Westrom's. [SRC: 8] Investigators found at least one other male DNA profile there, and, according to reporting on the case, its source has never been identified. [SRC: 8]

It's important to be careful with that fact. Science may yet answer that question. The same genealogy techniques that identified Jerry Westrom could, in principle, be used to identify the source of the second profile. Whether investigators have tried, or whether they ever will, hasn't been made public.

A second DNA profile doesn't mean a second killer. Jeanie's apartment was her home, and many people may have been there for many reasons. The jury heard about the other DNA, weighed it, and convicted Jerry Westrom. The Minnesota Supreme Court upheld that conviction.

But it does mean there's still a stranger in this story. A man whose genetic trace was in Jeanie's apartment, whose name nobody knows, and whose part in her life, or her last days, may never be explained.

[NORMAL]

[BETTY]
"I'll never forget," Betty Eakman said, "and love her the way I always did." [SRC: 3]
[/BETTY]

Jeanie's sister Cindy has said something about their mother that sums up this whole story. "I give my mom a lot of credit," she said, "because she never gave up." [SRC: 12]

And Cindy has said something about her sister that every person who hears this story should hold on to. People, she said, would tell her that Jeanie had lived a lifestyle that led to her fate. "But I don't believe that," she said. "She was still a human being." [SRC: 12]

Let's end with Jeanie, the way her mother would want.

Jeanie Childs was born in April nineteen fifty-eight. She was a big sister who adored her little sister. She was a stepmother who stayed with children who weren't hers, after the marriage that made her their stepmother was over. She was, according to the people who loved her, happy, compassionate, and her own person. Her life was hard in ways that some people have used, for thirty years, as an excuse to look away from her.

For decades, almost nobody outside her family knew her name. Now, thanks to the work of investigators, reporters and her own family's persistence, her case has been covered by national television, taught in university forensics classes, and argued in the US Supreme Court. [SRC: 8] That attention came thirty years late. But it came.

She deserved the same urgency, the same press coverage, the same billboards, the same outrage, as any other murder victim in Minneapolis in nineteen ninety-three. She didn't get them. What she got, eventually, was a cold-case team, a genealogist, an FBI partnership, and a jury that took two hours.

[NORMAL]

So, the question we started with. Who left bare, bloody footprints in Jeanie Childs's apartment, and why did it take twenty-six years to find out?

A jury, and two courts above it, have answered the first half. Jerry Westrom was convicted of her murder.

The second half is harder. It took twenty-six years because the technology didn't exist until it did. But also because, in nineteen ninety-three, too few people outside her family were demanding an answer. Jeanie's case didn't go cold only because the evidence was hard to read. It went cold because, for a long time, not enough people cared to keep it warm.

There's also a lesson in this case about patience. Nothing about the evidence changed between nineteen ninety-three and twenty eighteen. The footprints were the same footprints. The DNA on the towel was the same DNA. What changed was what people could do with it, and whether anyone was willing to try. Cold cases aren't solved by evidence alone. They're solved when evidence meets someone who refuses to let it sit on a shelf.

What's striking about this case is that Jeanie herself left the most important evidence. She fought. Her killer had to step in her blood to leave. And that footprint, made in the last minutes of her life, waited twenty-nine years to tell a jury who had been in the room.
