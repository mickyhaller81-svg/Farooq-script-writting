# Strange Things That Were Completely Normal in the 1950s | History for Sleep

## Part One — Around a Quarter Past Four in the Afternoon: The Streetlights and the Milk Box

The light is going now, slowly, the way it does at the end of a short winter day.

Over the flat land of Long Island, the January sky has turned a pale, washed blue, fading towards apricot in the west, where the sun is sliding down behind a long row of bare young maple trees. They were planted only a few years ago, and they're still thin, no taller than the rooftops. Beneath them, along a gently curving street, there are houses. Dozens of them. Hundreds of them, if you could rise up and look. Low, neat, single-storey houses with steep little roofs and big front windows, each one set back the same distance from the street, each one with its own square of frozen lawn, each one with a thin, straight line of smoke rising from a short metal chimney into the cold, still air.

A boy on a bicycle comes coasting down the middle of the street with no hands, his breath steaming, his cheeks red, a cap pulled down over his ears. A dog runs beside him, barking with joy at nothing in particular. Somewhere a screen door bangs. A mother's voice calls a name, long and sing-song, from a back step. And on the front stoop of one of these small houses, beside the door, sits a squat metal box, painted white, with a hinged lid. Inside it, rinsed clean and waiting, are two empty glass milk bottles.

This is Levittown, New York. The day is Monday, the twenty-fourth of January, 1955. And the evening that is beginning right now is an ordinary one, in an ordinary house, on an ordinary street, in what was then one of the most famous new towns in America.

When most of us picture the 1950s, we picture something we've seen on a screen. A spotless kitchen. A mother in a starched dress and pearls taking a roast out of the oven. A father in a hat coming through the door and calling out that he's home. Children who say "gee whiz" and are home by supper. Everything bright, everything simple, everything in black and white, with a laugh track.

But that's not quite where we're going tonight. Not the 1950s of the sitcom reruns. The real decade was stranger than that, and gentler in some ways, and harder in others, and far more interesting. It was full of things that seemed completely normal to the people who lived through them, and that seem astonishing to us now. Things they did every single day without a second thought.

And here is something I should tell you honestly before we go inside. If you were taken from your own home tonight and set down in this little house in your slippers, knowing only what people knew in January 1955, you might not have kept yourself safe until morning. Not because the people here were careless, but because some of the most ordinary things around them, the things they trusted, have turned out to be dangerous. And if you were set down here knowing everything you know now, you would spend the whole evening staring, puzzled and amazed, at things that nobody else in the house would even notice. Either way, without someone to guide you, you would not have got through to morning without a few surprises.

So tonight, I'll be your guide. Slowly. One thing at a time.

And while we go, I'd like you to carry three small puzzles with you, the way you might carry three smooth pebbles in a coat pocket.

The first is this. Why would a smiling doctor in a white coat be recommending a brand of cigarettes from the pages of a magazine on the family's kitchen table, and why would almost nobody in the house find that odd?

The second. Why would a seven-year-old boy, who isn't even afraid of the dark, know exactly what to do if he ever saw a flash of light brighter than the sun? And why did the grown-ups who taught him believe it might help?

And the third. Why, last thing at night, would a careful mother roll up a little note, tuck it into an empty glass bottle, and leave it outside her front door all night long, where anyone at all could take it?

I'll answer each of them in its own time. The first a little before dinner is over. The second when the children are going to bed. And the third when the whole house is asleep. There's no hurry. We have the whole night.

Now, before we go up the path to that front door, I want to be clear about something, because it matters on this channel. The family you're about to meet is invented. Their names, their ages, their little habits and quarrels and kindnesses, I have made up. But almost everything around them is real. The town is real, and you can drive through it today. The houses were built exactly as I'll describe. The television programmes, the advertisements, the films the children watched at school, the laws and the court rulings and the census figures, those are real, and I'll tell you the names and dates as we go, so you can look them up yourself, one quiet afternoon, if you ever want to.

So. Let's meet them.

The boy on the bicycle is Michael. He's seven years old, in the second grade, and he has been out since school let out at a little after three, which nobody thinks is strange at all. He's been at the edge of a half-built ball field, and in a friend's yard, and riding up and down streets with names he knows by heart. Nobody knows exactly where he's been. Nobody expects to. The rule in this house, as in most of the houses on this street, is very simple. When it gets dark, you come home.

Here's a small question to begin with, one we'll answer before this part is done. Why does this whole street look so much alike? Why are the houses so similar, the trees so young, the families so similar in age?

Imagine, now, that you are standing on the sidewalk as Michael drops his bicycle on the frozen lawn, wheels still spinning, and runs up the short concrete path. You can smell coal smoke, no, not coal, oil smoke, faint and sweetish, from a hundred furnace flues. You can smell the cold. The front window of the house glows yellow. And through it, you can see a living room, small and neat, with a sofa, a lamp with a fringed shade, and in the wall, set right into the wall itself the way a fireplace might be, the grey glass face of a television set.

Michael doesn't bother with the front door. Hardly anyone does. He goes around the side, past the milk box, to the kitchen door, and in he goes, and the door bangs behind him, and his mother's voice calls out from inside, "Shoes!"

His mother is Dorothy. Everyone calls her Dot. She's thirty, born in Brooklyn, the daughter of a trolley conductor, and she moved out here with her husband in the spring of 1950, with one baby and another on the way. She's standing at the kitchen counter in a cotton house dress and a cardigan, with an apron over the top, and her hair is pinned back, and there is a cigarette burning in a glass ashtray by the sink.

At the kitchen table, doing her arithmetic homework with her tongue between her teeth, is Carol, who is ten, in the fifth grade, and who considers herself practically grown. And on the linoleum floor, lining up wooden blocks in a long road and driving a small tin car along it, is Linda, who is four. Linda was one of the most popular names for baby girls in all of America in the years around her birth, and so there are, at this moment, two other Lindas on this very street.

And then there's the dog, a scruffy black-and-white mutt called Pepper, who came from a litter in a garage three streets over and now sleeps wherever he pleases.

The father of the house is not home yet. His name is Frank. He's thirty-three, and in a little while we'll meet him on his drive home from work.

Five people. One dog. Two bedrooms, more or less, with a third tucked under the roof. One television set, built into the wall.

Now, that small question. Why does this whole street look alike?

Because almost all of it was built at once, by one company, in a few astonishing years.

Only eight years before our evening, this land was farmland, open fields with a scattering of buildings, in an area that was known as Island Trees. After the Second World War, millions of young American servicemen came home, married, started families, and found that there was almost nowhere for them to live. Couples crowded into their parents' apartments, into spare rooms and rented corners, into whatever they could find. And a family building firm called Levitt and Sons, run by a father, Abraham Levitt, and his two sons, William and Alfred, decided to build houses for them the way Henry Ford had built cars. On an assembly line. Except that here, the houses stood still, and the workers moved.

Crews went down the streets in teams, each team doing one job and one job only. One team poured the concrete slabs. The next set up the framing. The next did the roofs, the next the plumbing, the next the painting, house after house after house. At the peak, by one well-known account, a new house was being finished roughly every two hours. The first families moved in on the first of October, 1947. They were about three hundred families, every one of them headed by a former serviceman, moving into brand-new little houses in the Cape Cod style. In 1948, the name Island Trees was dropped, and the place became Levittown. And by 1951, when the last house was finished, there were seventeen thousand four hundred and forty-seven of them.

That's the answer to our small question. The street looks alike because it was born all at once, from one set of plans, in a single burst of building, for a single generation of young families who had all come home from the same war.

There's a lovely detail about how it became possible, and it comes from a real public record. In the 1940s, the building rules of the Town of Hempstead, which covered this area, required houses to have basements. Digging basements took time and cost money, and the Levitts wanted to build on flat concrete slabs instead, poured straight onto the ground. So on the twenty-seventh of May, 1947, at a meeting of the Hempstead town board, the first item on the agenda was a request to repeal the section of the building code that required cellars. It was the section numbered 809. The board agreed. And that small vote, at a town meeting on Long Island, is part of the reason that tens of thousands of children grew up in houses with no basement at all, and with something very unusual under their feet, which we'll come back to later tonight.

The Novak family, because that's the name I've given them, live in one of the later houses, the kind called a ranch, which Levitt and Sons introduced in 1949 to replace the first Cape Cods. These were a little bigger, about twenty-five feet by thirty-two, and they came with things that most Americans in the late 1940s had only seen in magazines. A refrigerator. An electric range. A Bendix washing machine, in the kitchen. And in many of them, an Admiral television set, built right into the living-room wall. The price was seven thousand nine hundred and ninety dollars, and Levitt's advertisements called it the housing buy of the century. For a veteran, the monthly payments could be lower than the rent on a city apartment.

Let's set that beside a modern home for a moment. Today, if you bought a new house, you would probably expect a kitchen with appliances, of course. You'd expect central heating, and wiring for the internet, and maybe a place to charge a car. You'd be startled if the builder threw in a television and fixed it into the wall. But in 1950, for a family who had spent the war years in a cold-water flat or a barracks, walking into a brand-new house with a washing machine and a television already installed must have felt almost like walking into the future.

And now, our first place where historians disagree, and it's a big one, because it's about the whole decade.

To many people, then and now, the 1950s were a golden age. Wages were rising. Jobs were plentiful. A man with a high-school education could often support a whole family on one income, buy a house, buy a car, and expect his children to do better still. Families stayed together. Neighbourhoods were safe. In this view, places like Levittown were the American dream made real, and much of what came afterwards was a falling away.

But in 1992, the family historian Stephanie Coontz published a book called The Way We Never Were, with the subtitle American Families and the Nostalgia Trap. I'll paraphrase her argument rather than quote it. She argued that the 1950s family we remember is, to a large degree, a myth, assembled partly from television, and that real families of the period were often poorer, more anxious, more conflicted and more varied than the picture suggests. She pointed out, too, that the decade was historically unusual, not typical, and that much of its prosperity rested on special conditions and on large amounts of government support for young families. Others have pushed back, and argued that something real and valuable was lost in the decades that followed. The debate goes on, at dinner tables as well as in universities. Tonight, I'll try to steer between the two, and show you this house as it might really have been: not a sitcom, and not a nightmare. Just a family, on a Monday.

Back in the kitchen, Dot has taken the bottle of milk out of the refrigerator and is pouring Linda a glass. It's a heavy glass quart bottle, with the name of a local dairy printed on its side, and a round cardboard cap that you pry out with your thumbnail. The milk came this morning, in the dark, before anyone was up. It will come again tomorrow morning, in the dark, before anyone is up. And the two empty bottles out in the metal box on the stoop are waiting for it.

Outside, the last of the sunset is fading. According to a modern astronomical calculation, the sun went down over Levittown that evening at about one minute past five. There'll be no moon tonight. It was almost exactly new. So when the dark comes, it will be a deep winter dark, broken only by the streetlights, and the glowing windows, and the faint blue flicker of television screens.

And far off, on the main road, a stream of headlights is beginning to come home.

## Part Two — Around Five O'Clock: The Drive Home

Here's the small question for this part of the evening. Imagine you are about to ride home in Frank's car. What is the one thing you would reach for, without even thinking, the moment you sat down? And why isn't it there?

Hold on to that. We're going a few miles away, to the place where Frank works.

He's thirty-three years old. In the war he was in the Navy, a machinist's mate on a supply ship in the Pacific, and when he came home in 1946 he married Dot, whom he'd known since they were both fifteen, and went to work. Now he works at an aircraft plant in Bethpage, a few miles from Levittown, where the Grumman company builds planes for the Navy. Thousands of men from Levittown and the towns around it worked in plants like that in the 1950s, building aircraft for a country that had only just finished one war and was now living in the long, cold shadow of another. Frank runs a machine that cuts and shapes metal parts. He's good at it. He doesn't talk about it much.

It's five o'clock, and the shift is over. The whistle has gone, and the men pour out into the cold, under the floodlights, pulling on their jackets and lighting cigarettes, hundreds of small orange points of light blooming in the dusk like fireflies. Frank lights one too. He's smoked since he was sixteen. Nearly everyone he knows smokes. In the Navy, cigarettes came cheap, and sometimes free, and a cigarette was the first thing you reached for when the work was over, and it still is.

Imagine you are walking beside him now, across the frozen car park, your breath steaming. The cars are big, rounded, heavy things, in dark greens and blues and creams, with chrome bumpers glittering under the lights. Frank's is a 1950 Ford sedan, dark green, bought second-hand, with a dent in the back fender that Michael is not supposed to know about. He opens the passenger door for you. The door is heavy, and it swings wide. And you slide onto the seat, which is one long bench of cloth and vinyl running the whole width of the car, and you reach, without even thinking about it, over your shoulder.

And there's nothing there.

That's the answer to our small question. You reach for the seat belt. And there isn't one. There's no belt across your lap, no strap over your shoulder, no little chime reminding you to buckle up. There's just the bench, the big steering wheel, a metal dashboard with a radio and an ashtray in it, and the windshield. Frank climbs in, sets his cigarette between his lips, and starts the engine.

Now, why isn't there a seat belt?

Not because nobody had thought of one. Seat belts had existed for a long time, in aeroplanes and racing cars. And in fact, a few years before our evening, an American carmaker called Nash had become one of the first to offer lap belts as an option in an ordinary family car. It was around 1949 or 1950; sources differ on the exact model year. Nash installed belts in about forty thousand cars. And the story goes that when the company checked a year later, only about a thousand of those customers were actually using them. Many people disliked them, and some felt that offering seat belts at all suggested the car was unsafe. So Nash quietly dropped the idea.

In this very year, 1955, Ford would begin to offer seat belts too, but only as an option, installed by the dealer if you asked and paid for it. The next year, 1956, Ford made a whole advertising campaign out of safety, with a package it called Lifeguard: lap belts, a padded dashboard, a deep-dish steering wheel and stronger door locks. By most accounts, it was a slow seller. Safety, it turned out, was much harder to advertise than horsepower. Also in 1955, a bill was introduced in the United States House of Representatives that would have required seat belts in vehicles used for interstate travel. It didn't pass. For most American families, in most cars, on most roads, the seat belt was still years away.

So Frank drives home the way almost everybody drives home, with nothing between him and the windshield but the steering wheel and his own good sense. If he had the children with him, they'd be bouncing on the back seat, or standing up on the front seat beside him, holding on to the dashboard to see better. If Dot were holding Linda, she'd hold her in her lap. That was how families travelled. Nobody thought twice about it.

Let's set that beside your own car for a moment. A modern car is full of quiet machines for keeping you alive: belts that tighten in a crash, airbags folded away in the wheel and the dashboard and the doors, crumple zones designed to fold like an accordion, sensors that watch the road, child seats bolted firmly to the frame. Frank's Ford has a heater, a radio, and an ashtray. And the ashtray, I think, gets more use than anything else in the car.

The drive home is short, along wide, straight roads past open fields and new shopping centres and long rows of houses, with headlights swinging around him in the dark. The radio is playing something by Perry Como.

And if you look closely at that radio, at the dial with its row of numbers glowing faintly orange, you'll notice two tiny triangles printed among the numbers. One sits at 640. The other at 1240. Every radio made for sale in America from about 1953 onwards, home radios and car radios alike, had to carry those two little marks. They belonged to a system called CONELRAD, short for Control of Electromagnetic Radiation, which had been set up in 1951. The idea was that if enemy bombers were ever on their way, ordinary radio stations would go silent, so that the bombers couldn't use their signals to find the cities. And then a few stations would come back on, taking turns, on just those two frequencies, to tell people what to do. The triangles were there so that a frightened driver, or a mother in a kitchen, could find the right spot on the dial in a hurry. Frank has never once tuned to them. He hardly notices they're there. They're just part of the dial, like the numbers. That's how deeply the Cold War had settled into ordinary life by 1955. It was printed on the radio.

Frank's whole life here was made possible by another government programme, a kinder one. In 1944, before the war had even ended, Congress passed the Servicemen's Readjustment Act, which everyone called the GI Bill. Among many other things, it offered returning veterans home loans backed by the government, often with little or nothing to pay down. Without it, Frank, a machinist's mate with a few hundred dollars in the bank, could never have bought a house at twenty-eight. With it, he did, and so did millions of others. It's one of the reasons the suburbs grew so fast, and one of the reasons historians like Coontz point out that the self-reliant young family of the 1950s was, in fact, standing on a great deal of public help. And as Frank turns off the main road and into the curving streets of Levittown, the houses come into view, rank after rank of them, lit windows glowing gold, and in some of them, the cool blue shimmer of a television set already on.

Now I want to tell you something about these streets, gently, and honestly, because it's true and it matters, and because this channel would not be honest if it left it out.

Every family Frank passes on his way home is white. Every single one. That was not an accident.

When Levitt and Sons rented and sold its first houses here, the paperwork included a written rule about who could live in them. In the lease that the first families signed, there was a clause, Clause 25, which said, and I'll give you only the essential phrase, that the house could not be used or occupied by anyone other than "members of the Caucasian race." One account notes that the company set this out in capital letters and bold type. Rules like this were called racial covenants, and they were common across America at the time, in deeds and leases in cities and suburbs alike.

Then, in 1948, the year after the first families arrived, the United States Supreme Court decided a case called Shelley versus Kraemer. The court ruled that racially restrictive covenants like these could no longer be enforced by the courts. And so Levitt and Sons took the written clause out. But the practice didn't change. In 1949 the company said, in effect, that it kept the right to decide who it would sell or rent to. And it went on, quietly, turning away Black families who wanted to buy.

William Levitt himself spoke about it in an interview in 1954, the year before our evening, and I'll paraphrase what he said, because I haven't been able to check his exact words. He said, roughly, that most white people preferred not to live in mixed communities, and that this attitude might well be wrong morally, but that it was not his job as a builder to change it. And the company argued, as many builders did, that integration would lower the value of the houses.

Here we reach a place where the records, and the historians, don't quite agree, at least about the numbers. According to one source drawing on the census of 1960, five years after our evening, Levittown had sixty-five thousand two hundred and seventy-six residents, and fifty-seven of them were Black. Less than one person in a thousand. But the historian Kenneth Jackson, in his landmark history of the American suburb, Crabgrass Frontier, published in 1985, put the population of Levittown in 1960 at eighty-two thousand, and wrote that not one of them was Black. The difference seems to come partly from where you draw the town's edges, and partly from how the figures were counted and later repeated. Either way, the numbers come to almost exactly the same thing. Nearly no one.

And there's a deeper question that historians still discuss. Who was responsible? Levitt, in his own telling, was only following what his buyers wanted. Many historians, Jackson among them, have pointed out that the builders, the banks, and the federal agencies that insured the mortgages all helped to shape who could live in the new suburbs, and that the result, in place after place across the country, was a pattern of separation that lasted for generations. Because these houses were how millions of ordinary families built up their savings, as the houses rose in value year after year, the families who were shut out of them were shut out of that too.

I don't tell you this to spoil the evening. Frank and Dot never thought about it much, I suspect, which is part of what makes it worth remembering. It was simply one of the things that was normal here, in 1955, and it is one of the reasons we should be careful when anyone tells us that a decade was simply golden. The light was real. So were the shadows.

And here is the gentler side of the same story, because there is one. In later decades, Levittown changed, as America did. The covenants are long gone, and today it's a much more varied place than it was then, though historians and residents still talk about how slowly that change came. The houses, many of them extended and remodelled almost beyond recognition, are still lived in, by families of every background, and the young maple trees on Frank's street have grown tall enough to meet over the road.

Frank pulls into the short driveway beside his house. The Ford's headlights sweep across the front lawn, and the white milk box on the stoop, and Michael's bicycle lying on the grass where it was dropped.

He sighs. He turns off the engine, and the car ticks and creaks as it begins to cool. He sits for a moment in the dark, finishing his cigarette, the way men often did, a small private moment between work and home. Then he grinds it out in the dashboard ashtray, picks up his lunch pail, a black metal box with a domed lid for the Thermos flask, and climbs out into the cold.

On the way to the kitchen door, he stops, as he does every evening, and picks up Michael's bicycle, and wheels it around the side of the house, and leans it against the wall. He doesn't lock it. Nobody on this street locks their bicycles. Most people don't lock their front doors either, not in the daytime, and many not at night. Whether the streets were truly as safe as people remember is a harder question than it sounds, and the honest answer is that it varied a great deal from place to place. But on this street, in this winter, it is simply how things are done.

He lifts the lid of the milk box, out of habit, and glances in. The two empty bottles are there, rinsed and upside down. He lets the lid fall shut with a small metal clank.

And then he opens the kitchen door, into the light and the warmth and the smell of cooking, and the noise of a dog going wild with joy, and a four-year-old shouting "Daddy!" at the top of her voice, and he is home.

## Part Three — Around a Quarter to Six: The Warm Floor and the Aluminium Tray

Here's this hour's small question, and it's one that Linda could answer, though she couldn't tell you why. On a freezing January evening, why is the kitchen floor of this house warm under a four-year-old's bare feet?

Because it is. Linda has kicked off her slippers, as she always does, and she's padding across the linoleum in her socks, and the floor beneath her is gently, evenly warm, like a stone that has been lying in the sun. There are no radiators anywhere in this house. There's no grate, no fireplace, no vent blowing hot air. And yet the whole house is warm.

Imagine you are standing in this kitchen now, in your own socks, and you've just come in from the cold. It's a small room, bright under a ceiling light, with cabinets painted white, a counter, a sink under a window that looks out onto the dark back yard, and appliances in the gleaming white enamel that every new kitchen had in those years. A refrigerator with rounded corners and a big chrome handle. An electric range. And in one corner, the thing that Dot is proudest of in the whole house, the washing machine, a Bendix, which came with the house. It's Monday, and in many American homes, Monday was wash day, as it had been for generations, and the Bendix has been churning and thumping for half the afternoon.

You notice the floor. You look down. You crouch, and put your palm flat against it. Warm.

That's our small question, and here's the answer. Underneath your feet, under the linoleum and the concrete, there are pipes. When Levitt and Sons poured the four-inch concrete slab that each of these houses stands on, they first laid out soft copper tubing on the ground, running back and forth in long loops, like the coils at the back of a refrigerator. Then they poured the concrete right over it. And in a small utility room off the kitchen, an oil-fired boiler heats water and pumps it through those buried coils, so that the whole floor of the house becomes one large, gentle radiator. It's called radiant heating, and in 1955, in an ordinary family house, it was quite remarkable. People who visited from the city were often astonished by it.

Why did Levitt use it? Not for luxury. A heating engineer and writer named Dan Holohan, who has written about these houses for years, says that Levitt chose radiant heating because it was the cheapest system he could find. No ducts, no radiators, no basement for a furnace. The pipes were simply cast into the floor that he had to pour anyway.

There was a catch, though, and it's one of those gentle, true footnotes to the story of these houses. There was no insulation under the slab, or at its edges, and no barrier to stop moisture rising from the ground. And the copper tubing, buried in the damp concrete, tended to corrode. Over the years, small leaks could seep away into the soil without anyone noticing. Many owners gave up on their floor heating after fifteen or twenty years and put in baseboard radiators instead. But tonight, in 1955, the system is only five years old, and the floor is warm, and Linda is padding across it happily in her socks, and Pepper the dog is lying stretched out flat on it with his eyes closed, like a sunbather.

Let's set that beside a modern home. Today, underfloor heating is often thought of as a luxury, something you might put in a smart new bathroom, with electric mats or hot-water pipes laid under the tiles. In 1955, here, it was the cheap option, built into the whole house, for a working family. It's one of those odd little reversals that history is full of.

Now, what's for dinner?

Dot opens the freezer compartment at the top of the refrigerator, a small, frosty box hardly bigger than a shoebox laid on its side, with a crust of ice around its little door. And she takes out three flat cardboard boxes. On the front of each box, printed in bright colours, is a picture of a television set, and on the screen of the television set is a picture of a dinner. Turkey, gravy, peas, and a sweet orange mound of sweet potatoes, each in its own neat compartment. It's a Swanson TV dinner.

Tonight, because it's wash day, and because she's tired, and because Michael begged, there will be TV dinners for the children and for Frank. Dot will have the leftover pot roast from yesterday. She thinks the TV dinners are a little bit of a cheat, and she half suspects her own mother in Brooklyn would be scandalised by them. But they're also, to her, slightly thrilling. They are modern. They are the future, in a box.

It's worth pausing on that little freezer box for a moment, because it's newer than it looks. Freezing food quickly, so that it kept its taste and texture, was an idea that had been made practical only about thirty years earlier, in the 1920s, largely by an inventor named Clarence Birdseye, who had watched people in Labrador, in the far north-east of Canada, freeze fish in the bitter wind and noticed that, cooked months later, it tasted almost fresh. His name is still on packets of frozen peas today. But for a long time, frozen food was a novelty, because so few homes had anywhere cold enough to keep it. Dot's mother, in Brooklyn, still had an icebox in the 1930s, a wooden cabinet lined with metal, kept cool by a block of ice that the iceman carried up the stairs on his shoulder, with a pair of tongs. Now, in her daughter's kitchen, there's a refrigerator with its own small freezer, humming quietly to itself. The iceman is almost gone. The milkman, as we'll see, is still very much here.

Inside each box there's an aluminium tray, divided into three compartments, covered with foil. You put the tray straight into the oven, at a high heat, for about twenty-five minutes. No pots. No pans. No washing up. When it's done, you peel back the foil, and there it is, steaming. In 1955, that felt very much like magic.

The TV dinner was brand new. The Swanson company, of Omaha, Nebraska, began selling its frozen turkey dinners in 1953, a little over a year before our evening. They cost ninety-eight cents each. Swanson had expected to sell perhaps five thousand of them in the first year. By the Smithsonian's account, in 1954, the first full year of production, they sold ten million.

And here we come to our disagreement, and it's a cheerful one. Who invented the TV dinner?

For many years, the credit went to a Swanson salesman named Gerry Thomas. His story, which he told many times, and which varied a little in the telling, was that in 1953 the company found itself with a huge surplus of frozen turkey left over after Thanksgiving, so much that, in the best-known version, it had to be kept in refrigerated railway cars that went back and forth across the country because there was nowhere else to put it. Thomas said that he saw a metal tray of the kind used for airline meals, and had the idea of putting turkey and the trimmings into trays like that, and freezing them, and selling them, and calling them TV dinners, so that people could eat them in front of the new television sets that were spreading so fast. It's a wonderful story, and it's the one most people know.

But it's disputed. The family of Clarke Swanson, one of the two brothers who ran the company, came to argue that the idea was really the brothers' own. ## Part Four — Around a Quarter Past Six: The Doctor in the Magazine

Here is the small question for dinner time. Look at the kitchen table. Count the things on it. There are five trays and plates, five glasses, the milk bottle, the salt and pepper. What's the one other object, sitting right there among the food, that you would almost never see on a family dinner table today?

Imagine you're sitting at the table with them now, squeezed in at the corner between Michael and Carol. The kitchen is warm, from the floor and from the oven, and the window over the sink has fogged over so that the dark yard outside is just a blur. That's the answer to our small question. The ashtray.

In 1955, ashtrays were everywhere. On kitchen tables and coffee tables and bedside tables, on office desks, on the arms of cinema seats, in the dashboards of cars, on trains, in hospital waiting rooms, in doctors' surgeries. People gave ashtrays as wedding presents. Children made them in school out of clay and gave them to their parents at Christmas, painted in bright colours, and their parents used them. Smoking was not something you did outside, or apart, or with an apology. It was something you did everywhere, with everyone, all day long.

Frank glances at it and smiles. "That's my brand," he says. And he goes back to his newspaper.

So here, at last, is the first puzzle I gave you at the beginning of the evening. Why would a smiling doctor in a white coat be recommending a brand of cigarettes from the pages of a magazine on the family's kitchen table, and why would almost nobody in the house find that odd?

Let's start with the advertisement itself, because it's real, and it has a real history.

In 1946, the year Frank came home from the Navy, the R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Company, which made Camel cigarettes, began an advertising campaign built around a single, simple claim: More doctors smoke Camels than any other cigarette. It became the mainstay of the company's advertising for about the next six years. It ran in magazines like Life, and Time, and The Saturday Evening Post, in medical journals that doctors themselves read, and on many of the most popular radio programmes in the country. The magazine Carol is holding is a few years old, from that time. And according to the advertisements, this claim was not just a slogan. It was the result of a survey. The ads said that 113,597 doctors, from every branch of medicine, had been asked which cigarette they smoked, and that the answer that came up most often was Camels.

Now, how was that survey actually done?

In 2006, two historians of medicine, Martha Gardner and Allan Brandt, published a study of these advertisements in the American Journal of Public Health. Their article took its title from another Camel slogan of the time: The Doctors' Choice Is America's Choice. And what they found was this. The survey that the ads called independent was actually carried out by Reynolds's own advertising agency, the William Esty Company. Its staff questioned doctors at medical conventions and in their offices. And according to Gardner and Brandt's account, many of the doctors were asked which brand they preferred just after they had been handed free cartons of Camels.

So that's one half of our answer. The doctor in the magazine was there because a tobacco company had paid to put him there, and had arranged a survey that was almost certain to produce the result it wanted.

But that's not quite the whole answer, because it doesn't explain why the advertisement worked. Why would a doctor make anyone feel better about smoking?

Because, in the 1940s and early 1950s, smoking was simply part of ordinary life, for doctors as much as for anyone else. Many doctors did smoke. And there was a real worry, in the public mind, about whether cigarettes might irritate the throat, or cause a cough. A doctor's face in a cigarette advertisement was a reassurance. It said, in effect: the people who know the most about health smoke this brand. It must be fine. It might even be the gentlest one.

And now we reach the second half of the answer, the one that matters most. Why does nobody in this house find it odd?

Because in January 1955, the evidence that cigarettes caused serious disease was only just beginning to reach ordinary people, and many of them didn't believe it.

In May of 1950, the Journal of the American Medical Association published a study by Ernst Wynder, then a young researcher, and the surgeon Evarts Graham, based on six hundred and eighty-four proven cases of lung cancer. They concluded, and this is their own wording, that "excessive and prolonged use of tobacco, especially cigarettes, seems to be an important factor in the induction of bronchiogenic carcinoma." That is, in the development of lung cancer. A few months later, on the thirtieth of September, 1950, in Britain, the British Medical Journal published a study by two researchers, Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill, who had set out in 1948 without expecting cigarettes to be the main culprit. They reached the conclusion, again in their words, "that smoking is a factor, and an important factor, in the production of carcinoma of the lung."

That was five years before our evening. The news did spread. A Gallup poll in January 1954 found that the great majority of Americans had heard or read that smoking might cause lung cancer. But when Gallup asked people in 1954 whether they themselves thought cigarette smoking was one of the causes of lung cancer, only forty-one per cent said yes. Thirty-one per cent said no. And twenty-six per cent said they didn't know.

The tobacco companies, for their part, had answered the new research in a very public way. On the fourth of January, 1954, a year before our evening, a group of the largest American tobacco firms published a full-page advertisement in hundreds of newspapers across the country. It was called A Frank Statement to Cigarette Smokers. I'll paraphrase it, rather than quote it, because I haven't been able to check its exact wording for myself. It said, in essence, that the companies took the health of smokers seriously, that the research linking cigarettes and cancer was not conclusive, that there was no proof cigarette smoking was one of the causes, and that the industry would fund research of its own. Historians, Allan Brandt among them, have since described that advertisement as the beginning of a long, deliberate campaign to keep the public uncertain. But in 1954, to a man like Frank, reading his newspaper over breakfast, it would simply have sounded reasonable. The experts disagreed. The companies said they were looking into it. Why worry?

And there were smaller things, too, all around the children, that made smoking look as natural as breathing. In the corner shop down by the shopping centre, Michael can buy candy cigarettes, little white sticks of sugar with a pink tip, in boxes made to look like real cigarette packets, and he and his friends hold them between two fingers and pretend to blow smoke on cold mornings, when their breath makes it look real. In the films at the cinema on Saturday afternoons, the heroes smoke. The cowboys smoke. On the television, which we'll come to shortly, the stars of some of the most popular programmes in the country light up on screen, and many programmes are paid for by tobacco companies, whose names are spoken at the beginning and the end. Lucy and Desi, whom we'll meet later tonight, were sponsored for years by a cigarette brand, and they were shown smoking its cigarettes in the advertisements.

So in a house like this one, in 1955, the doctor in the magazine was not a joke, or a scandal. He was simply a doctor. And the question of whether cigarettes were really dangerous was, for most people, still an open one. Something you'd heard about in the newspaper, and then forgotten, and then lit another cigarette.

And here is where the scientists disagreed, sharply, at the time. One of the most famous statisticians in the world, the British scientist Ronald Fisher, refused to accept that the studies proved smoking caused cancer. In two letters to the journal Nature in the summer of 1958, three years after our evening, he argued that the link might be explained by something else entirely. Perhaps, he suggested, some people were born with a constitution, a genetic make-up, that made them both more likely to enjoy smoking and more likely to develop lung cancer. In that case, he said, smoking and cancer would go together without one causing the other. It was a clever argument, and as a point about the logic of statistics, it's still taught today. But other scientists replied that the evidence didn't support it, and as study after study piled up through the 1950s and 1960s, the scientific world came to agree with Doll, Hill, Wynder and Graham. It has also been reported that Fisher did consulting work for the tobacco industry, which he said had no effect on his views. I mention that only as a reported claim, because I haven't been able to confirm it from a primary source.

Let's set all of this beside our own world. Today, in many countries, cigarette packets carry large printed warnings, sometimes with photographs. Smoking has been banned in most restaurants, offices, trains, aeroplanes and hospitals. Advertising cigarettes on television is long gone. And a doctor who appeared in a cigarette advertisement would lose his licence, and probably his career. In Frank's kitchen, the doctor in the magazine is smiling, and Frank is smiling back, and the smoke from his cigarette is drifting gently up towards the kitchen light.

I want to say something gently here. Frank isn't foolish. Dot isn't careless. They are doing what nearly everyone around them is doing, what their doctors do, what their parents did, what the films and the magazines and the radio all show them. They're smoking because, in 1955, it is completely and utterly normal. It's one of the strangest things about this whole decade, to our eyes. And it's one of the most important reminders that ordinary people, in any age, can only act on what they know.

Carol has cut out the doctor, very neatly, with her round-ended scissors, and laid him in her scrapbook under the heading Modern Life. Frank has finished his cigarette and stubbed it out in the big glass ashtray. Dot is clearing away the aluminium trays, which go straight into the rubbish, because that's the whole point of them, and nobody is quite sure what else you'd do with them anyway.

Michael is asking whether they can watch television now.

Dot looks at the clock on the kitchen wall, a round electric clock with a red second hand that sweeps smoothly around without ticking. It's just after half past six.

"Homework first," she says. And Michael groans, the long, heartfelt groan of a seven-year-old boy who has been asked to do something completely unreasonable.

Inside the cabinet there are no microchips, no circuit boards of the kind we know. There are vacuum tubes, a whole row of them, glass bulbs about the size of your thumb, with glowing filaments inside, like little light bulbs. They have to heat up before they work. And the picture itself is made by a much bigger tube, the picture tube, which fires a beam of electrons at the inside of the glass screen and sweeps it back and forth, line by line, five hundred and twenty-five lines to each picture, thirty pictures every second, painting the image out of light. It all takes a little while to get going. In the 1950s, everyone who watched television knew the small ritual of turning it on and waiting. And everyone knew, too, the way the picture shrank down to a single bright dot in the middle of the screen when you turned it off, and then slowly faded away.

Frank turns the big channel knob, and it clunks round with a heavy, satisfying click from one number to the next. In the New York area, in 1955, a family like this one could pick up a handful of stations. Let's set that beside our own world. Today, you might have a television the size of a dining table, a screen as thin as a picture frame, hundreds of channels, and thousands of films and programmes waiting to be streamed at any hour, on demand. You probably carry a screen in your pocket that is clearer than anything Frank could imagine. And you'd never wait for any of them to warm up. But in Frank's living room, the little grey picture in the wall is the newest, most astonishing thing in the house, and everyone sits facing it, together, in the same room, watching the same thing at the same time. If you wanted to see a programme, you had to be there, in front of the set, when it was on. Or you missed it. Perhaps forever.

On the screen now is a western, a cowboy on a white horse riding across a dusty landscape that, in shades of grey, could be anywhere. Michael is transfixed. He knows the cowboy's name, and the horse's name, and which hat the cowboy wears, and he has a toy gun and holster set of his own, in his bedroom, that he got for Christmas, along with a cowboy hat. Half the boys on the street have the same one.

It is hard to overstate how quickly television arrived in homes like this. In 1950, when the Novaks moved in, only a small share of American households had a set at all. Most sources put it at around nine per cent, though one standard encyclopedia gives a higher figure, just under twenty per cent, which shows how hard it is to measure something that was changing so fast. By 1955, it was something like two-thirds of all households. The standard historical statistics for the United States count about five million households with a television in 1950, and more than thirty million in 1955. In five years, a machine that had been a rare wonder became something that most families owned. And by 1960, nearly nine in every ten households would have one. Few inventions in history have spread so fast.

There's a famous evening that shows how big television had become, and it happened just two years before ours. On Monday, the nineteenth of January, 1953, the programme I Love Lucy, starring Lucille Ball and Desi Arnaz, broadcast an episode called Lucy Goes to the Hospital, in which Lucy Ricardo gives birth to a son. On that very same day, in real life, Lucille Ball had given birth to her own son. The programme was watched by an estimated forty-four million people. Two and a half months after that, in April 1953, a new little weekly magazine called TV Guide went on sale across the country for the first time, a pocket-sized booklet listing every programme on every channel, and the picture on the cover of its very first national issue was Lucille Ball's new baby son, Desi Arnaz Junior. In just a few short years, the whole shape of an American evening had changed.

I Love Lucy, by the way, is on tonight. It's Monday, and on Monday nights, at nine o'clock, on CBS, it was the most popular programme in America. And now, a place where writers and historians have disagreed, and it's about streets exactly like this one, and evenings exactly like this.

Look out of the front window, past the curtains, if you like. Up and down the street, in house after house, the windows are glowing that same faint, cool, flickering blue. In many of those living rooms, at this moment, families are sitting just as the Novaks are, watching the same few programmes on the same few channels. To some critics in the 1950s, that was a nightmare. The writer John Keats, not the poet, but an American journalist of the same name, published a scathing book in 1956 called The Crack in the Picture Window, attacking the new suburbs as soulless places of identical houses and identical lives. And a few years later, in 1961, the great urban historian Lewis Mumford, in his book The City in History, described the new suburbs as rows of uniform houses, lined up on uniform roads, inhabited by people of the same class, the same income and the same age, all living the same kind of life. I'm paraphrasing him there, but that was the image: a place where everyone was the same, and television made them even more so.

But in 1967, a sociologist named Herbert Gans published a book called The Levittowners. It wasn't about this Levittown, but about a later one that Levitt and Sons built in New Jersey. The people who wrote most fiercely about the suburbs often didn't live in them.

## Part Six — Around a Quarter to Eight: Comic Books, New Shoes and the Bones of Your Toes

Here's the small question for this part of the evening, and it's a strange one. Carol is ten years old. She has never broken a bone, never been to hospital, never had an X-ray at the doctor's. So how does she know exactly what the bones of her own toes look like?

We'll come to that. But first, Michael is back, running down the hall in his socks, skidding on the warm floor, with a comic book clutched to his chest like treasure.

It's new. He bought it on Saturday with his own money, ten cents, from the spinning wire rack at the drugstore in the shopping centre, and he's read it four times already. It's a cowboy comic, bright and thin, printed on cheap paper that smells faintly of ink, and on its cover, in the top right-hand corner, there's a small stamp, a little white shield-shaped box with a few words printed inside it. Michael doesn't know what the stamp means. He's never noticed it. But it's the newest thing about the comic, newer even than the story inside, and it has a story of its own.

Imagine you're sitting beside Michael on the rug as he opens it, and you look over his shoulder. The colours are flat and bold, red and yellow and blue, made of tiny dots. There are cowboys and horses and a stagecoach and a lot of gunsmoke. Nobody gets hurt very badly. The bad men are caught. The sheriff is wise. The hero tips his hat. It's all rather gentle, actually, for a cowboy story, and there's a reason for that.

Only a year or so before our evening, comic books had been at the centre of a national storm.

In 1954, a New York psychiatrist named Fredric Wertham published a book called Seduction of the Innocent. In it, he argued that comic books, especially the crime and horror comics that were hugely popular with children in those years, were harming the young people who read them. He claimed that they taught violence, cruelty and crime, that they were behind a great deal of juvenile delinquency, and that they were damaging children's minds. He drew on his own clinical work with troubled young people, and he described case after case of children who, he said, had been led astray by what they read. The book caused a sensation. That same spring, the United States Senate held hearings on comic books and juvenile delinquency, in New York, and Wertham was among those who testified. Parents' groups held meetings. In some towns, in those years, piles of children's comics were even gathered up and burned.

And the comic-book publishers, frightened of what the government might do, decided to police themselves before anyone else could. Late in 1954, an industry group set up something called the Comics Code Authority, with a long list of rules about what comics could and could not show. No excessive violence. Nothing gruesome. Nothing that might make crime look attractive, or authority look foolish. Respect for parents, for the police, for the law. Comics that followed the rules could carry a little seal of approval on the cover. Comics that didn't found that most shops wouldn't sell them. Several of the horror and crime comics simply disappeared. And the small white stamp on the corner of Michael's cowboy comic, the one he's never noticed, is that seal.

So, that's what that stamp is. A trace of a great argument, printed in the corner of a ten-cent comic.

And here we come to our disagreement, and it's a remarkable one, because it was settled, in a sense, nearly sixty years later.

For decades, Wertham's book was taken seriously by many people, and argued over by many others. But in 2010, the Library of Congress opened Wertham's private papers to researchers, including his notes on the children he'd seen in his clinic. And a scholar of information and libraries at the University of Illinois, Carol Tilley, went through them, and compared them, case by case, with what Wertham had written in Seduction of the Innocent. In 2012, she published an article in the journal Information and Culture setting out what she'd found. Many of the quotations in his book, she showed, didn't match his own notes. In some cases he had changed the children's ages. In others he had altered what they had said, or combined the stories of different children, or left out other troubles in their lives that might have explained their behaviour far better than the comics did. In one example she described, a boy whom Wertham presented as harmed by comics had in fact been through terrible experiences that Wertham didn't mention, and had told him that his favourite comic was Superman. Tilley's conclusion was that Wertham had manipulated and in places invented his evidence to support the argument he wanted to make.

Not everyone who worried about comics in the 1950s was wrong to wonder what children were reading, of course. And Wertham, in other parts of his career, did serious and admirable work. But his famous book, it turns out, was built in part on evidence that he had reshaped. Which is a good reminder, on a channel like this one, that even a frightening book by a respected expert deserves to be checked against the sources.

Michael, of course, knows nothing about any of this. He is reading his comic for the fifth time, lying on his stomach on the warm floor, with his chin in his hands and his feet waving in the air.

Now, to our small question. How does Carol know what the bones of her toes look like?

On Saturday, the same day Michael bought his comic, Dot took Carol to the shoe store in the shopping centre, because Carol's feet had grown again, as they seem to do every few months, and her school shoes were pinching. And in the shoe store, in among the chairs and the shelves of boxes and the little slanted footstools, there was a machine.

Imagine you are standing in that shoe store now, beside Carol. The machine is about the height of a grown man's chest, a handsome wooden cabinet, polished, with a curved front and a little step at the bottom. At the foot of the cabinet there's an opening, where you slide your feet in, wearing your new shoes. And on top, there are viewing windows, like the eyepieces of a microscope. Often there were three of them. One for the child, one for the parent, and one for the salesman.

Carol puts on the new shoes, black patent leather with a strap, and steps up, and slides her feet into the opening. The salesman presses a switch. There's a faint hum. And Carol leans forward, and puts her eyes to the viewer.

And there, glowing a soft greenish colour, in the dark, she sees her own feet. Not the skin. Not the socks. The bones. Every small bone of every toe, delicate and pale, like the branches of a little tree, and around them, the dark outline of the shoe. She wiggles her toes. The bones move. She can see exactly how much space there is at the end of the shoe, which is the whole point. She thinks it's the most wonderful thing she has ever seen. And so do Michael and his friends, who are known to go into the shoe store just to look at their feet, when nobody is watching.

That's the answer to our small question. Carol knows what the bones of her toes look like because she has seen them, glowing, in an X-ray machine at the shoe store.

These machines were called shoe-fitting fluoroscopes. A fluoroscope is a device that sends a continuous beam of X-rays through something, in this case your feet, onto a special screen that glows where the rays strike it, so that you can watch a live, moving X-ray picture. They had been used in shoe shops since the 1920s, and they reached the height of their popularity in the early 1950s, right around the time of our evening. According to the Smithsonian, there were something like ten thousand of them in use in shoe stores across the United States. Children loved them. Parents trusted them, because they seemed so scientific, so modern, so careful. A shoe that fitted properly, as anyone could see on the glowing screen, was a shoe that wouldn't harm a growing foot.

What nobody in the shop was thinking about, on Saturday, was the radiation.

X-rays, as we now know very well, and as doctors were already beginning to understand in the 1950s, can damage living tissue. The machines in shoe shops were often poorly shielded, and the dose they delivered varied from one machine to another. Estimates of exactly how much radiation a child received in one of these fittings vary a great deal between sources, and I'd rather not give you a number that might not be right. But the salesmen who stood beside them all day, and anyone standing nearby, were exposed too. And children, who often went back to look again and again, could receive far more than a single fitting.

The tide turned in the years just after our evening. In 1957, the state of Pennsylvania became the first in the country to ban shoe-fitting fluoroscopes. Other states followed, and insurers began to refuse to cover shops that used them. By around 1960, they had largely vanished from American stores. By 1970, some thirty-three states had banned them outright. There was never a single nationwide ban, but there didn't need to be. It is one of those quiet historical endings: a machine that everyone loved, that seemed perfectly safe, simply disappearing from the shops within a few short years once people understood it. And I should add, in fairness, that the Smithsonian notes that researchers have never been able to establish whether the machines actually caused any particular harm to the children who used them. Perhaps they did. Perhaps, for most children, the exposure was too small to matter. We don't know.

Let's set that beside our own lives. Today, if you have an X-ray at the dentist's, a heavy lead apron is laid across your body, and the technician steps out of the room, or behind a screen, before the machine is switched on, and the dose is measured and kept as low as it can be. The idea of putting X-rays in a shoe shop, so that children could see their toes for fun, seems to us almost unbelievable. In 1955, it was simply what a good shoe store did.

There's one more thing in this house that Dot worries about far more than X-rays, far more than comic books, and it isn't in the house at all. It comes with the summer.

Every summer, in those years, parents across America dreaded polio. It was a virus that spread most in the warm months, and in a small share of the children it infected, it attacked the nerves and left them paralysed, sometimes for life, sometimes needing a great machine called an iron lung to help them breathe. Nobody knew exactly how it spread. And so, in summer, careful mothers kept their children away from crowded swimming pools and beaches, and from cinemas, and worried over every fever and every stiff neck. In 1952, three years before our evening, the United States had its worst polio epidemic on record, with more than fifty-seven thousand cases reported. Dot remembers that summer very well. Michael was four, the age Linda is now.

But this winter, there is hope, though nobody yet knows whether it's justified. Last spring, in 1954, a vaccine developed by Dr Jonas Salk was tested on an enormous scale, in a field trial involving about one point eight million children in the United States, Canada and Finland. All through this winter, the results are being studied at the University of Michigan. And in a little under three months from tonight, on the twelfth of April, 1955, a doctor named Thomas Francis will stand up in an auditorium in Ann Arbor and announce that the vaccine works, and that it is, in the words of the press release that day, safe, effective, and potent. Church bells will ring. Dot will cry in the kitchen when she hears it on the radio. But tonight, on this cold January evening, she doesn't know any of that yet. She just knows that summer is coming, eventually, and she would rather not think about it.

And so, in between, in the long cold months and the safe parts of the year, the children of this street run free. Michael's afternoon today took him to the edge of a fenced-in drainage basin, one of the big hollows that towns on Long Island dug to catch rainwater, which local children called the sump, and which they were absolutely forbidden to climb into, and which they climbed into all the time. Then to a friend's back yard, then to a half-built ball field, then up and down the street on his bicycle. No one walked him there. No one knew precisely where he was. That, too, was completely normal.

Carol's new shoes are in their box now, by the front door, ready for school tomorrow, with the tissue paper still folded around them. She is very pleased with them. She has no idea that she'll remember that green glow for the rest of her life, and tell her grandchildren about it, and that they won't quite believe her.

On the television, a quiz show has come on. Frank is asleep in his chair, his chin on his chest. Dot nudges his foot with hers, and he wakes with a snort and says he wasn't sleeping. And then she looks at the clock, and at Michael, and at Linda, who is lying across the dog, and she says the words every child in every house on this street is hearing, at about this time, every single evening.

"Bath time."

## Part Seven — Around a Quarter Past Eight: Bath Time, Bedtime and Bert the Turtle

Here's the small question for this part of the evening, and it's a gentle one. When Michael and Linda are finally tucked into bed tonight, what are the last words they will say, and who will they be saying them to?

But first, the bath.

The bathroom in this house is tiny, with a tub, a sink and a toilet all within arm's reach of one another, and walls tiled halfway up in pale pink, and a small frosted window high up, black with night. Dot has run the bath, a few inches of hot water steaming in the cold air near the window, and Linda goes in first, with a rubber duck and a plastic boat, and then Michael, protesting, after her, in fresh water, because he has spent the afternoon in the sump.

Imagine you are sitting on the closed lid of the toilet, the only seat in the room, holding a towel ready, while Dot kneels beside the tub with her sleeves rolled up, scrubbing Michael's neck with a washcloth. The air is warm and damp and smells of soap, a big bar of white soap that floats. The mirror over the sink has steamed over completely. And Michael, in the middle of being scrubbed, is talking. He talks all the time in the bath. It's the only place where he has an audience that can't leave.

He's telling his mother about school. About the boy who got sent to the principal. About the fish tank in the classroom. About his teacher, who has a voice like a sergeant. And then, quite casually, the way children say the most important things, he tells her that they had a drill again today.

Dot's hands stop, just for a second. Then they go on scrubbing.

"Did you, sweetheart?" she says.

"Uh-huh," says Michael. "Miss Kessler said 'Drop!' and we all got under our desks." And then, because he's seven, and proud of it, he shows her. He scrunches down in the bath, right down, until his knees are up by his ears, and he puts both hands over the back of his neck, and squeezes his eyes shut, and the water sloshes over the side of the tub onto the tiles.

"Like that," he says, through his knees. "With your hands on your neck. And you don't look at the window."

Dot laughs, and tells him he's a good boy, and that he's getting water everywhere, and to sit up. And he sits up, very pleased with himself.

Linda, wrapped in her towel on your lap, is watching all of this with great interest.

Bath time is over quickly. Pyjamas, warm from the radiator of the floor, where Dot has laid them out flat, which is one of the best things about this house. Teeth brushed, with a great deal of reminding. Hair combed. And then, down the short hall, to bed.

The children's bedrooms are small and the beds are close together. Linda shares the second bedroom with Carol, who is still up, and Michael sleeps in a little room under the slope of the roof, up a narrow staircase, in the space that Levitt left unfinished and that Frank finished himself, on weekends, with plasterboard and a borrowed saw. Many families in Levittown did exactly that. The houses were built to grow with them.

Michael has a cowboy bedspread, with horses and lassos on it, and a little lamp with a cowboy on its base. On the shelf, there's a model aeroplane that he made with Frank, a navy fighter, painted dark blue, hanging from the ceiling on a thread. On the wall, a pennant from a baseball team in Brooklyn, the Dodgers, because Frank grew up four blocks from their ballpark, and Grandma still lives there, in the same apartment on the third floor, and Michael has been taken to see a game exactly once, and has talked about it ever since. A great many of the young families who came out to Levittown had come, like Frank and Dot, from the crowded neighbourhoods of Brooklyn and Queens, from apartments with shared stairwells and fire escapes and no yards at all. They brought their accents, and their loyalties, and their baseball teams with them. On a summer evening, with the windows open, you could hear the same game on a dozen radios up and down the street. On Sunday afternoons, many of them drove back into the city to visit their parents, and drove home again in the dark, the children asleep in the back seat, to the little houses with the lawns.

On the bedside table, there's a glass of water, and a flashlight, and a library book about a dog, which Michael is reading slowly, with his finger under the words. And downstairs, on the shelf in the kitchen beside the Sears catalogue, there's another book, the one that Dot has read more often than any other in her life. Its spine is cracked. Its pages are soft. There are pencil marks in its margins, and a ring on its cover where she once set down a coffee cup. It's called The Common Sense Book of Baby and Child Care, and its author is a paediatrician named Benjamin Spock.

It came out in 1946, the year Frank came home, and it arrived at exactly the right moment, just as millions of young couples, many of them living far from their own mothers and grandmothers for the first time, began having babies. It sold more than half a million copies in its first six months, and over the decades that followed it became one of the best-selling books in American history. In Levittown, where hardly anyone's mother lived nearby, it was very nearly a member of the family. When Linda had croup, when Michael wouldn't eat, when Carol had nightmares, Dot reached for Dr Spock before she reached for the telephone.

What did it say? A great many things, about feeding and fevers and teething and toilet training and sleep. But its central message, and here I'll paraphrase rather than quote it, was a reassuring one. It told young parents that they knew more than they thought they did. That they should trust their own common sense. That babies and children were not little machines to be trained on strict schedules, as some earlier experts had insisted, but people, each one different, who needed affection as well as discipline. For a young mother like Dot, alone all day in a new house with a new baby, that message was a kind of comfort.

And on the subject of bedtime, Spock's advice was much like what Dot does every night. A regular, calm, loving routine. A bath, a story, a prayer, a kiss. Not too much excitement. The door left open a crack, if that's what the child wants. Many of the small rituals of a 1950s bedtime, in houses like this one, came straight from that cracked and coffee-stained book on the kitchen shelf.

Let's set that beside our own world for a moment. Today, a young parent with a question at two in the morning has the whole internet to turn to: forums, videos, apps that track feeding and sleep, a hundred experts who disagree with each other. Dot had one book, a doctor she could telephone in the daytime, and the woman next door. And somehow, the children grew up.

Dot tucks him in. She sits on the edge of the bed. And here's the answer to our small question, because Michael, as he does every night, folds his hands together on top of the coverlet, and closes his eyes, and says his prayers.

Now I lay me down to sleep. It's a very old children's prayer, one that had been printed for American children in schoolbooks for about two hundred years by then, and in 1955, in countless houses across the country, children were saying it at bedtime, just as Michael is. He goes on, in a quick, practised murmur. And then, as children often did, he adds a list. God bless Mommy and Daddy. God bless Carol and Linda. God bless Pepper. God bless Grandma in Brooklyn. God bless the fish in the classroom.

So that's our small question answered. The last words Michael says tonight are a prayer, and he's saying them to God, with his mother listening, and the dog, who has followed them up the stairs, lying on the rug by the bed.

And that brings us, at last, to the second puzzle I gave you at the beginning of the evening. Why would a seven-year-old boy, who isn't even afraid of the dark, know exactly what to do if he ever saw a flash of light brighter than the sun? And why did the grown-ups who taught him believe it might help?

Because he had been taught, at school, by a film. And, in a sense, by a turtle.

The film is called Duck and Cover. It was made in 1951 for a government agency called the Federal Civil Defense Administration, which had been set up to prepare ordinary Americans for the possibility of an atomic attack. It was produced by a New York company called Archer Productions, written by Raymond J. Mauer and directed by Anthony Rizzo, and narrated by an actor named Robert Middleton. Many of the children who appear in it were real New York schoolchildren. It was first shown to the public in January 1952, as part of a travelling civil defence exhibition called Alert America, and over the following years, it was shown in schools all across the country. According to the Library of Congress, it was seen by millions of schoolchildren in the 1950s.

It begins with a cartoon. A cheerful turtle called Bert walks along, and when danger comes, he pulls his head and legs into his shell, and he's safe. And then the film switches to real children, in real places, walking to school, having a picnic, sitting in a classroom, and shows them what to do if they ever see a sudden, brilliant flash. Drop to the ground. Get under something, a desk or a table, or against a wall. Curl up. Cover the back of your neck. Cover your face. Duck, and cover. Like Bert.

That's what Michael showed his mother in the bath. He learned it from Bert the Turtle, and from Miss Kessler, and from drill after drill in his classroom, where a teacher would call out, at any moment, without warning, and twenty-five small children would dive under their desks.

So why did the grown-ups believe it might help? Because, in the early 1950s, many of the people who planned for an atomic attack thought that the greatest dangers to people some distance from the explosion would be the blast, the heat, and the flying glass and debris that the blast would hurl through the air. Comparatively little was yet understood, among the public, about radioactive fallout. And there was real evidence, from Hiroshima and Nagasaki, about the terrible injuries caused by shattering windows. Survivors had described how people standing behind window glass when the bomb went off had been badly wounded, while others nearby, not behind glass, were less hurt. A child who dropped to the floor and covered his face and neck, away from the windows, in the few seconds between the flash and the arrival of the blast, really might be protected from some of that.

That's the answer to our second puzzle. Michael knows what to do because the government made a film to teach him, and his school drills him in it. And the grown-ups believed it might help because, for some people, in some circumstances, it really might have done.

Now, here's where historians, and scientists, have disagreed, and still do.

For many years after the 1950s, Duck and Cover became a kind of joke. People pointed to it as the perfect example of the absurdity of the early Cold War: the idea that a wooden school desk could protect a child from an atomic bomb. Critics argued that the drills were really about something else, about keeping the public calm and giving them a feeling of control, and that they frightened a whole generation of children while offering them almost no real protection. Many people who grew up with the drills have said that they remember the fear more than anything else.

But some historians and scientists who study nuclear weapons have argued that the joke isn't quite fair. The historian of science Alex Wellerstein, for example, has pointed out that ducking and covering probably wouldn't help you much if the bomb went off right overhead, but that it could make a real difference for the many people who were a mile or more away, close enough to be hurt by the blast, the heat and the glass, but far enough away to survive. Modern emergency guidance still tells people, in a nuclear emergency, to get down, get inside, and stay away from windows. So the simple advice in the film was not entirely foolish. What it couldn't do, of course, was make the danger itself go away. And as weapons grew much more powerful in the years that followed, the advice in a film made in 1951 came to seem more and more out of date.

There's a smaller, stranger detail from these years, and it comes from New York City, just a few miles west of here. Civil defence drills began in the city's schools in the autumn of 1950. And at around the same time, schoolchildren in New York were given small metal identification tags to wear, a little like soldiers' dog tags, stamped with their names. The idea, which no one said aloud to the children, was that if the worst ever happened, they could be identified. During the Second World War, the city had done something similar, and by 1942, more than one and a half million New York schoolchildren had received tags. In the early fifties, it happened again.

Let's set this beside our own world, gently. Today, schools in many countries hold drills too: fire drills, and in some places other kinds of drills, for emergencies their pupils hope never to see. Children have always been asked to practise for things the grown-ups around them fear. And then, as now, the grown-ups have hoped very much that the practice would never be needed.

I want to say something about Michael, too, because it matters. He isn't frightened tonight. He showed his mother the drill in the bath as if it were a game, because, to him, mostly, it is one, like a fire drill, or Simon Says. It's Dot who stopped scrubbing for a moment. It's the grown-ups who carry the fear, in 1955, and try very hard not to pass it on.

Dot kisses Michael on the forehead. She turns off the cowboy lamp. She leaves the door open a crack, so that a thin line of light from the hall falls across the floor, because Michael says he isn't afraid of the dark, but likes the door open anyway.

"Goodnight, Mike."

"Night, Mom."

And down the stairs she goes, to put Linda to bed. The model aeroplane turns very slowly on its thread in the dark, catching the light from the door. And Pepper, on the rug, sighs, and settles his chin on his paws, and stays.

## Part Eight — Around Nine O'Clock: Lucy and Ricky Go to Tennessee

Here is this hour's small question. In a few minutes, Carol and her parents will be laughing at the television. And as they laugh, they'll hear other people laughing too, a great warm wave of laughter, rising and falling with theirs. Who are those people? Are they real?

Linda went to bed at half past eight, with very little fuss, which is rare. She was asleep almost before Dot reached the end of the story, a picture book about a little red engine, with her thumb in her mouth and a stuffed rabbit under her chin. The book was a small, thin one with a shiny cardboard cover and a strip of gold foil down its spine, one of the Little Golden Books, which had first gone on sale in 1942 for just twenty-five cents each, cheap enough to be sold in supermarkets and drugstores as well as bookshops. Before them, a new picture book with coloured pictures had often been a luxury. By the 1950s, they were everywhere, by the hundred million, and there's a whole row of them on the shelf in Linda's room, their spines cracked and their corners chewed, with titles about puppies and kittens and trains and a little boy who goes to the doctor's and isn't afraid. Linda can't read a word of them. She knows every one by heart, because Dot has read them aloud a thousand times, and if Dot skips a page, out of tiredness, Linda notices at once.

Carol, who shares the room, will tiptoe in later. For now, she has the living room, and the sofa, and her parents, all to herself, which almost never happens. She's sitting tucked up in the corner of the sofa in her dressing gown, with her knees under her chin, and her new shoes, still in their box, on the floor beside her, where she can see them.

That's the answer to our small question. Yes, those people are real. I Love Lucy was filmed in Hollywood in front of a live studio audience, on a stage with the sets laid out side by side, with several film cameras rolling at once, so that the audience could watch the actors perform each scene almost like a play, and laugh, and their laughter went onto the soundtrack. It was one of the things that made the programme feel so alive. When Carol laughs tonight, she's laughing along with a room full of strangers in California who sat in their seats and laughed at the same moment, weeks or months ago, at the same joke.

And because it was filmed, rather than broadcast live as most television was in those years, something else became possible, something that hardly anyone yet understood the importance of. The cameraman who lit and shot those first seasons was Karl Freund, a veteran of the cinema who, decades earlier in Germany, had photographed the great silent film Metropolis. He brought the skills of the movies to a television sitcom. And the film itself could be kept, and copied, and shown again. Most live television of the early 1950s simply vanished into the air the moment it was broadcast, and survives today, if at all, only in rough recordings. But the Lucy films survived, crisp and clear. They could be shown again the next summer, and the year after, and twenty years after, and fifty. It's one of the main reasons that I Love Lucy is still so well known today, and one of the reasons why, when we picture the 1950s, we so often picture them through the sitcoms. The reruns are, in a sense, the decade's own home movies, though they were never really of homes like this one at all.

Dot, by the way, is drinking instant coffee, which she thinks is wonderfully modern, and which her mother in Brooklyn, who still boils hers in a pot on the stove, thinks is barely coffee at all. Powdered instant coffee had been sold in jars since before the war, and the American army had drunk it by the ton during the fighting. Frank first tasted it on a ship in the Pacific. Now it sits in a jar on the kitchen counter, beside the sugar and the cigarettes, one more small, convenient, modern thing in a house that's full of them.

In the season we're in now, the season of 1954 to 1955, I Love Lucy was the most popular programme on American television. By one standard rating, on an average week, it reached about half of all the television homes in the country. It's very likely that, at this moment, on this street, a great many of the living rooms with that cool blue flicker in their windows are watching exactly what the Novaks are watching. Up and down the curving road, and across the whole of Levittown, and across the whole of America, millions of families are laughing at Lucy in jail in Tennessee.

Let's set that beside our own world. Today, if you watch a comedy, you might watch it alone, on a tablet in bed, or on a train, on your phone, with headphones. You might watch it a year after it came out, or ten years, or pause it in the middle to make a cup of tea. You're unlikely to know that half the country is watching the very same thing at the very same minute. In 1955, that was what television was: one programme, one moment, a whole nation in its living rooms, laughing together. It was one of the most shared experiences people had ever known.

There was something else unusual about I Love Lucy, which you can see right there on the screen. Lucy Ricardo is played by Lucille Ball, who was born in a small town in New York State. And Ricky Ricardo is played by her real husband, Desi Arnaz, who was born in Cuba, and came to America as a teenager, with his family, after a revolution, with almost nothing. He speaks English with a strong Cuban accent, and when Ricky gets angry or excited on the show, he bursts into a torrent of Spanish, and the audience roars. In a decade we often think of as narrow and uniform, the most popular programme in America starred a Cuban bandleader and his wife. When the show began, in 1951, the network had been nervous about that pairing. The audience wasn't.

And I'd like to tell you something about the episode two years ago that we heard about earlier, the night Lucy had her baby, because it's one of my favourite small facts about this whole decade. When Lucille Ball became pregnant in real life, in 1952, the network and the sponsors were worried about whether it was proper to show a pregnant woman on television at all. In the end, Lucy's pregnancy was written into the show. But, as the story is usually told, the word pregnant itself was not allowed to be spoken. The characters said that Lucy was expecting. It's a small thing. But it tells you a great deal about what was considered normal, and not quite normal, in 1952, on the most popular programme in the land.

At half past nine, I Love Lucy ends, and the bright tune plays again over the closing titles, and Carol sighs with deep contentment. Another comedy follows on CBS on Monday nights that season, a gentle one called December Bride, about a widow and her family, which was itself among the ten most watched programmes of the year. But Carol doesn't get to see it.

"Bed, missy," says Frank.

And she goes, carrying her shoebox, without complaining, because she's ten and she got to stay up for Lucy, and tomorrow she'll be able to tell everyone at school that she saw it, and half of them will have seen it too.

Now, while Carol tiptoes into the darkened bedroom and slides into bed beside her sleeping sister's bed, here's our disagreement for this hour. It's about the book on the kitchen shelf, the one by Dr Spock.

For years after it came out, Spock's book was simply loved, and trusted, and used, in millions of homes like this one. But in the late 1960s, when the children who had been raised on it were growing up, something changed. Some of those children, by then young adults, were protesting in the streets, against the war in Vietnam, against their parents' values, against almost everything. And Spock himself, by then an old man, had joined the protests against the war. And some critics began to blame him. They said his book had made a whole generation of parents too soft, too permissive, too unwilling to say no, and that it had raised a generation of spoiled, rebellious children. The Vice President of the United States, Spiro Agnew, was among those who accused Spock of corrupting the youth of America.

But many others, then and since, have argued that this was unfair, and that the critics simply hadn't read the book. Spock, they pointed out, had always written about the need for firm limits as well as affection. His book had been a reaction against the very strict, cold, clock-watching childcare advice of earlier experts, such as the psychologist John Watson, who had warned parents against showing their children too much affection. It was never an argument for having no rules at all. Even some conservative writers on parenting have since described the permissiveness charge as a kind of scapegoating. Spock himself, when asked about his influence, tended to play it down. I think, if you watched Dot tonight, giving Michael his bath, and listening to his prayers, and saying "Bed, missy" with Frank, you'd find it hard to see her as either a permissive parent, or a strict one. Just a parent, doing her best with a book and her common sense, as Spock had told her to.

The children are all in bed now. Linda is asleep, her rabbit under her chin. Michael is asleep upstairs, under his cowboy bedspread, with the aeroplane turning slowly above him in the dark. And Carol, in the bed beside her sister's, is lying awake for a little while, in the faint light from the hall, looking at the ceiling, and thinking about Lucy in her jail cell, and about her new shoes, and the green glow of her toes, and about whether her friend Nancy saw the show too.

And then, quite suddenly, she isn't thinking about anything at all.

In the living room, the television murmurs. Frank and Dot sit together on the sofa now, side by side, with their feet up on the hassock, and the dog across both of them. For the first time all day, it's just the two of them. Dot leans her head on Frank's shoulder. Frank puts his arm around her. The cigarette smoke drifts up slowly into the lamplight and spreads out under the ceiling.

Outside, the street is dark and quiet. The streetlights glow on the frozen lawns. A car goes slowly by, its headlights sweeping across the curtains. And on the front stoop, in the cold, the white milk box sits beside the door, with its two empty bottles inside, waiting.

## Part Nine — Around Ten O'Clock: The Last Cigarette and the Note in the Bottle

Here's the small question for this hour, as the evening winds down. When the television is finally switched off tonight, what would Frank and Dot have found if they'd left it on, much later, in the very small hours?

The answer is: nothing. Or almost nothing. And that, too, is one of the strange, ordinary things about this world.

It's ten o'clock. The children are asleep. The house has gone quiet in the way that houses do when the children are asleep, as if the walls themselves have relaxed. Frank and Dot are still on the sofa, watching a drama on CBS, a serious one, about a family and a court case, with actors in suits speaking very earnestly into the camera. Dot isn't really following it. She's doing the crossword in Newsday with a pencil, and asking Frank for help with clues, and ignoring his answers. Frank is on his last cigarette of the day, or so he says.

Imagine you're sitting in the armchair across from them, Frank's chair, which he's given up for the evening, with the hassock under your feet and the standing ashtray at your elbow. The lamp is low. The floor is warm. The television hums. You can hear, very faintly, the sound of the oil boiler in its little room off the kitchen, a soft, steady roar, like wind far away, as it heats the water that runs through the pipes beneath you. And every so often, from far off, the long, lonely whistle of a train on the Long Island Rail Road, carrying the last commuters home from the city.

That boiler drinks oil, and the oil has to come from somewhere. Every few weeks in the winter, a fuel-oil truck comes down the street, a heavy tanker with a hose on a reel at the back, and the driver pulls the hose across the lawn to a small pipe that sticks up out of the ground beside the house, unscrews its cap, and pumps a couple of hundred gallons of heating oil into the tank. It's another one of the delivery men, like the milkman and the bread man and the man who brings the dry cleaning, who keep this little house running. Dot pays him by cheque. He tips his cap. In a cold month, the oil bill is one of the bigger bills on the kitchen counter, and Frank grumbles about it, and turns the thermostat down a degree, and Dot turns it back up again when he's gone to work.

Let me tell you about that small question. Television, in 1955, did not go on all night. In the early hours, most stations simply stopped broadcasting. At the end of the evening's programmes, many stations played the national anthem, often over pictures of the flag, or of soldiers, or of aircraft flying. And then, for the rest of the night, the screen showed a test pattern, a fixed picture of circles and lines and numbers, sometimes with a drawing in the middle, accompanied by a single, steady tone. Or else just snow. A hissing grey storm of static, filling the little screen. If you woke up at three in the morning and turned on the television, that's what you'd see. Nobody would be there.

One of the most famous of those test patterns, made for the RCA company at the very beginning of American television, before the war, had at its centre a drawing of a Native American man in a feathered headdress, surrounded by circles and wedges and numbers that engineers used to adjust the picture. A whole generation of Americans who stayed up too late, or woke too early, remember it, staring out at them in silence from the screen, with that one long note humming beneath it. Some of them remember sitting in front of it on Saturday mornings, too, waiting for the cartoons to begin.

That's our answer. Late at night, the television, like the town, went to sleep.

Let's set that beside our own lives. Today, there is no hour of the day or night when there's nothing to watch. There are twenty-four-hour news channels, films on demand, programmes from every country in the world, and somewhere, always, someone is broadcasting something. In Frank and Dot's world, the night was still a time when the screens went dark. The day had an end. And people, mostly, went to bed.

Dot puts down her crossword and goes to the kitchen, and you follow her, padding across the warm linoleum.

She has a few small jobs to do before bed, the same jobs she does every night. She wipes the counter one last time. She takes the empty glass ashtray from the kitchen table and tips the ashes and ends into the rubbish and gives it a quick wash. She puts out three cereal bowls on the table, and three spoons, ready for the morning. She checks the back door, and leaves it unlocked, as she always does, because Frank will lock it, he says, though he usually forgets.

Tomorrow is Tuesday. There's an old rhyme, one that Dot's mother used to say, and her mother before her, about the days of the housewife's week. Wash on Monday. Iron on Tuesday. Mend on Wednesday. Churn on Thursday, though nobody on this street has churned butter in living memory. Clean on Friday. Bake on Saturday. Rest on Sunday. Dot doesn't follow it exactly. But today was wash day, and in the basket by the Bendix there's a great heap of clean, dry, crumpled cotton shirts and dresses and pillowcases, waiting for tomorrow, and the iron, and the ironing board, and the radio. Almost nothing in 1955 was made of fabrics that didn't need ironing. Frank's work shirts, the children's school clothes, the sheets, the handkerchiefs, the tablecloths. All of it. Tomorrow morning, after the children have gone to school, Dot will set up the board in the kitchen and stand at it for most of the morning, with a sprinkler bottle of water and the radio playing, and the steam rising.

And then she takes the milk bottle out of the refrigerator, the one from this morning, which has a little milk left in the bottom, and pours what's left into a jug, and rinses the bottle under the tap, swirling the water round until it's clean and clear. She sets it upside down in the drying rack beside the others.

Then she takes a pencil from the drawer, and a small scrap of paper, torn from the edge of the shopping list pad, and she writes, in her neat, round handwriting, a short note.

Imagine you are leaning on the counter beside her, reading over her shoulder. It's very short. It says something like this: Two quarts tomorrow please, and one pint of cream. Thank you. And it's signed, simply, with the house number.

She rolls the note into a little tube, and goes out of the kitchen door into the cold, in her slippers, with her cardigan clutched around her, and you go with her. The night air is sharp and still and smells of frost and oil smoke. She walks around to the front stoop, to the white metal milk box, and lifts the lid, and takes out one of the empty bottles that are waiting there, and pushes the little rolled-up note down into its neck. Then she puts it back in the box with the other, and closes the lid. And then, shivering, she hurries back inside, and shuts the door behind her.

You'll notice that's our third puzzle, happening right in front of us. Why would a careful mother leave a note in an empty bottle outside her front door all night long, where anyone at all could take it? We'll come to the answer in the dead of the night, when the house is asleep. But now you've seen it happen.

Back in the kitchen, Dot warms her hands under the tap. She looks around at her kitchen, at the white refrigerator and the white range and the Bendix in the corner, all of it gleaming under the light, and she feels, as she sometimes does at this hour, a small, tired, complicated feeling, made up partly of pride, and partly of something else she couldn't quite name.

And that brings us to our disagreement for this hour. It's about women like Dot, and how they really felt.

In 1963, eight years after our evening, a writer named Betty Friedan published a book called The Feminine Mystique. It was based partly on a survey she had carried out of her former classmates from college, women who had married and moved to the suburbs and were raising families, in houses not so different from this one. And Friedan argued that many of these women, despite their comfortable homes, their appliances and their healthy children, were quietly, deeply unhappy. They had been told, by magazines, by advertisers, by experts, by their whole culture, that being a housewife and mother was the only true fulfilment a woman could want. And yet many of them felt empty, restless, trapped, without being able to say why. Friedan gave that feeling a name, or rather, she named its namelessness. She called it the problem that has no name. Friedan wrote, too, from her own experience. She was herself a suburban mother of three, living in a house up the Hudson River from New York City, writing magazine articles at the kitchen table in the gaps of the day. Her book became one of the most influential of the century, and it helped to spark a new wave of the women's movement.

But historians since have complicated that picture. The historian Joanne Meyerowitz, in an influential essay published in 1993, looked closely at the popular magazines of the postwar years that Friedan had criticised, and found that they were not nearly as uniform as Friedan suggested. Alongside the articles praising housewives, there were many articles admiring women who worked outside the home, women in politics, women with careers. And other historians have pointed out that Friedan's survey drew mostly on educated, middle-class women, and that millions of other women in the 1950s, working-class women, Black women, immigrant women, many of them already working outside the home because their families needed the money, lived very different lives that her book largely left out. In fact, the share of married women working for wages grew steadily all through the 1950s. Friedan, they argue, described something real, but not something universal.

And there's another, quieter argument, about all those gleaming machines. The historian of technology Ruth Schwartz Cowan, in a book published in 1983 called More Work for Mother, argued that the new household appliances, the washing machines and vacuum cleaners and electric ranges, did not actually reduce the time women spent on housework nearly as much as the advertisements promised. Instead, she argued, standards rose. Clothes were washed more often. Floors were expected to be cleaner. Meals were expected to be more varied. And work that had once been shared with servants, or with other members of the family, or sent out to laundries, came to rest on the housewife alone. The machines made each task easier, but there were more tasks. Not everyone agrees with all of her argument, but it changed the way historians think about the kitchen.

I suspect that Dot, tonight, would recognise a little of all of them. She loves her house. She loves her children. She is proud of her Bendix. She is also, at the end of a Monday, very tired, and sometimes, at this hour, standing alone in her clean kitchen, she wonders what else she might have done. Both feelings are true. On this channel, I'd rather leave her with both of them than choose one for her.

Frank comes into the kitchen behind her, yawning, with the empty whisky glass in one hand. He sets it in the sink. He looks at the back door, and turns the little lock, and then, a moment later, absent-mindedly, unlocks it again, so that it'll be easier in the morning when he takes out the trash. Dot sees him do it and says nothing. It's what he always does.

In the living room, the drama has ended. A news bulletin comes on, a man at a desk reading from papers, about the President, and the weather, and something about China and the islands off its coast that Frank half listens to and then switches off. The picture shrinks to a single bright point in the middle of the screen, and lingers there for a moment, glowing, and then slowly fades. The room is dark, except for the lamp.

Frank turns off the lamp.

The house is dark, except for the thin line of light from the hall that falls through the crack of Michael's door, and a small night light in the shape of a seashell plugged into the wall by Linda's bed.

Frank goes round the house the way his own father did, in the old apartment in Brooklyn, checking on each child in turn. He looks in on the girls. Carol is curled up on her side, one arm flung over her new shoebox, which she has brought into bed with her. Linda has kicked off her covers, and he draws them back up to her chin, and she sighs without waking. Then he climbs the narrow stairs, very quietly, to look in on Michael, who is asleep on his back with his mouth open, and his cowboy bedspread half on the floor, and the model aeroplane turning above him in the dark. Frank straightens the bedspread. Pepper, on the rug, thumps his tail once against the floor, but doesn't get up.

And then Frank goes back down, and into the main bedroom, where Dot is already sitting on the edge of the bed, taking the pins out of her hair, and the bedroom door closes softly behind him.

Outside, on the frozen stoop, the white milk box sits in the dark, with its two clean bottles inside, and a little rolled-up note in the neck of one of them, waiting.

## Part Ten — Around One in the Morning: The Sleeping Street

Here's a small question for the very middle of the night. On a street like this, where every house is dark and every family asleep, who is awake?

Let's go and find out.

It's about one o'clock in the morning. The house is completely still. Frank and Dot are asleep in the main bedroom, Frank on his back, snoring very gently, Dot on her side, curled towards the wall, with her hair in pin curls under a scarf, the way many women slept in those years so that their hair would be set in the morning. Every evening, before bed, she winds small locks of damp hair around her finger into flat little coils and pins each one to her head with a crossed hairpin, two dozen of them or more, and ties a scarf over the top. In the morning, she'll take them out and brush the curls through. A trip to the beauty parlour was a treat, for special occasions. The rest of the time, like millions of other women, she did it herself, at home, at night, by feel, while the television played. Carol is asleep with her arm over her shoebox. Linda is asleep with her rabbit. Michael is asleep under his aeroplane. Pepper has come back down the stairs, at some point, and is asleep on the warm floor of the hall, outside the girls' door, stretched out flat, twitching slightly in a dream.

The only sounds are the small sounds of a house at night. The refrigerator humming in the kitchen, and then clicking off, and the sudden silence it leaves behind. The tick of the electric clock. The soft roar of the oil boiler, coming on for a while, and going off again. The faint creak of the roof timbers, contracting in the cold. And beneath everything, if you lay your hand flat on the floor, the gentle, steady warmth of the water moving through the copper pipes beneath you, round and round, all night long.

Imagine that you get up, very quietly, from the armchair where you've been dozing, and go to the front window, and draw back the edge of the curtain.

The street is asleep. Every window is dark. The streetlights stand in their pools of yellow light on the corners, and the frost on the lawns glitters faintly beneath them. There is no moon tonight. It was new, almost exactly, by a modern calculation, and so the sky above the rooftops is deep and dark, and full of stars, more than you'd see over most towns today, though fewer than you'd see over a field, because of the streetlights. If you look up, the brightest thing in the whole sky, high in the south-west, is not a star at all, but the planet Jupiter, shining steady and white. Below it and to the right, the great winter constellation of Orion, the hunter, with his belt of three stars in a row, is leaning down towards the western rooftops, and lower still, close to the horizon, Sirius, the brightest true star of all, is twinkling in many colours through the cold air. I worked those positions out, by the way, with a modern astronomical program, because nobody wrote down the sky over Levittown that night. I can't tell you whether it was clear. But if it was, that's what was there. A cat crosses the road, slowly, and disappears under a parked car. And on every front stoop, as far as you can see along the curving street, there's a small pale shape beside the door. The milk boxes. Dozens of them, in a long row, each one with its empty bottles inside, waiting.

Now, the answer to our small question. Who's awake?

Quite a lot of people, actually, though not here.

A few miles away, at the aircraft plants, the night shift is working. In the mid-1950s, the great factories of Long Island ran around the clock, and men worked through the night under the floodlights, with machines humming, building aircraft and their parts. Some of Frank's neighbours work nights. They come home at dawn, and sleep through the day, while their wives try to keep the children quiet.

At the hospitals, the night nurses are walking softly down the corridors. At the telephone exchange, the night operators sit at their switchboards, with their headsets on, connecting the few calls that come in at this hour. In 1955, by the way, telephone numbers in America still began with a word. Each local exchange had a name, and the first two letters of that name were dialled as part of the number, using the letters printed on the dial beside the digits. So instead of a string of seven numbers, you'd give your number as a name and five digits, and people remembered them the way you might remember a street address. The Novaks' telephone sits on a little table in the hall, heavy and black, with a cord and a rotary dial that whirrs back after every number, and it rings perhaps three or four times a day. Most of those calls are from Grandma in Brooklyn. Calling her is a long-distance call, or close enough to one that Dot keeps an eye on the clock while she talks.

On the Long Island Rail Road, the last trains have run, and the track workers are out with their lanterns. In the bakeries, the bakers are already at work, mixing the dough for the morning bread. And at the dairies, in the cold, brightly lit loading yards, the milkmen are arriving for work.

That's our answer. While the street sleeps, a whole other town is awake around it, quietly making the morning.

The milkman's day, in 1955, often began somewhere around this hour, in the very small hours of the night. He'd arrive at the dairy, in the dark, and load his truck with crates of glass bottles, freshly filled, still cold from the dairy's refrigeration rooms, along with butter and eggs and cream and cottage cheese. Picture that loading yard for a moment, under its electric lights in the frozen dark. The trucks are lined up in a row, box-shaped and painted in the dairy's colours, and the men in their caps and heavy jackets are swinging wire crates of bottles up onto the backs of them, two at a time, with a clinking like a thousand small bells. The air smells of cold milk and diesel and coffee from a big urn by the door. Somebody has a radio on. The men talk about the weather and the Dodgers and their children. And then, one by one, the trucks pull out of the yard and into the empty roads. Then he'd set off on his route, street by street, house by house, so that the milk would be on every doorstep before the families woke for breakfast.

Let's set that beside our own lives. Today, perhaps, you have groceries delivered too, by a van booked through an app on your phone, arriving in a window of an hour or two, with a text message to tell you when it's on its way. Or packages left on your doorstep by a delivery driver who rings the bell and is gone before you reach the door. In a sense, the milkman has come back, in a new form. But in 1955, there was no app. There was no phone call. There was only the note, in the bottle, in the box.

Before we come to that note, though, let's look one more time at all those sleeping houses, because there's something about them that historians have argued over for a long time, and it's gentle enough for this hour of the night.

Think of how many children are asleep along this street. In almost every house, there are two, or three, or four. Carol and Michael and Linda are typical. Levittown was full of children, so full that the schools could hardly be built fast enough. In 1946, the year after the war ended, about three and a half million babies were born in the United States. By the mid-1950s, the figure was over four million a year, and it would go on rising until 1957, when it peaked at about four point three million, before beginning, slowly, to fall. Each of those children needed a crib, a high chair, a pram, shoes, schoolbooks, a desk, a teacher. Whole industries, whole towns, grew up to serve them. This one, in a sense, among them. And it wasn't just Levittown. All across America, in the years after the war, the number of babies born each year shot up, and stayed high, for nearly two decades. It's what we call the baby boom. And the question is: why?

The answers vary a great deal. One famous explanation came from the economist Richard Easterlin. His idea, put very simply, was that couples decide how many children to have partly by comparing what they can earn with what they expect to want. The young parents of the 1950s, he argued, had grown up during the Great Depression and the war, with very little, and so their expectations were modest. Then, after the war, jobs were plentiful and wages were good. Compared with what they'd expected, they felt rich. And so they had more children.

Another, more recent explanation is about the very machines in Dot's kitchen. In 2005, three economists, Jeremy Greenwood, Ananth Seshadri and Guillaume Vandenbroucke, published a paper called The Baby Boom and Baby Bust, which argued that the boom was driven, in part, by a sudden burst of new technology in the home. Washing machines, refrigerators, frozen food, electric ranges: all of these made running a household with children less work than before, and so, they argued, having children became, in a sense, cheaper.

But that idea, too, has been challenged. In 2011, two other economists, Martha Bailey and William Collins, looked at where and when appliances spread across America, county by county, and found that the places where more households owned appliances did not have more babies. If anything, the reverse. And they pointed out a striking fact. The Amish, who used very little modern household technology, had a baby boom of their own at about the same time. So the machines couldn't be the whole story. Other scholars have pointed to the end of the war, to rising wages, to cheap mortgages, to people marrying younger, and to a general mood of hope. The truth is that economists still haven't reached a single answer. Perhaps there isn't one. Perhaps, all across the country, in millions of separate bedrooms, millions of young couples simply decided, for millions of separate reasons, that it was a good time to have a family.

Upstairs, Michael turns over in his sleep and mutters something about a horse. The aeroplane turns above him on its thread.

Now, back to the milk box on the stoop, and the note in the bottle.

And so, at last, here is the third puzzle I gave you at the very beginning of the evening. Why, last thing at night, would a careful mother roll up a little note, tuck it into an empty glass bottle, and leave it outside her front door all night long, where anyone at all could take it?

Because that was how you talked to the milkman.

The milkman came in the dark, while everyone was asleep, and he was gone again before anyone was up. You almost never saw him, except on collection day, when he came round in the daytime for his money, or when you left a note asking him to knock. So the empty bottle on the stoop became a kind of letterbox. If you wanted more milk tomorrow, or less, or a pint of cream, or a dozen eggs, or a pound of butter, or nothing at all because you were going away, you wrote it on a scrap of paper and rolled it up and pushed it into the neck of the empty bottle. In some places, people left money in the bottle too, coins, wrapped in the note. And in the morning, there it would be: exactly what you'd asked for, on your step, in the box, with the empties taken away to be washed and filled again.

And why leave it all night, where anyone could take it?

Partly because there was no other way. The milkman came at four or five in the morning. Nobody wanted to be up then to meet him. But partly, too, because in a street like this, in 1955, people simply didn't expect the note to be taken, or the bottles, or the milk. Nobody would want them except the milkman. It was a small, everyday act of trust, repeated on every doorstep, every night, all along the street, and on millions of streets across the country.

And the box itself, that squat white metal box with its hinged lid, had its own purpose. It was insulated, more or less, to keep the milk cool on summer mornings, before the family woke, and to keep it from freezing solid on a winter night like this one. It kept the cats and the dogs and the birds away from it. And it gave the milkman a place to leave things, neatly, that he knew everyone would look for.

That's the answer to our third puzzle. The note in the bottle was an order, and a message, and a small act of faith, all at once, left out in the cold for a man the family hardly ever saw, who would come quietly up the path in the dark and read it by the light of the streetlamp.

Home delivery like this was still very common in the 1950s. A little after our evening, in 1963, by the figures usually quoted, nearly thirty per cent of all the milk sold in the United States was still delivered to the home. By the government's figures for 1965, it was more than one gallon in every four. But it was already beginning to fade. As more families bought big refrigerators and cars, and as supermarkets spread through the new suburbs, more and more people simply bought their milk at the shop. By 1990, home delivery was down to about one per cent. By 2005, the last year for which the Department of Agriculture published a figure, it was less than half of one per cent.

But tonight, in January 1955, on this street, the milkman is still very much here. Somewhere, a few miles away, he's already at the dairy, loading his truck.

And on the stoop, in the dark, the note is waiting for him.

## Part Eleven — Around Half Past Four in the Morning: The Milkman's Round

Here's a small, gentle question for the deepest part of the night. How do you deliver hundreds of glass bottles, to hundreds of houses, in the dark, without waking a single person?

Let's ride along and see.

The milkman on this route is a man I'll call Eddie. He's invented, like the Novaks, though there were thousands of men exactly like him. He's forty-one, with a moustache and a flat cap and a heavy wool jacket with the dairy's name stitched on the pocket, and he has been doing this route, in one form or another, for nine years. Before that he drove a truck in the Army. He knows every house on these streets. He knows which families take two quarts and which take three, which ones have a new baby and need extra, which ones are behind on paying, which ones leave a cup of coffee in a Thermos on the stoop for him at Christmas, and which dogs bark. He knows the Novaks' Pepper, and Pepper knows him, and never barks at all.

Imagine you are riding beside him now, in the cab of his milk truck. It's a boxy, rounded little van, painted white, with the dairy's name on the side in curly red letters. And here's the first strange thing. Eddie is driving standing up. Many of the milk trucks of this era were built so that the driver could stand at the wheel, or perch on a high stool, and step straight down out of a wide sliding door without ever having to climb in and out of a seat. On a route with hundreds of stops, that saved a great many steps. The best known of these trucks were made by a company called Divco, and their rounded faces and round headlights were a familiar sight on American streets in the small hours for decades.

Behind you, in the back, there are crates and crates of bottles, stacked in rows, kept cold tonight by nothing more than the winter air itself, with the chill coming off them in waves. Each crate is a wire basket holding a dozen or so quart bottles, and the bottles clink gently against each other as the truck rolls over the bumps in the road. It's very cold in here. The heater barely works. The sliding door is open, so that Eddie can step in and out quickly, and the frozen air pours in. Your breath steams. His breath steams. He doesn't seem to notice.

The truck stops. Eddie picks up a wire carrier, a sort of basket with a handle, holding six or eight bottles at once, and steps down onto the street. And now you see the answer to our small question.

He walks softly. His shoes have thick rubber soles. He carries the bottles so that they don't knock against each other, holding the carrier steady against his leg. He goes up each path quickly and lightly, sets down the full bottles in the box, lifts out the empties, and reads any note by the light of the streetlamp, or by a small flashlight, without ever setting a bottle down hard on the concrete. He closes each lid gently, so that it doesn't clang. And then he's gone, to the next house, and the next, moving along the street in a sort of quiet rhythm, like a man dancing a slow dance he's done ten thousand times. Very occasionally, a dog barks. Very occasionally, a light goes on in an upstairs window and then goes off again. But mostly, the street sleeps on, and never knows he's been.

That's our answer. You deliver the milk without waking anyone the same way you do anything well: with practice, with care, and with rubber-soled shoes.

The bottles themselves are younger than you might think. For most of history, if you bought milk in a town, the milkman came round with a horse and cart and big metal churns, and ladled the milk out of the churn into whatever jug or can you brought to the door. Glass bottles with sealed caps only came in towards the end of the nineteenth century, and one of the early designs that is often credited with starting it all was patented in 1884 by a man named Harvey Thatcher, in the small town of Potsdam, in the north of New York State. A sealed glass bottle was cleaner, and you could see the milk inside. Within a few decades, the glass milk bottle had become one of the most familiar objects in America.

And even in Eddie's own time, the horse has only just gone. When he started on the routes, before the war, some dairies in the city still used horse-drawn wagons, because a horse that knew the route would walk on by itself from house to house, and stop at the right doors, while the milkman ran up and down the paths. Old milkmen used to say that the horses knew the customers better than they did. Eddie's truck doesn't know anyone. But it starts in the cold, mostly, and that's something.

The milk itself has changed, in Eddie's lifetime. When he was a boy, milk came with the cream risen to the top, a thick yellow band in the neck of the bottle that you could see through the glass. People called it the cream line, and a high cream line meant good, rich milk. Some families poured off the cream for coffee, or for their breakfast cereal, and some shook the bottle to mix it back in. But by the 1950s, more and more of the milk on Eddie's truck was homogenised, which means that it had been forced through tiny openings under high pressure, breaking the fat into droplets so small that they stay evenly mixed through the milk, and never rise. No more cream line. Some people missed it. Many didn't notice.

In Britain, at around this time, there was a lovely story about cream lines. Small birds called blue tits, and sometimes their cousins the great tits, had learned to perch on the bottles left on doorsteps in the early morning, and peck through the thin foil caps to drink the cream from the top. Nobody knows exactly where or when the first clever bird worked it out. But other birds, watching, copied it, and the habit spread across the country. British families learned to put a cup or a stone over their bottles. In Levittown, the bottles have cardboard caps and sit inside a closed metal box, and the American birds, so far as anyone knows, never learned the trick.

Let's set that beside our own kitchens. Today, milk usually comes in plastic jugs or waxed cartons, from a supermarket fridge, and it keeps for a week or more. There are dozens of kinds: whole, skimmed, semi-skimmed, lactose-free, and a whole shelf of milks made from oats and almonds and soybeans. In Dot's refrigerator, there is milk. Just milk. In glass. Delivered by hand, before dawn, by a man in a cap who knows her dog's name.

And here's a gentle disagreement to go with it, because it's about milk, and butter, and cream, and the bacon that Dot will fry for breakfast.

In the 1950s, a scientist at the University of Minnesota named Ancel Keys became famous for arguing that a diet rich in saturated fat, the kind of fat in butter and cream and red meat, raised cholesterol in the blood and contributed to heart disease. He spent years building that case, comparing the diets and the health of people in different countries, and his ideas went on to shape a great deal of official dietary advice in the decades that followed. But a British nutritionist named John Yudkin argued that Keys was looking in the wrong place, and that sugar, not fat, was the greater danger. He set out that case in a book in 1972, with the memorable title Pure, White and Deadly. For many years, Keys's view largely won the argument, and Yudkin's was pushed aside. More recently, some researchers have argued that Yudkin was treated unfairly, and that the role of sugar was underestimated. Others have defended much of Keys's work. Like so many arguments about food, it's not completely settled, and it may never be. Eddie, for his part, delivers both the cream and the sugar-coated cereal, and has no opinion on either.

Now, the street is changing a little, very slowly, as the night goes on.

At a house on the corner, a light comes on in the kitchen. It's a neighbour, a man who works the early shift, getting ready to go out. You see him through the window, filling the coffee pot, in his undershirt, scratching his head, half asleep. And then, a little later, at another house, a car pulls into the driveway and its headlights go off. A night-shift worker, coming home. He walks slowly up his path, past his own milk box, which Eddie filled ten minutes ago, and lifts out a bottle, and carries it in with him, and the door closes, and in a minute, his kitchen light goes on, and then off again, and he is going to bed as the rest of the street is getting ready to get up.

That's one of the quiet truths of a town like this. There is never a moment when everyone is asleep. Somebody, somewhere, is always just going to bed, and somebody else is always just waking up.

The cold is at its deepest now. The coldest part of a clear winter night usually comes not at midnight, but just before dawn, when the ground has been losing its warmth to the open sky for hours and hours. The frost on the lawns has thickened into a white fur on every blade of grass. The windscreens of the parked cars are glazed with it. The roofs of the houses are white with it, every one, from end to end of the street, except, faintly, in a few places, where the warmth from inside is leaking through the attic and melting a dark patch around the chimney. On a morning like this, if there were a light dusting of new snow, you could tell, just by looking at the rooftops, which houses had good insulation and which didn't.

Once a week, on collection day, Eddie comes round again in the daylight, in the late afternoon, with a little leather bag and a book of receipts, and knocks at each door, and the women of the street come to the door in their aprons and pay him for the week's milk, and sometimes chat for a minute about the weather, or the children, or the price of butter. Some of them leave the money in an envelope in the box instead. In December, many families leave him a little extra, or a bottle of something, or a tin of homemade cookies, tied with ribbon. He's not quite a friend, and not quite a stranger. He's the milkman. Every street had one, and most people remembered his name for the rest of their lives.

Eddie works his way down the street, house by house. He's had his own breakfast already, hours ago, a sandwich and coffee from a Thermos in the cab. He'll finish his route a little after the sun comes up, and then go back to the dairy, and count his empties, and do his paperwork, and go home in the afternoon, and sleep, and get up again in the dark tomorrow, and do it all again. Six days a week. It's hard work. It's cold work. But he likes it. He likes the quiet. He likes being the first one out. He likes knowing that, on every one of these doorsteps, in a few hours, a family will open the door and find what they asked for, exactly as they asked for it.

Above the eastern rooftops, very faintly, the sky is beginning to change. Not light, yet. Just a little less dark. A deep blue, at the very bottom of the sky, where before there was only black.

Inside the Novak house, everyone is still asleep. Frank is on his back, snoring very gently. Dot is curled towards the wall, in her pin curls. Carol is clutching her shoebox. Linda has her rabbit. Michael is lying sideways across his bed, with one foot hanging over the edge, under the slowly turning aeroplane. And Pepper, on the warm floor of the hall, opens one eye, and lifts his head, and listens.

Because he can hear something, out in the street, that nobody else in the house can hear yet. The soft hum of a truck, coming slowly closer. And the faint, familiar clink of glass on glass.

## Part Twelve — Around Six in the Morning: The Milk on the Step

The last small question of our night is a simple one. On a winter morning in this house, before the coffee, before the radio, before even waking the children, what is the very first thing Dot does?

Let's wait and see.

It's a little before six. Eddie's truck has come slowly round the curve of the street, humming in the dark, and stopped two houses down. He steps out, with his wire carrier, and comes up the Novaks' path on his quiet rubber soles. On the rug in the hall, Pepper's tail thumps once against the floor, and that's all. Eddie lifts the lid of the white milk box. He takes out the two empty bottles, and finds the little rolled-up note in the neck of one of them, and unrolls it, and holds it up to the light of the streetlamp on the corner. Two quarts tomorrow please, and one pint of cream. Thank you.

He nods. He sets two full quart bottles in the box, and a small pint bottle of cream beside them. He tucks the note in his pocket, so that he can mark it in his route book. He puts the empties in his carrier. And he lowers the lid, gently, so that it doesn't clang. And then he's gone, back down the path and into the truck, and the truck hums away round the corner, and the street is silent again.

The bottles sit in the box, in the dark, in the cold. Waiting.

Up above the eastern rooftops, the sky is changing now, slowly. Black has become a deep, dark blue. Deep blue is becoming a paler, greyer blue. And low along the horizon, between the bare young maple trees, a thin band of pale, clean light is spreading, like water seeping into paper. By a modern calculation, the first light of morning, what astronomers call civil dawn, came to Levittown at about twenty to seven that day. The sun itself would rise at about ten past seven.

Inside the house, in the main bedroom, the alarm clock goes off. It's a round, wind-up clock with two bells on top and a little hammer between them, and it makes a noise like a fire engine in a biscuit tin. Dot's hand shoots out from under the covers and finds it and silences it in one practised movement. Frank groans and pulls the pillow over his head.

And Dot gets up.

She pulls on her dressing gown, quilted and pale blue, over her nightdress, and finds her slippers with her feet, and pads out of the bedroom and down the hall, past the girls' door, past Pepper, who stands up, stretches, and follows her. She takes the scarf off her pin curls as she goes, and stuffs it in her pocket. But she doesn't go into the kitchen. Not yet. She goes to the front door. She opens it.

That's the answer to our last small question. The first thing Dot does, every morning, is bring in the milk.

Imagine you're standing beside her in the doorway now. The cold comes in like a wave, sharp and clean and smelling of frost. The sky in front of you is pale and luminous, the colour of the inside of a shell. Up and down the street, the roofs are white with hoarfrost. The lawns are white. The parked cars are white. And all along the street, on every stoop, the milk boxes sit beside the doors, and here and there, a door is opening, and a woman in a dressing gown is stepping out, and bending down, and lifting the lid, just as Dot is doing now.

She lifts the lid. And there they are.

Two quart bottles of milk, and a little pint of cream, the glass misted with cold, beaded with tiny crystals of frost. The cardboard caps are white with it. When Dot lifts the first bottle out, it's so cold that it seems to burn her fingers. And at the top, just beneath the cap, the milk in the neck has begun to turn to slush. On the coldest mornings of all, in those years, people remember finding that the milk had frozen so hard that the frozen cream had pushed the cardboard cap clean up out of the neck of the bottle, and stood up an inch or two above the rim, like a little white chimney, with the cap balanced on top. People from all over the colder parts of the country remember it, and I've read that a famous magazine cover painted the scene, back in the 1940s. This morning isn't quite that cold, or the box has kept the worst of it off. But the milk is very nearly ice.

Dot gathers up the bottles in her arms, against her dressing gown, and shuts the door with her hip, and shivers, and carries them through into the warm kitchen, where the floor is gently, evenly warm under her slippers, as it has been all night.

She sets the bottles down on the table, in a row. Three of them. The cold glass begins at once to fog over with mist in the warm air, and then to bead with droplets, which run slowly down the sides.

And then the morning begins, all at once, the way mornings do.

The coffee pot goes on the stove. The radio on the counter clicks on, warming up, like the television, and then a man's cheerful voice comes out of it with the weather and the time. The oil boiler roars softly in its room. Dot goes down the hall and knocks on doors and says the words every mother on the street is saying at this moment. Time to get up. Rise and shine. Up you get.

Carol is first, because she has new shoes to wear. She is sitting up in bed before her mother has finished speaking, opening the shoebox, lifting the tissue paper. Linda is next, because Carol has woken her, and she comes padding into the kitchen with her rabbit under her arm and her hair standing on end, and climbs onto a chair, and looks at the milk bottles with great seriousness, and says that the milk is crying. Dot laughs, and tells her it's just cold. Michael is last, of course, and has to be called three times, and comes down the narrow stairs at last in his pyjamas, with his cowboy hat on, and won't explain why.

Frank comes into the kitchen shaved and dressed in his work clothes, smelling of soap and shaving cream, and kisses Dot, and kisses each of the children on the top of the head, including Michael's hat, and pours himself coffee, and lights the first cigarette of the day.

Breakfast is on the table. Three cereal bowls, the ones Dot set out last night, with spoons. A big box of cereal, with a cartoon animal on the front, the same one that sang on the television last night, which Linda points out. And the milk. Dot prises the cardboard cap off the first bottle with her thumbnail, and the slushy, ice-cold milk pours out over the cereal in a thick white stream, and the cereal crackles. Frank has his coffee with a little of the new cream in it, as a treat. He says it's the best thing about a Tuesday.

Let's set this beside our own mornings, one last time. Perhaps, tomorrow, you'll wake to an alarm on your phone, and before you've even sat up, you'll be reading the news, or your messages, on that bright little screen. You might take your milk from a plastic jug, bought at a supermarket, with a date stamped on it a week away. Your coffee might come from a machine that grinds the beans by itself. You'll probably never meet the person who brought your milk to the shop, or know their name. Dot's morning has none of that. But it has something we've mostly lost. A note in a bottle, last thing at night, and an answer to it on the doorstep, first thing in the morning, left by someone she trusts, in the dark, while she sleeps.

And now, our last disagreement, a gentle one, because it brings us back to where we began.

At the start of the night, I told you about Stephanie Coontz, and her argument that the 1950s family we remember is largely a myth, and about the many people who feel that something real and good was lost when the decade ended. But it's worth knowing that the two sides agree on more than you might think. Coontz herself has acknowledged that the 1950s had real and unusual strengths for families like this one. Steady jobs. Rising wages. A narrower gap between rich and poor than in the decades that followed. Cheap mortgages for young families. Her point is not that those things weren't real, but that they came from particular economic conditions and a great deal of public support, rather than from some special virtue in the families themselves, and that they weren't shared by everyone. And many of those who look back fondly on the decade would agree that it had its shadows too. So perhaps the honest answer is the one we've seen all night, in this one small house. Some of it was better than we remember. Some of it was worse. And most of it was simply ordinary life, lived by people doing their best with what they knew.

There's one last small record I want to share with you, because it belongs to this town, and to the start of everything we've seen tonight. On the first of October, 1947, a little over seven years before this morning, the very first family moved into a brand-new house in what was then still called Island Trees. According to local historians, they were a young couple, Mr and Mrs Theodore Bladykus. Within a few years, there would be more than seventeen thousand families here, and tens of thousands of children, and tens of thousands of milk bottles on tens of thousands of stoops. But on that first morning, there was only one. And somewhere, I like to think, a milkman found his way down a brand-new street, past empty lots and piles of lumber, to leave the first bottles of milk ever delivered in Levittown, on a single doorstep, in the dawn.

Out in the street now, the sun is coming up. It lifts over the eastern rooftops in a burst of pale gold light, and every frosted lawn and every frosted roof begins at once to glitter, as if the whole street had been dusted with sugar. Frank goes out to the car, and scrapes the frost off the windscreen with the edge of an old ruler, and the Ford coughs and starts. Carol and Michael set off down the street towards school, in their coats and hats and mittens, Carol walking carefully in her new shoes, Michael running ahead, kicking at the frost, his breath streaming out behind him. Nobody walks them. Nobody needs to. Linda waves to them from the window, with the dog beside her.

And in the kitchen, in the warm, the milk bottles stand in a row on the table, in the morning light. The sun comes slanting through the window over the sink and falls across them, and the glass glows. The frost on them has melted now, and the droplets of water run down their sides and gather in little shining pools on the table. One of them is half empty already. The other is full. The little pint of cream stands beside them, with its cap pushed back. Tonight, after supper, Dot will rinse them, and set them out in the box again, with a note. And in the dark, before dawn, Eddie will come up the path on his quiet rubber soles, and the bottles will be full again in the morning, just as they were today, and just as they'll be tomorrow, and the day after, for as long as anyone on this street can imagine.

Thank you for spending this long winter night with me, and with Frank and Dot, and Carol and Michael and little Linda, and Pepper the dog, and Eddie the milkman, in their small, warm house on a curving street in Levittown. If this story helped you rest, or helped you drift off somewhere along the way, I'm truly glad. And if you'd like to come back for more nights like this one, you might consider liking this video, and subscribing to the channel, gently, whenever you're ready. There's no hurry at all.

Next time, we'll travel further back, and across the ocean. We'll spend a winter night in a narrow brick terrace house in Victorian London, in the 1880s, with a family who lights their rooms with gas, warms them with coal, and is woken before dawn by a man who earns his living walking the dark streets with a long pole, tapping on bedroom windows. We'll find out which of the strange things they did were perfectly normal, and which of them might have kept you awake. I hope you'll join me there.

Until then, sleep well. Keep warm. And may there always be something good waiting for you on the doorstep in the morning.
