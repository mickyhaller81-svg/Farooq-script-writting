# Titanic: The Final Night, Hour by Hour | History for Sleep

## Part One — Around Seven O'Clock in the Evening: Dinner in the Second-Class Saloon

The sun has just gone down behind the ship.

Far out in the North Atlantic, more than a thousand miles from the nearest city, the western sky is a long band of fading gold, and above it the blue is deepening, minute by minute, towards night. The sea is very calm. Not the grey, heaving Atlantic of the storybooks, but something almost like a lake, smooth and dark and wide, rolling away to the edge of the world without a single white cap on it. The air is cold, and getting colder. And moving across that smooth dark water, with no sound but a deep, steady hum from somewhere far below, is a ship so long that, if you stood at one end of her, the people at the other end would look like small figures on a distant street.

Her four tall funnels are buff-coloured, with black tops. Thin streams of smoke drift back from three of them into the dusk. Along her side, row after row, the portholes are lighting up, one after another, until she looks like a whole town afloat, every window glowing yellow against the darkening sea.

This is the Royal Mail Steamer Titanic. The date is Sunday, the fourteenth of April, 1912. It is about seven o'clock in the evening by the ship's own clocks, and she is four days out from Southampton, heading west for New York.

Now, I think almost all of us carry a picture of this ship in our minds already. We've seen the films. We've seen the grand staircase, and the glittering ballroom, and the band in white jackets, and the proud men in evening dress saying that not even God could sink her.

So let me say, gently, before we go any further: tonight is not the Titanic of the movies. There was no ballroom on board. The famous claim that she was unsinkable was never quite the boast that legend makes it. And the people we're going to spend this night with aren't millionaires in the first-class suites. They're an ordinary family, travelling in second class, who saved for a long time to buy their tickets.

I should also tell you something honestly, before we go in to dinner. If you had been aboard this ship tonight, as a man travelling in second class, the plain truth is that you would very probably not have survived until morning. Of all the groups of people on board, the second-class men had one of the lowest chances of living through this night. But we're going to walk through it together, slowly and calmly, hour by hour, and I promise you that the family we follow will reach the morning. All of them.

And as we go, I'd like you to carry three small mysteries with you, the way you might keep three smooth pebbles in the pocket of a coat.

The first is this. On a night that was perfectly clear, with more stars in the sky than most of us will ever see, why was it so very hard for two sharp-eyed men in the crow's nest to see a mountain of ice in front of them?

The second. When the lifeboats began to go down the side of the ship, why did so many of the first ones leave with empty seats?

And the third. In the small hours of the morning, as a little ship raced through the dark to reach the survivors, why would her captain order the heating and the hot water in his own ship to be switched off?

I'll answer each of them in its own time. The first a little before midnight. The second in the hour after. And the third as the night is turning towards dawn. There's no hurry. We have the whole night.

Now, before we go any further, I want to be clear with you about something, because it matters on this channel. The family you're about to meet is invented. Their names, their faces, their little habits and worries, I have made up. So has the young steward who looks after them. But everything around them is real. The ship was real. The times and the messages and the testimony are real, and the people of the crew and the passengers who appear tonight under their own names appear only as the records show them, doing what they were seen to do, and saying only what they themselves said they said. Where I quote someone, it comes from the inquiries held in America and in Britain in 1912, or from the accounts survivors wrote down soon after. And where I can't be sure of the exact words, I'll tell you that I'm paraphrasing. Real people lived and died on this night, and they deserve the truth.

So. Let's meet our family.

At a long table in the second-class dining saloon, down on D deck, towards the back of the ship, sits a man named Daniel Hollis. He's thirty-seven years old. He's a printer, a compositor, which means that for twenty years he has stood at a sloping wooden case of metal type, picking out the letters one by one with his fingers, faster than you could follow, setting them into lines, the lines into pages. He's leaving his job in Reading, in England, because his younger brother has written from Toronto, in Canada, to say that there's work there for a good printer, and a house with a garden, and a better wage. The family will land in New York and go on to Canada by train.

Beside him is his wife, Ellen. She's thirty-four. She has sold most of their furniture, packed what was left into two trunks and a carpet bag, and said goodbye to her mother at Reading station, and she has not yet let herself think too much about that.

Across the table sit their children. Rose is eleven, sharp and serious, and she has read every notice and plan of the ship she can find, and can tell you how many lifeboats there are, which deck the library is on, and how long the ship is, almost to the foot. Tom is seven, and has spent four days trying to see the engines. And on Ellen's lap, too sleepy now to sit up properly, is May, who is four, with her thumb in her mouth and her eyes half closed.

And standing just behind them, in a short white jacket, with a napkin folded over his arm, is a young man named Joseph Wren. He's twenty-three, from a terraced street in Southampton, and he's a second-class steward. Five of the family's meals a day pass through his hands, and he's already taught Tom how to fold a paper boat.

Here's a small question to begin with, one we'll answer before this part is done. What did a second-class family actually eat for dinner, on this last night?

Imagine that you're sitting at this table now, on a swivel chair bolted to the floor, the way chairs were on ships, so that they couldn't slide in a swell. The room around you is long and bright, panelled in pale oak, with white tablecloths on long tables running across the room, and the soft glow of electric lights. Electric lights. Remember that in 1912, many homes in Britain still had no electricity at all. The air smells of soup and fresh bread and coffee. There's a low murmur of voices, a clink of china, the occasional laugh. And under all of it, through the soles of your feet, through the legs of your chair, comes that steady hum of the engines, so constant that after four days you hardly notice it.

Joseph sets down the soup. And here, at last, is our answer, because we know exactly what was served tonight, course by course, thanks to a printed menu card that survived.

It was kept by a passenger named Mrs Bertha Marshall, who was travelling in second class, and it's held today at the National Maritime Museum in Greenwich, in London. It's printed as a postcard, with its own envelope. The dinner begins with consommé with tapioca, a clear soup with little pearls of tapioca floating in it. Then baked haddock with sharp sauce. Curried chicken and rice. Spring lamb with mint sauce. And roast turkey with cranberry sauce. There are green peas, and puréed turnips, and boiled rice, and potatoes boiled and roasted. And for afters, plum pudding, wine jelly, a coconut sandwich, American ice cream, nuts, fresh fruit, cheese and biscuits, and coffee.

That's our answer. A second-class dinner on the Titanic was a feast that most families in England would have eaten only at Christmas, if then. Tom has had two helpings of the ice cream, and Ellen is pretending not to have noticed.

Some people have pointed to the roast turkey with cranberry sauce as a little nod to the country the ship is sailing towards. It's the kind of dish an American family would recognise from its own Thanksgiving table. Nobody knows for certain whether the chefs meant it that way. But it's a nice thought, on a ship full of people on their way to new lives.

Let's set this beside your own life for a moment. Today, if you sit down to dinner, you might look at your phone to see the weather, or a map, or a message from someone on the other side of the world. In this saloon tonight, there is no way at all for Daniel to know what the weather is doing a hundred miles ahead. There's no radio in his pocket and no television on the wall. And yet, somewhere above him, in a small room near the top of the ship, two young men with headphones are sending and receiving messages through the air, in Morse code, by wireless telegraphy, which in 1912 is one of the newest wonders in the world. We'll visit them later.

And now, about that word, unsinkable.

You've almost certainly heard that the Titanic was advertised as unsinkable. And there's some truth in it, but not the truth that legend tells. In about 1910, the White Star Line, which owned the ship, printed publicity material for its two great new sister ships, the Olympic and the Titanic. And in it, the company said that, as far as it is possible to do so, these two wonderful vessels are "designed to be unsinkable." Look at the careful words. Designed to be. As far as it is possible to do so. In 1911, a respected trade journal called The Shipbuilder, and some newspapers, described the ship as "practically unsinkable." Practically.

And this is our first place where people disagree. After the disaster, the White Star Line insisted that it had never claimed the ship could not sink, only that she had been designed with care, with watertight compartments and doors that could be closed from the bridge. Some modern researchers, such as the Titanic historian George Behe, have argued that this was a bit too convenient, and that whatever the small print said, the company wanted the public to come away believing she was as safe as a ship could be. Others point out that one surviving copy of the brochure has a misprint in it, and may have been only a proof that never went into wide circulation. And the Titanic Historical Society has traced how, in reprinted newspaper stories over the years, the word "practically" quietly fell away, until all that was left was the single word that everyone remembers. Unsinkable.

Daniel, for his part, has read the word in a newspaper, and he believes it, more or less, the way you or I might believe that a big modern airliner is perfectly safe. Not because anyone has promised. Just because it is so very big, and so very new, and so obviously built by people who knew what they were doing.

May has fallen asleep against her mother's shoulder now, a warm, heavy weight. Ellen strokes her hair. Rose is reading the menu card again, as if she means to memorise it. And Daniel takes something out of his waistcoat pocket and lays it on the white tablecloth beside his coffee cup.

It's a pocket watch. A plain silver one, a little worn at the edges, with a white face and black hands and a small dial for the seconds near the bottom. It belonged to Daniel's father, who was also a printer, and who carried it every working day of his life. It ticks, very quietly. If you lean close, you can hear it under the murmur of the saloon. Tick, tick, tick, tick.

Daniel opens the back of the case with his thumbnail. He's been doing something every night of this voyage that has fascinated Tom. Because every night, at about midnight, the clocks on this ship are changed.

Here's why. As a ship sails west, she's chasing the sun. Noon comes a little later each day, the further west you go. So the ship's officers measure the sun's height at noon each day with their sextants, and work out the local time for the ship's position, and the clocks on board are put back, a little at a time, usually around midnight, so that by the morning, noon will be when the sun is highest in the sky again. On a westward crossing, that can mean somewhere around three quarters of an hour every night.

So the time on Daniel's watch, and on all the clocks of the Titanic, is not London time, and it isn't New York time either. It's the ship's own time, belonging only to her, to this one patch of ocean on this one night. Remember that, because every hour I'll give you tonight is ship's time, the time that the people on board were living by.

Daniel winds the watch. Slowly. Click, click, click. He sets it carefully to match the clock on the saloon wall. Then he snaps the case shut and puts it back in his pocket, and pats it, the way he always does.

Joseph Wren comes round with the coffee pot and asks if there's anything more they'd like. Tom asks if he can see the engines. Joseph says, very seriously, that he'll see what he can do, and winks at Rose.

Outside, beyond the portholes, the last of the gold has gone from the sky. The stars are coming out over the sea, more and more of them, until they are thick as frost. There is no moon tonight. And the air on the open decks has dropped, in the last hour, to something very close to freezing.

The night has begun.

## Part Two — Around Eight O'Clock: Hymns in the Saloon

Here is the small question for this part of the evening. On a calm, safe Sunday night, in a warm and brightly lit room, why would a hundred people choose, again and again, to sing hymns about danger at sea?

Hold that question. Because the tables in the dining saloon are being cleared now, and something is beginning.

Dinner is over. The stewards, Joseph among them, are moving between the long tables, gathering up the plates and the cups and the little silver dishes, wiping down the cloths. But a good many of the passengers haven't left. They've pulled their swivel chairs around to face one end of the room, where there's a piano. And standing beside it is a clergyman.

His name is the Reverend Ernest Carter. He's a real person, an Anglican vicar from a parish in the East End of London, travelling in second class with his wife, Lilian. And tonight, on this Sunday evening, he has offered to lead a hymn-singing in the saloon, for anyone who'd like to join in.

We know about this evening because one of the passengers in the room wrote about it, only a few weeks later. His name was Lawrence Beesley. He was a science teacher from Dulwich College, in London, thirty-four years old, a widower, travelling in second class to America. And in 1912, he published a book called The Loss of the SS Titanic, one of the very first and calmest accounts of this night, written by a man trained to observe carefully and to say exactly what he saw. He'll be one of our most trusted companions tonight.

Beesley describes how Mr Carter got some hundred passengers singing hymns, with someone at the piano to accompany them, and how, before each hymn, Mr Carter told a little about the hymn itself and the person who'd written it. I'll paraphrase Beesley rather than quote him, because I want to be careful with his words. But one thing in his account has stayed with readers ever since. He noticed, he wrote, how curious it was that so many of the people in the room chose hymns that dealt with the dangers of the sea.

Imagine you're sitting among them now. The Hollis family has stayed. Daniel has turned his chair towards the piano, and Ellen is beside him with May asleep across her lap, and Rose and Tom are perched on the end of the bench of a neighbouring table. The lights are warm. The room is full of people in their Sunday clothes, clergymen and teachers and shopkeepers and young couples and grandmothers, all from different towns and different countries, all going to America. Someone passes you a hymn book. The pianist plays the opening bars. And all around you, a hundred voices begin to sing.

One of the hymns that was sung that night, according to the accounts that have come down to us, was "Eternal Father, Strong to Save." You probably know its refrain, even if you don't know its name. It ends each verse with a prayer for those in peril on the sea. Others that have been remembered include "On the Resurrection Morning" and "There Is a Green Hill Far Away." And the service seems to have ended, fittingly for the close of a Sunday, with "Now the Day Is Over."

So why would people on a calm night choose to sing about danger at sea? That's our small question, and I think the answer is very simple, and very human. "Eternal Father" was known to everyone in Britain as the sailors' hymn. It was sung in churches in seaside towns, and in the navy, and by families with sons at sea. When you are on a ship, in the middle of the ocean, those are simply the hymns that come to mind. Nobody in this room is frightened. They're singing about the sea because they're on it, and because, for many of them, it is the biggest and strangest journey they have ever made. Beesley found it curious only because of what happened afterwards.

And I'll say this now, gently, so that it isn't a shock later. The Reverend Ernest Carter and his wife Lilian were among those who did not survive this night. But at this hour, he is standing by a piano in a warm saloon, telling a room full of strangers the story of a hymn, and they are singing.

Rose knows the words to "There Is a Green Hill." She sings it loudly, and slightly ahead of everyone else, and Tom pokes her. Daniel has a good, steady voice, a printer's voice, used to calling out across the noise of a press room. Ellen sings softly, so as not to wake May.

Now, while they sing, let's leave the saloon for a moment, and climb up through the ship, to the room I mentioned earlier. The wireless room.

It's a small room on the boat deck, just behind the bridge, near the base of the first funnel. And inside it, sitting at a desk with headphones on, is a young man named Jack Phillips. He's twenty-five, the senior wireless operator. His assistant, Harold Bride, is twenty-two. They aren't, strictly speaking, members of the ship's crew. They work for the Marconi company, which runs the wireless service, and most of their work, on a crossing like this, is sending and receiving private messages for the passengers: greetings, business telegrams, news of arrival, all of it paid for by the word.

But they also pass along messages from other ships. And today, all day, those messages have been about ice.

Through the day, several ships ahead of the Titanic, or crossing the same waters, had sent warnings of ice in the shipping lanes. In the morning, the Cunard liner Caronia had sent one. In the early afternoon, the Baltic, another White Star ship, had sent another, reporting icebergs and field ice. A German liner, the Amerika, had reported ice too. And at about half past seven this evening, Harold Bride had heard the Leyland liner Californian sending a warning about three large icebergs, to another ship, and passed it on.

I'm summarising these messages, rather than quoting their wording, because the exact texts were argued over at the inquiries afterwards, and I'd rather not put words on the page that I can't be sure of. But the inquiries agreed on the main point. Ice had been reported, more than once, in the waters ahead.

None of this was unusual in itself. In April, the great ice fields that drift down from the Arctic along the coast of Greenland and past Newfoundland often reach the shipping lanes. Ice warnings were a normal part of an April crossing. And ships, at that time, very often kept up their speed through such waters, trusting to the lookouts and the officers to see any ice in good time on a clear night.

Let's think about that in modern terms for a moment. Today, a ship crossing the North Atlantic has radar that can see an iceberg miles away in the dark or in fog. She has satellite positioning that tells her where she is to within a few yards. And since 1914, there has been an International Ice Patrol, set up because of this very night, that tracks icebergs and broadcasts their positions to every ship at sea. On the Titanic tonight, there is no radar. There is no ice patrol. There are messages, written down on paper forms, and the eyes of men on watch.

And now here is a place where historians genuinely disagree, and it's about the very hours and minutes we're following.

I told you that every time I give you tonight is ship's time. But how far was the Titanic's time ahead of New York time? You'd think that would be a simple question. It isn't. The American inquiry, held in Washington, worked on a difference of one hour and thirty-three minutes. The British inquiry, in London, used a difference of one hour and fifty minutes. A later legal hearing in America, in 1913, implied yet another figure. And many modern researchers, including Samuel Halpern, who has written in great detail about the timing of this night, have argued that the Titanic's clocks were in fact about two hours and two minutes ahead of New York time, and that the inquiries' figures were muddled, partly because of confusion over whether the ship's clocks had been put back that night or not. So when you read a time from that night in an old newspaper, or in a message sent by another ship, it may have been in New York time, or in that ship's own time, or in the Titanic's time, and the differences can be half an hour or more. It's one of the reasons that survivors' memories of when things happened don't always agree. They were all, in a sense, living by slightly different clocks.

Down in the saloon, the singing goes on.

There's one other real person I want to mention at this hour, because he's dining in another part of the ship, and he'll matter a great deal later. The captain of the Titanic is Edward John Smith. He's sixty-two, with a white beard, one of the most experienced and respected captains of the White Star Line, and this is said to have been planned as one of his last voyages before retirement. Tonight, according to the standard accounts, he's at a dinner party in the ship's à la carte restaurant, as the guest of a wealthy first-class family. We'll meet him on his bridge in the next hour.

And somewhere far to the south of us, about fifty-eight miles away, or something close to that, as we'll see, there's another ship, a much smaller one, that none of our family has ever heard of. She's a Cunard liner called the Carpathia. She left New York on Thursday, bound for the Mediterranean, for Gibraltar and Italy and the port of Fiume. Her passengers are just finishing their own Sunday evening. Her captain is a man named Arthur Rostron. Remember the name. We'll come back to him.

The singing will go on for a good while yet, hymn after hymn, story after story. But small children cannot stay up for all of it, and families with little ones are already slipping away to their cabins.

Ellen is looking at the clock on the saloon wall. May is heavy in her arms. Tom is yawning hugely and claiming not to be tired. It's time to go to bed.

Daniel takes his father's watch out of his pocket and checks it against the saloon clock. They match, to the minute. He nods, and puts it back.

The family makes its way out of the saloon and along the corridor, white-painted and brightly lit, with a soft carpet underfoot, towards the second-class staircase. Joseph Wren passes them going the other way with a tray, and stops to say goodnight, and asks Tom whether he's still hoping to see the engines. Tom nods, sleepily. Joseph tells him that tomorrow, perhaps, he'll take him to the top of the stairs where you can hear them loudest. Tomorrow.

Their cabin is down on E deck, aft, a little way along a narrow white corridor. It's a small, clean, tidy room, with two pairs of bunks, one above the other, a washbasin with a little mirror above it, a folding wooden seat, and a single round porthole, black now against the night. There are white sheets and good wool blankets, tucked in tight the way stewards tuck them. And it is warm. Steam heating keeps the cabins comfortable, even with the cold outside. The hum of the engines is louder down here, deeper, more of a feeling than a sound.

Ellen puts May into the lower bunk, and the little girl doesn't wake. Rose climbs into the bunk above. Tom insists on the other upper bunk, because it's higher and he can see the porthole. Ellen washes their faces with a flannel and they complain. Daniel tells them a story, a short one, about a printer's devil, the boy apprentice who runs errands in a print shop, which is what Daniel was himself at Tom's age. Tom is asleep before the end of it.

Daniel doesn't go to bed yet. He tells Ellen he wants a breath of air, and she tells him to take his coat, and he does. He climbs the stairs, up and up, past the landings, until he steps out through a heavy door onto the open deck at the back of the ship, where second-class passengers may walk.

The cold hits him like a wall. It's a still, dry, biting cold, the kind that makes the inside of your nose prickle and your eyes water. There's no wind at all, except the breeze that the ship makes herself by moving through the air at more than twenty knots, about twenty-five miles an hour. He turns up his collar. His breath steams.

And above him, the sky is extraordinary. There's no moon tonight. I worked that out, by the way, using a modern astronomical model, because nobody on board would have needed to write it down: the moon had set in the afternoon, and it was only a thin sliver, a few days from new, and it would not rise again until about five o'clock in the morning. So the whole sky belongs to the stars. They come right down to the sea, all the way round, so many and so bright that the horizon is hard to find. The Milky Way runs overhead like a long pale cloud.

Daniel walks to the rail and looks forward along the ship. She is so long, nearly nine hundred feet, that the front of her seems a long way off, a dark shape crowned with lights, with the four great funnels rising against the stars. Somewhere up there, at the very top of the front mast, in a small round crow's nest, two men are standing watch in the cold. He can't see them. He doesn't think of them. He thinks of Toronto, and his brother, and the garden.

There are more than two thousand people on board this ship tonight. Passengers and crew. And almost all of them, at this hour, are doing what Daniel is doing, or settling down to sleep, or reading, or talking quietly, or playing cards in the smoking rooms, warm and safe and a long way from home.

He takes out his watch, and holds it up to the light from a deck lamp. It's a little after half past eight. He winds it a turn or two more, out of habit, though it doesn't need it. Then he goes back inside, to the warmth.

Up on deck, behind him, the stars are thicker than ever. And the temperature is still falling.

## Part Three — Around Nine O'Clock: The Captain on the Bridge

The small question for this hour is a sailor's question. When an officer on a ship in 1912 asked the lookouts to keep a sharp watch for "growlers", what exactly were they meant to be looking for?

We'll find out on the bridge. So let's go there now.

Imagine that you're climbing the last steep stair to the very front of the boat deck, high above the sea, in the cold and the dark. You step into a long, narrow space, open at the sides to the night, with a row of windows looking forward over the bow. In the middle stands the wheelhouse, enclosed, with the great steering wheel inside it, and a quartermaster standing at it, his hands on the spokes, his eyes on the compass in front of him, lit by a small lamp. Everything else is kept dark. Deliberately dark. Because the men here need their eyes to be used to the night, to see as far ahead as possible across the black water. The only lights are the faint glow of the compass, and the stars, and the long, pale gleam of the ship's own deck lights spilling down her sides far behind you.

It's very quiet. There's no shouting, no bustle. The officer of the watch walks slowly from one side of the bridge to the other, and back again, looking out. That's his whole job, for four hours at a time. To look out, and to think.

The officer on watch at this hour is the second officer, Charles Herbert Lightoller. He's thirty-eight, a tall, weathered, experienced seaman who first went to sea as a boy of thirteen, on sailing ships, and has been shipwrecked before. He will survive this night, and he will give long, careful testimony afterwards, in Washington and in London. So we can listen to him.

At about five minutes to nine, Lightoller said, Captain Smith came onto the bridge.

The two men stood together for a while, and talked about the weather. And in the British inquiry, Lightoller described that conversation. They spoke about how calm the sea was, and how there was no wind at all. And when he was asked about it in London, Lightoller put it simply: "No, it is a flat calm as a matter of fact."

A flat calm. That phrase is going to matter a great deal tonight, and we'll come back to it before midnight.

Lightoller also remembered that he and the captain talked about the ice that had been reported ahead, and about the conditions for seeing it. And then, at some point not long after nine, Captain Smith left the bridge to go to his own quarters nearby. Before he went, Lightoller recalled, the captain told him, in effect, that if it became at all doubtful, he was to let him know at once, and that he would be just inside. I'm paraphrasing that, rather than quoting it word for word, because the versions of the line passed down in later books and articles differ a little, and I'd rather be honest with you than tidy. But the sense of it is clear. The captain was not far away. If anything changed, he was to be called.

And here's the answer to our small question. Lightoller testified that he sent a message to the men in the crow's nest, asking them to keep a sharp lookout for ice, and particularly for small ice and growlers. So what is a growler? It's a small piece of ice, broken off from an iceberg, floating low in the water, perhaps no bigger than a cottage or a grand piano, with most of its mass hidden below the surface. Growlers are dangerous precisely because they're so low. They don't stand up against the sky the way a great berg does. And the name, sailors say, comes from the sound they can make as they roll and grind in a swell, a low, rumbling growl. Tonight, of course, there's no swell at all. Tonight, they'll make no sound.

Lightoller did one other small, practical thing in this hour that tells us how cold it was getting. As the temperature dropped towards freezing, he sent word to the ship's carpenter to look after the fresh water supply, so that the pipes and tanks wouldn't freeze. That's the kind of thing an officer does on a cold night at sea. Routine. Sensible. Nothing to alarm anyone.

Now, here's a place where the two inquiries afterwards came to different conclusions, and where people still disagree today.

Why was the Titanic still steaming at something like twenty-two knots, nearly her full speed, into waters where ice had been reported? The British inquiry, led by Lord Mersey, the Wreck Commissioner, found that this was the usual practice of the great liners at the time. Ships kept up their speed in clear weather, trusting the lookouts to see ice in time, and the inquiry said, in essence, that the captain had made a very serious mistake, but one that many other experienced captains would have made too, and that it could not be called negligence. The American inquiry, led by Senator William Alden Smith of Michigan, was much harsher. It spoke of overconfidence, and of an indifference to danger that it thought had become far too common on the North Atlantic. Historians and maritime writers have weighed those two views ever since, and many would say both are partly true. The practice was common. And the practice was wrong. It took this night to prove it.

I'm giving you both inquiries' findings in my own words, by the way, because their full wording is long and legal, and I'd rather give you the sense of it fairly than a fragment out of context.

Let's set this beside our own lives for a moment. When you drive at night, you have headlights that throw a beam of bright white light a long way down the road ahead. On a modern ship, the radar screen glows on the bridge, sweeping round and round, showing every obstacle for miles. Lightoller has neither. He has his own eyes, two men up the mast with theirs, a calm sea, and the stars. If you've ever walked down a country lane on a moonless night, with no torch, trying to see a dark gate across the path, you have some idea of what it was like to look for ice from this bridge.

Back down in the ship, the evening is quietly drawing in.

Lawrence Beesley, our science teacher, has gone to his cabin on D deck. He'll tell us later that he was lying in his bunk, reading. In the smoking rooms, men are playing cards and talking business. In the lounges, people are writing letters to post in New York, letters that will never be posted. In the third-class areas, down near the bottom of the ship and at either end, families from Ireland and Sweden and Syria and a dozen other lands are settling into their cabins. And in the wireless room, Jack Phillips is working.

He's busy. Very busy. Harold Bride later explained, in the account he gave to the New York Times after the rescue, that the wireless set had developed a fault the night before, and that he and Phillips had spent hours finding and mending it. So now there's a backlog of passengers' messages to send. And tonight, at last, the Titanic is within range of a powerful wireless station on the coast of Newfoundland, at Cape Race. So Phillips is sending, and sending, message after message, greetings and business and arrangements for arrival, clicking out the Morse code at speed, while the replies come crackling back. Bride has gone to bed, to rest before taking over his shift at midnight. Phillips is alone at the key.

And far below him, at the very bottom of the ship, there's another world entirely, one that most of the passengers will never see.

The hum that Daniel feels through the soles of his feet comes from here. The Titanic is driven by steam, and the steam comes from twenty-nine enormous boilers, arranged in rows in six boiler rooms along the bottom of the hull, fed by a hundred and fifty-nine furnaces. Every one of those furnaces has to be fed with coal, by hand, with a shovel, all day and all night. The men who do it are called firemen, or stokers, and the men who wheel the coal to them from the bunkers are called trimmers. They work in shifts, four hours on and eight hours off, in a heat that can be fierce, stripped to their vests, their faces black with coal dust, in the red glare of the open furnace doors. By the usual estimates, a ship like this burned something like six hundred tons of coal in a single day at sea.

It's worth pausing on that, for a moment, beside your own home. When you turn up the heating, or switch on a light, the energy arrives silently, through a pipe or a wire, from somewhere you never see. On the Titanic, every bit of warmth in Daniel's cabin, every electric lamp in the saloon, every turn of the great propellers, came in the end from a man with a shovel, far down in the heat, feeding a fire. Several hundred of them worked down there. And many of them, too, were young men from Southampton, from the same streets as Joseph Wren.

Joseph himself is off duty now, for a little while. The second-class stewards, like the rest of the crew, live in crowded quarters of their own, deep in the ship, with rows of narrow bunks. He's sitting on his, polishing his shoes, and talking with two of the other stewards about the girl he's courting at home, and about what they'll do with their few hours ashore in New York. He has a younger sister and a widowed mother in Southampton. Most of his wages go to them. He is thinking, as he polishes, that the Hollis children are good children, and that the little boy really ought to see the engines, and that perhaps tomorrow he could ask one of the engineers.

Captain Smith, for his part, has been at sea for more than forty years. He'd commanded many of the White Star Line's finest ships, including the Titanic's elder sister, the Olympic, on her maiden voyage the year before. He was known among wealthy passengers as a calm, courteous, reassuring presence, and among his officers as a fine seaman. Whatever was decided about him afterwards, there is no record that anyone on board, at this hour, felt the slightest unease about the man in command.

Let me take you to one small moment of this evening, in the Hollis family's cabin, because it's the last quiet hour that Daniel and Ellen will have for a long time.

Imagine you're sitting on the little folding seat by the washbasin, in the soft light of the single electric lamp. The children are asleep, all three. May has kicked off her blanket, and Ellen has tucked it back. Rose is lying on her back with one arm flung over her face. Tom is curled up against the wall, his face towards the porthole. The steady hum of the ship surrounds you like a cradle. And Daniel and Ellen are sitting side by side on the edge of the lower bunk, talking in low voices.

They talk about Toronto. About the house Daniel's brother has found for them, with its four rooms and its yard. About whether the winters there are as cold as everyone says. About Ellen's mother, and the letter they'll send her from New York to tell her they've arrived safely. About Rose, who will need a new school. About Tom, who wants to be a sailor now, of course, after four days on this ship. About May's cough, which is better. About nothing much.

Daniel takes his watch out again, and holds it in the palm of his hand, and Ellen smiles, because he does this all the time. It's ten past nine. And because she's in the mood to tease him, she asks what time it is in Reading. He thinks about it. It would be past midnight in England, he says. Her mother will be asleep. And Ellen goes quiet for a moment, and then says that's nice, to think of her asleep, safe in her bed, and of them all safe in theirs, so far apart.

There's another kind of record I'd like to tell you about now, because it's the reason we know so much about who was on this ship. Every passenger who sailed on the Titanic had their name written down: on the ship's passenger lists, on the tickets, on the contract tickets of the White Star Line. Many of those records survive. And in the years since, researchers have built up, passenger by passenger, a remarkable record of the people on board. Who they were, where they came from, why they were travelling, and what became of them. One of the largest of these efforts is the online database called Encyclopedia Titanica, which has gathered biographies of almost every person aboard. If the Hollis family had been real, you would find them there.

And that's how we know, for example, that there were children of many ages aboard tonight, in all three classes. Babies, toddlers, schoolchildren. Some of them, like Tom, were curious about everything. Some, like May, slept through everything. And in second class, as we'll see by morning, something remarkable happened to every single one of them. But that is for later.

Up on the bridge, Lightoller walks from one side to the other, and back again. He looks out at the flat, black, starlit sea. The temperature, by his own account, has dropped to about freezing now, and it will drop a little more before he hands over his watch at ten o'clock.

Down in the cabin, Daniel winds his watch one last time, very gently, and lays it on the little shelf above the washbasin, beside Ellen's hairbrush, where he can reach it in the morning. It ticks there, very quietly, in the warm, humming dark.

Ellen turns out the lamp. And the cabin is dark, except for the faint grey circle of the porthole, full of stars.

## Part Four — Around Ten O'Clock: The Lookouts Change

Here is the small question for this hour. How long does a man stand in the crow's nest of a great liner on a freezing night, before someone comes to take his place?

At ten o'clock, the answer climbs up the mast.

The crow's nest of the Titanic is a small, round, open steel platform, fixed to the front of the foremast, high above the deck, and higher still above the sea. You reach it by climbing a ladder inside the hollow mast itself, up through the dark, and out through a little door at the top. There's room inside for two men to stand side by side, with a rail around them at about chest height, a bell above their heads, and a telephone that connects them directly to the bridge.

Imagine you're climbing up behind them now, rung over rung, in the dark tube of the mast, with the cold seeping through your gloves. You come out at the top, into the open air, and the cold takes your breath away completely. The ship is moving at more than twenty knots, and up here, nothing shelters you from the wind she makes as she goes. It's below freezing. The sea is so far beneath you that it seems to be another sky, black and full of reflected stars. And ahead of you, there is nothing. Just darkness, and the faint line where the stars stop.

The two men coming on watch are Frederick Fleet and Reginald Lee. Fleet is twenty-four, a seaman who has been a lookout on White Star ships before. Lee is a little older. They take over from the two men who've been up here since eight o'clock, and those two pass on the message that Lightoller sent up earlier: keep a sharp lookout for ice, particularly small ice and growlers. Then they climb down, gratefully, to the warmth.

That's the answer to our small question. A lookout's watch in the crow's nest lasted two hours. Fleet and Lee will stand here, in the cold, from ten o'clock until midnight, looking forward into the dark.

At the same hour, on the bridge below them, Lightoller hands over the watch to the first officer, William Murdoch. Murdoch is thirty-nine, a Scot, calm, careful and highly regarded. Lightoller, by his own account, passed on what he knew: the ice reports, the captain's instruction, the cold. And then he went off to make his rounds of the ship, and then to his cabin, and then to bed.

And so now, at last, with the lookouts in their places, we've come to the first of our three mysteries. I asked you at the very beginning of the night: on a night that was perfectly clear, with more stars in the sky than most of us will ever see, why was it so very hard for two sharp-eyed men in the crow's nest to see a mountain of ice in front of them?

The answer has three parts. And the strange thing is that every one of them is something that would, on any other night, seem like a blessing.

The first part is the darkness. There is no moon tonight. On a moonlit night, an iceberg shines. Its white face catches the light and stands out against the black water like a sail. Tonight there's only starlight, which is beautiful, but very faint.

The second part is the calm. And this is the one that the officers themselves came back to again and again at the inquiries. On a normal night in the North Atlantic, there's always some swell, some ripple, some wind on the water. And when waves meet the foot of an iceberg, they break against it, and that breaking water makes a line of white foam around the base of the berg, a pale ring that a lookout can see from a long way off, even in the dark. Tonight, Lightoller had told the captain, it was a flat calm. Hardly a ripple. So there was no white water at the base of the ice. Nothing to catch the eye. An iceberg tonight would sit on the sea like a dark shape on dark glass.

And the third part is the berg itself. Not every iceberg is a brilliant white. An iceberg that has recently rolled over in the water, turning a fresh, wet face upwards, can look dark, even blackish, in the night, until you're close to it. Some of the survivors who saw this one described it as dark rather than white.

So that's our first mystery solved. On a moonless night, on a sea as flat as a millpond, the very calm that made this such a pleasant crossing also took away the one thing that usually gave an iceberg away at night: the white water breaking at its foot. The clearest of nights was, for a lookout, one of the hardest.

Now here's where it gets more complicated, and where people still disagree, quite sharply.

The first disagreement is about binoculars. Fleet maintained, at both the American and the British inquiries, that if he and Lee had had binoculars, they would have seen the iceberg sooner. There had been a pair for the crow's nest earlier, but none were available to the lookouts on this voyage, and neither inquiry ever fully explained why. Many experienced seamen, however, have argued that binoculars would have made little difference. A lookout's job is to scan the whole horizon with the naked eye and spot something, and only then to raise glasses to look at it more closely. Binoculars narrow your view. On a night like this, many experts think, they might not have helped at all.

The second disagreement is about the horizon itself. Reginald Lee, at the British inquiry, spoke of a haze lying ahead on the horizon in the minutes before the collision. Fleet, too, spoke of a slight haze. Other witnesses, on the bridge and elsewhere, said the night was perfectly clear. In more recent years, the British historian Tim Maltin, in a book called A Very Deceiving Night, published in 2012, has argued that the cold water of the Labrador Current, lying under warmer air, may have created a kind of optical illusion, an unusual bending of light near the horizon, that blurred the line between sea and sky and could have both hidden the berg and confused other ships watching from a distance. Other researchers are not convinced, and think the simple explanations, darkness and calm, are enough. The debate goes on.

What's certain is that Fleet and Lee are standing in their nest now, at about ten past ten, looking ahead into the darkness, with their hands in their pockets and their collars up, and that for the next hour and a half, they see nothing at all.

Now let me take you back about half an hour, to the wireless room, because something happened there at about twenty to ten that the inquiries afterwards came back to again and again.

At about nine forty, Jack Phillips received a message from a ship called the Mesaba, a liner of the Atlantic Transport Line, which was crossing the same waters. It was addressed to the Titanic and to all eastbound ships. And because the British inquiry printed it in full in its report, we can read part of it word for word. It gave a position, roughly between forty-two degrees and forty-one degrees twenty-five minutes north, and between forty-nine and fifty degrees thirty minutes west, and then it said, in the clipped language of telegrams: "saw much heavy pack ice and great number large icebergs, also field ice. Weather good, clear."

Those positions lay directly across the Titanic's path. And the British inquiry concluded that this message, if it had reached the bridge, might perhaps have affected the navigation of the ship. But as far as the inquiry could find, it was never delivered to the captain or to any of the officers.

Why not? Nobody can be completely sure. Phillips did not survive to explain. But the most likely answer is the one we've already seen. He was alone. He was exhausted after the long night of repairs. He was in the middle of his long backlog of passengers' messages to Cape Race. Perhaps he set the Mesaba's message aside, meaning to take it to the bridge as soon as he could. Perhaps he thought it was like the others already received. We simply don't know.

Let's think about that in modern terms. Today, if a warning is sent to a ship, an aircraft, or a phone, it can flash up automatically on a screen in front of exactly the right person. Alarms sound. Nothing depends on one tired young man in a small room deciding when to stand up from his desk and walk forty feet to the bridge. In 1912, wireless was so new that there were no rules at all about which messages had to go to the captain first. That too would change because of this night.

And one more message is still to come.

At around eleven o'clock, the Californian, the same small Leyland liner whose ice warning Bride had heard earlier, had stopped for the night. She'd come up against a great field of ice, and her captain, Stanley Lord, had decided not to try to push through it in the dark, but to wait for daylight. Her one wireless operator, a young man named Cyril Evans, was told to let other ships know. So he called the Titanic, to say, informally, that they were stopped and surrounded by ice.

He was so close that his signal must have been very loud in Phillips's headphones. And Phillips, in the middle of working Cape Race, cut him off. At the inquiries, Evans said that Phillips told him, in effect, to keep out, because he was busy working Cape Race. I'm paraphrasing there, because accounts of the exact words differ, and one detail that often gets left out is that Evans himself said, in London, that he hadn't taken it as an insult at all. That sort of reply was common between operators. Evans listened a little longer, heard Phillips still working Cape Race, and then, at around half past eleven by his own ship's clock, he took off his headphones and went to bed.

The Californian lay stopped, in the ice, a few miles away. Somewhere within sight of the Titanic, or perhaps not. That too is a debate we'll come to.

While the ship sails on, I'd like to tell you a little about the ice itself, because it has made a much longer journey than any passenger on board.

Almost all of the icebergs that drift into these waters began as snow, falling on the great ice sheet that covers Greenland. Over hundreds or thousands of years, that snow was pressed down by more snow above it, until it became solid glacier ice, dense and blue-white and full of tiny bubbles of ancient air. Slowly, the glaciers flowed down towards the sea, the way honey flows off a spoon, only far, far slower. And where they met the water, along the coast of western Greenland, great pieces broke off and fell into the sea with a roar. That's called calving.

Then the journey began. The new icebergs drifted north at first, around the waters of Baffin Bay, and then south, carried by the cold Labrador Current, down past the coast of Labrador and Newfoundland, towards the Grand Banks. A journey like that can take a year or more, and most icebergs melt or run aground long before they reach the shipping lanes. But some survive. And when they meet the warmer water of the Gulf Stream, south of Newfoundland, they start to melt fast, and break up into smaller bergs and growlers. That meeting place, where the cold current meets the warm, is very close to where the Titanic is sailing tonight.

One more true thing about icebergs, which you may already know. Ice is only a little lighter than seawater, so only a small part of an iceberg floats above the surface. Most of it, something like seven-eighths or more, lies hidden below. A berg that stands sixty feet high above the water may run down a long way under it, and spread out wide beneath the surface, in ledges and spurs that no one on a ship could ever see.

So the ice drifting somewhere ahead of the Titanic tonight may be older than any building in Reading. Older, perhaps, than the oldest church in England. It fell as snow on Greenland long before anyone on this ship was born, long before anyone had dreamed of steamships, or wireless, or electric light.

And now it lies quietly on the flat black sea, waiting.

Down in the Hollis family's cabin, everyone is asleep. Ellen and Daniel have the two lower bunks now, May tucked in with her mother. On the shelf over the washbasin, beside the hairbrush, the silver watch is ticking in the dark. Tick, tick, tick, tick. It's twenty-five past eleven.

Lawrence Beesley is still awake in his cabin, reading. And in a few minutes, he'll notice something. Not a crash. Not a bang. Just something, under the steady hum of the engines, that feels a little different.

## Part Five — Around Twenty to Twelve: The Ice

Here is the small question for this part of the night, and the answer may surprise you. When the largest moving object ever built by human beings struck an iceberg in the middle of the ocean, what did it actually feel like to the people asleep inside her?

Let's go back up to the crow's nest, to find out how it began.

It's about twenty to twelve. Fleet and Lee have been up here for an hour and a half. They're cold right through, stamping their feet, looking ahead into the dark, as they have been all along. In twenty minutes, they'll be relieved, and they'll climb down to hot drinks and their bunks.

And then Fleet sees something.

Right ahead of the ship, on the line where the stars meet the sea, there's a dark shape. Small at first, and then, very quickly, not small.

At the American inquiry, a month later, Frederick Fleet told the senators exactly what he did. First, he struck the bell above his head three times. Three bells, he explained, meant an object right ahead. Then he picked up the telephone to the bridge. Someone on the bridge answered and asked what he had seen. And Fleet told him: "iceberg right ahead." The voice on the bridge thanked him. And Fleet put the telephone down.

That's all. Just three words. Fleet was asked again and again afterwards how far away the berg had been when he first saw it, and how big. He did his honest best. But he also told the inquiry, simply, that he was not a good judge of distance. And who could blame him? He'd had a few seconds, in the dark, with no way to measure anything.

Down on the bridge, Murdoch acted at once. At the American inquiry, the quartermaster at the wheel, a man named Robert Hichens, testified that the order he was given was "hard a-starboard." That sounds, to modern ears, as though it means turn right. But in 1912, the old sailing-ship habit of giving orders in terms of the tiller was still in use, and so "hard a-starboard" meant, in practice, swing the ship's head to port, to the left. Hichens spun the wheel as far as it would go. And Murdoch, according to the accounts of the men on the bridge, rang down to the engine rooms to stop the engines, and then, it seems, to reverse them.

And for a long, slow moment, perhaps half a minute, nothing seemed to happen at all. A ship of over forty-six thousand tons, moving at more than twenty knots, does not turn quickly. Then, very slowly, the great bow began to swing to the left. The lookouts in the crow's nest watched the berg come nearer and nearer, and then begin to slide past on the right-hand side. Fleet and Lee said afterwards that, for a moment, they thought she might clear it.

She didn't. The ice struck her along the starboard side, below the waterline, near the bow, and scraped along it, as the ship swung past. Up on the forward well deck, chunks of ice broke off the berg and fell onto the deck with a clatter. And then the berg was gone, sliding away behind into the dark, a great black shape against the stars, and the Titanic moved on, slowing.

It was about eleven forty, ship's time. A Sunday night, the fourteenth of April, 1912.

Now let's go down, deep into the ship, and find out what it felt like.

Imagine that you're asleep in a bunk in the Hollis family's cabin. You're warm. You're deep in sleep, the heavy sleep of a long day at sea and a big dinner. And then, somewhere at the edge of your sleep, something changes. It's very small. A faint grinding, perhaps, far away, like a train going over a set of points. A slight shiver through the frame of the bunk, as if someone had bumped into it. You half wake, and listen. And then you notice something else, something much bigger, that was there all the time and suddenly isn't.

The hum has stopped.

For four days, the engines have been turning beneath you, day and night, a steady, deep, comforting vibration in everything you touch. You stopped hearing it on the first evening. Now it has gone, and the silence where it used to be is so strange that it wakes you more surely than any bang.

That's the answer to our small question, and it's one of the most remarkable things about this whole night. For most of the people on board, especially those further back in the ship, the collision itself was barely felt at all. Some felt a slight jar. Some heard a grinding, or a sound like tearing calico. Some slept straight through it. Many said that what woke them, or made them sit up and wonder, was not the blow but the sudden quiet when the engines stopped.

Lawrence Beesley was one of them. He was still reading in his bunk, he tells us, and he felt what seemed to be just an extra heave of the engines, a slightly different motion from the usual. Nothing more. There was no shock, no jolt to alarm anyone. And then he noticed that the engines had stopped. I'm paraphrasing his description, but the calm of it is exactly right. He got up, put on a dressing gown, and went up to the deck to see what was the matter. And what he found there, at first, was nothing at all. A few other passengers, who'd come up to look. The cold. The stars. A flat, empty sea. No sign of anything wrong. So, being a sensible man and very cold, he went back down to his cabin, and back to his book.

Up in first class, a little further forward, a man named Archibald Gracie was asleep in his cabin. He was an American, a retired army colonel and amateur historian, fifty-four years old, travelling home from Europe. He survived this night, and he spent the months that followed writing it all down, interviewing other survivors, and gathering their letters and statements, until he'd built up one of the most detailed accounts we have. His book was published in 1913 as The Truth About the Titanic. Sadly, Gracie himself didn't live to see it in print. He died in December 1912, his health never having recovered from this night. But his book, with all its patient detail, is still one of the great sources.

Gracie remembered being woken by a sudden shock, and a noise, and getting up and dressing and going out on deck to look. Like Beesley, he found nothing obviously wrong. I'm paraphrasing again, because I want to be careful with the exact wording of a book I can't hold open in front of me. But it's striking how alike the earliest moments were for so many people in every part of the ship. A small shock. A strange noise. The engines stopped. A look out on deck at a cold, calm, empty sea. And then, very often, back to bed.

The fourth officer, Joseph Boxhall, had been close to the bridge when it happened. He arrived there in time to hear Murdoch telling the captain about the iceberg, and then he went down, on the captain's orders, to look at the front of the ship. He told the inquiries that at first he found no damage at all, as far forward as he could get. It was only a little later, as reports came up from the men deeper down, that the officers began to understand.

So in these first ten minutes, the people who were closest to the danger, the people on the bridge, were still finding out. And the people furthest from it were mostly asleep.

Daniel Hollis wakes in the dark of the cabin and lies there listening, the way you do when something in the night is different but you can't think what. Then he realises. The engines. He gets up, feels for the shelf over the washbasin, finds his watch, and carries it to the porthole to read it by the starlight. Twenty minutes to twelve. He holds it to his ear. Tick, tick, tick. At least something on this ship is still running, he thinks, and almost smiles.

Ellen whispers to ask what's wrong. He says he doesn't know. Perhaps they've stopped for something. Perhaps a fault with the engines. He'll go and see. And he pulls on his trousers and his coat over his nightshirt, and opens the door into the corridor.

It's bright out there, still lit by the electric lamps, and almost empty. A few doors are open. A man in a dressing gown is standing in his doorway, looking puzzled. A woman further along asks Daniel if he knows why they've stopped, and he says he doesn't. And then, coming quickly along the corridor from the stairs, still in his white jacket, is Joseph Wren.

Daniel asks him what's happening. And Joseph, who knows no more than anyone else, says honestly that he's heard they may have touched some ice, but that there's nothing to worry about, and that they should go back to bed and keep warm. He believes it, too. Nobody has told the stewards anything else. Not yet.

Now let's set this beside our own world for a moment. If you're on a modern train or plane and something unexpected happens, the chances are that within a minute or two a voice will come over the loudspeaker to tell everyone, calmly, what's going on. On the Titanic there were no loudspeakers. There was no public address system at all. Every piece of news, every instruction, had to be carried by hand and by mouth, from the bridge to the officers, from the officers to the stewards, and from the stewards, door by door, cabin by cabin, to more than two thousand people spread over a ship nearly nine hundred feet long. That slow passing of news by word of mouth is going to shape much of what happens tonight.

And here's a place where people have disagreed for more than a century.

What if the Titanic hadn't turned at all? What if she'd hit the iceberg head-on? It's been suggested many times, by naval architects and by writers on the disaster, that a head-on collision, terrible as it would have been for anyone in the very front of the ship, might have crushed only the first one or two watertight compartments, and that the ship might then have stayed afloat. Others have answered that this is very easy to say with hindsight, and that no officer in Murdoch's place, with a few seconds to act, could have chosen to drive his ship straight into an iceberg. And there's a second, related argument, about the engines. Some have suggested that reversing the engines made the ship turn more slowly, because a ship's rudder works best with the propellers driving water past it. Others point out that we can't even be completely sure, from the conflicting testimony of the men on the bridge and in the engine rooms, exactly which orders were given, and when. It's one of those arguments that can never be settled, because the only test would be to live that half minute again.

On the forward well deck, under the stars, some of the third-class passengers, who've come up from their quarters at the front of the ship to see what's happened, have found the chunks of ice that fell from the berg. And, being people, some of them have started to play with them. Kicking them along the deck like footballs. Throwing small pieces at one another. Laughing in the cold.

It's one of the most often-remembered pictures of this night, and I think it tells us everything about these first few minutes. Nobody is afraid. Not yet. A ship this size, this new, this famous, simply cannot be in serious trouble. Can she?

Daniel goes back into the cabin and closes the door. Ellen is sitting up now, May asleep against her. Rose is awake, peering down from the upper bunk with wide eyes. Tom, astonishingly, is still asleep. Daniel tells them what Joseph told him. Some ice, perhaps. Nothing to worry about. Go back to sleep.

Rose asks if they've hit an iceberg. Daniel says he doesn't think so, and that if they had, he's sure they'd have felt it more. Rose says, quietly, that she felt the bunk shiver. Daniel says that was probably just the engines stopping. And Rose, who is eleven, and sharp, and who has read every notice on this ship, lies back down. But she doesn't close her eyes.

Daniel sits on the edge of his bunk, in the dark, holding his father's watch in his hand. He can hear voices in the corridor now, more of them than before, and doors opening and closing. The ship is very still beneath him. Stiller than she's been for four days. He looks at the watch again. Eleven fifty.

Up on the bridge, Captain Smith has come out of his quarters, and is standing beside Murdoch. And somewhere below the waterline, in the darkness at the front of the ship, the sea is coming in.

## Part Six — Around Midnight: The Call for Help

The small question for this hour is about three letters. When a wireless operator in 1912 tapped out the letters C, Q and D, what did they actually mean?

We'll come to that in the wireless room. But first, let's go up to the bridge, in the minutes just after the collision.

Captain Smith, as the officers later described it, came out onto the bridge almost at once and asked Murdoch what had happened. Murdoch told him they'd struck an iceberg. The watertight doors deep in the ship had already been closed, from the bridge, with the flick of a switch. And then the captain sent for the carpenter to sound the ship, that is, to measure how much water was coming in, and he went himself, with others, to see.

With him, or close behind, was a man named Thomas Andrews. He was thirty-nine, from County Down in Ireland, and he was the managing director of design at Harland and Wolff, the Belfast shipyard that had built the Titanic. He knew this ship better than anyone alive. He'd sailed on her maiden voyage with a small team from the yard, to watch how she behaved, and to make a list of small improvements for the future. Survivors later remembered him walking the ship all through the voyage with a notebook, checking everything, from the hooks in the cabins to the colour of the paint.

Now he and the captain went down, through the ship, to see what the ice had done.

I'm not going to put any words into their mouths, because we don't have a reliable record of exactly what they said to one another. But we know, from the inquiries and from the accounts of the officers who spoke with them afterwards, what they found. The ice had opened the ship's side along a length of the starboard bow, below the waterline. Water was coming in to the forepeak, to the first three holds, and into the first of the boiler rooms. The British inquiry concluded that the damage extended across the first five of the ship's watertight compartments, and into a sixth.

And that was the whole problem, very simply put. The Titanic had been divided by her builders into sixteen watertight compartments, separated by steel walls called bulkheads. She was designed so that she could float with any two of those compartments flooded, and even, in the case of the forward ones, with the first four flooded. That's the design that gave rise to all the talk of her being practically unsinkable. But not five. With five compartments open to the sea at the front, the bow would slowly be pulled down by the weight of the water, and as it went down, the water would rise over the tops of the bulkheads, which did not reach all the way up to the top deck, and spill into the next compartment, and then the next. Like water filling an ice-cube tray tilted at one end.

Later surveys of the wreck have suggested that the damage was not a great long gash, as was often imagined, but a series of fairly narrow openings, where the hull's steel plates had buckled and their rivets had given way under the pressure of the ice. It didn't need to be large. It only needed to be in the wrong place.

The accounts agree that Andrews worked out quickly, perhaps within a quarter of an hour, that the ship could not be saved. She would sink. Not at once, but within a couple of hours. And the captain gave the orders that followed. Uncover the boats. Get the passengers up, with their lifebelts on. And send for help.

Now, here we come to the wireless room, and our small question.

At some point around a quarter past twelve, Captain Smith came to the wireless room himself, and gave Phillips the ship's position, and told him to send the call for assistance. And the call that Phillips sent was CQD.

So what does it mean? You'll often hear that CQD stood for "Come Quick, Danger." It's a nice story, but it isn't quite right. CQ was the general call used by wireless operators, a signal meaning "all stations," attention, anyone listening. And the D was simply added to mean distress. So CQD meant, in effect, all stations, distress. It was the standard distress call of the Marconi company in 1912. There was also a newer international distress signal, three dots, three dashes, three dots, which we know as SOS, which had been agreed internationally a few years before, and was beginning to come into use. Harold Bride later remembered that, later in the night, he suggested to Phillips, half in jest, that he should try sending the new call too, because it might be his last chance to send it. I'm paraphrasing his memory of that moment, because the wording in the newspaper versions differs. But the Titanic did send both, and she is often remembered as one of the first ships ever to use SOS in a real emergency.

That's our answer. Three letters meaning simply: everyone listening, we are in distress.

And somebody was listening. Just.

About fifty-eight miles away, on the Cunard liner Carpathia, steaming east towards the Mediterranean, the ship's only wireless operator, Harold Cottam, was getting ready for bed. He was twenty-one. His day's work was over. His captain, Arthur Rostron, said afterwards, at the American inquiry, that his operator had been in his cabin, not on duty at all, simply listening as he got undressed. It was pure chance that he was still at his instrument.

Cottam had heard that there were messages waiting at Cape Race for the Titanic, and, in a friendly way, he called her up to let her know. And the reply came back at once. At the American inquiry, Cottam remembered the first message as: "Come at once. We have struck a berg. It is a C.Q.D."

He asked whether he should tell his captain. He was told yes, at once.

Imagine, for a moment, that you're Harold Cottam, half-undressed, in a tiny cabin on a ship in the middle of the night, with a pair of headphones over your ears, and those words coming through in Morse, dot by dot, from the largest ship in the world. You'd sit very still for a second, I think, not quite believing it. Then you'd run.

And here's a place where the times disagree, as they so often do tonight. Different accounts give different times for that first exchange between the two ships. Some say twenty past twelve, some twenty-five past, Titanic time. Cottam himself, at the American inquiry, gave the time in New York time, as about twenty past eleven, which shows you, once again, how many clocks were running that night. And historians who have studied the wireless logs of the other ships that heard the calls, such as the researchers who've reconstructed the night's timeline in detail, still argue over the minutes. What everyone agrees on is the order of events. The call went out. Carpathia heard it. And Carpathia came.

Cottam ran up to the bridge, and then, finding only the officer of the watch there, down to the captain's cabin, and woke Arthur Rostron. Rostron was forty-two, a quiet, deeply religious, very precise man, who'd been in command of the Carpathia for only a few months. And within moments, by his own account, he'd ordered the ship turned round and set on a course towards the Titanic's position. Only after he'd given the order, he said later, did he ask Cottam whether he was absolutely sure.

He was.

And now, before we go back to our family, I want to tell you plainly about the lifeboats, because there's no way to understand the rest of this night without it.

On the boat deck of the Titanic, there were twenty boats. Fourteen of them were large wooden lifeboats, each built to carry sixty-five people. Two were smaller wooden boats called emergency cutters, kept ready at the front of the boat deck, one on each side, for things like a man overboard, each holding forty. And four were what were called collapsibles, Engelhardt boats with wooden bottoms and canvas sides that could be folded down flat and raised when needed, each meant to hold forty-seven. Two of those collapsibles were stowed on the deck beside the cutters. The other two were lashed upside down on the roof of the officers' quarters, near the first funnel, where they would be very hard to reach.

Add them all up, and the boats could hold, in all, about one thousand one hundred and seventy-eight people.

There were more than two thousand two hundred people on board.

How could that be allowed? The answer is that the rules had not kept up with the ships. The British Board of Trade regulations in force in 1912 had been drawn up in the 1890s, when the largest ships were far smaller than this one, and they set the number of lifeboats a ship must carry according to its tonnage, on a scale that simply stopped at ships of ten thousand tons and over. A ship of forty-six thousand tons was treated, in effect, the same as one of ten thousand. By those rules, the Titanic actually carried more lifeboat capacity than the law required of her. She was entirely legal. And at the same time, if every seat in every boat were filled, there was room for only a little over half of the people aboard.

Nobody on the Titanic tonight, apart from a very few officers, has yet done that sum. And the sea is calm, and the ship is still bright with light, and help, everyone assumes, will not be far away.

Now, back on the Titanic, the news is beginning to move through the ship. Slowly, by word of mouth, door by door, as we've seen.

Joseph Wren has been called by the chief second-class steward and told what to do. Go to your cabins. Knock on every door. Tell the passengers to dress warmly, put on their lifebelts, and go up to the boat deck. Calmly. No alarm. Just a precaution. And Joseph, who has been told nothing more than that, and who still believes it's a precaution, goes down the corridor, knocking.

He comes to the Hollis family's door. Daniel opens it at once. Joseph tells him, as kindly as he can, what he's been told to say. And then he reaches up to the top of the wardrobe, where the lifebelts are kept, and takes them down, and shows Daniel how to put them on.

Here's what they're like. They're not inflatable, like the life jackets on a modern aircraft. They're made of slabs of cork, sewn into canvas, a stiff, bulky sort of waistcoat that goes over your head, with a block of cork at the front and another at the back, tied at the sides with tapes. They're heavy and awkward, and they make you look twice as wide as you are. There are smaller ones for the children, though May's still looks enormous on her.

Let's set that beside your own life. If you've flown in a plane, you've sat through the safety announcement, with the steward showing how to put on the life jacket and where the exits are. On the Titanic, there had been no lifeboat drill for the passengers on this voyage. Nobody had shown them where their boat was, or which boat was theirs, or how to put on a lifebelt. So Joseph, a twenty-three-year-old steward, kneels on the floor of a small cabin and ties the tapes of a cork lifebelt round a sleepy four-year-old girl, and tells her she looks like a little ship herself, and she giggles.

Ellen dresses the children in everything warm they have. Vests, jerseys, stockings, coats. Rose puts on two pairs of stockings without being told. Tom, awake at last, and delighted, wants to know if they're going in a lifeboat, and whether he can row. Ellen pins her hat on, and takes a shawl, and then, after a moment's thought, a second shawl, and a little bag with some biscuits in it from yesterday's tea.

And Daniel takes his watch from the shelf. He looks at it for a moment. A quarter past twelve, or near enough. And he puts it, not in his waistcoat pocket, but deep in the inside pocket of his coat, buttoned up, under the lifebelt, close to his chest. He doesn't know why he does that. He just does.

Then the family goes out into the corridor, and joins the slow, sleepy, puzzled stream of people moving towards the stairs. Some are laughing. Some are complaining about being woken. One man is in evening dress, and another in pyjamas and an overcoat. A woman is carrying a small dog. And somewhere above them all, very faintly at first, then louder and louder, there's a sound like a great roaring.

It's the steam. The ship's boilers, no longer needed for the engines, are blowing off their steam through the pipes on the funnels, high above the decks, with a deafening, endless, thunderous roar. It will go on for a long time, so loud that, up on deck, people will have to shout to be heard. And for most of the people on this ship, it's the first sign that something may be really wrong.

## Part Seven — Around a Quarter to One: The First Boats

Here is the small question for this hour. In the days before radio was everywhere, how did a ship in trouble at night call to another ship that she could see, but could not reach by wireless?

The answer is about to rise into the sky. But let's come up onto the boat deck first, with the Hollis family.

Imagine that you're stepping out through the doors at the top of the second-class staircase, onto the open boat deck, at about half past twelve. The cold hits you first, harder than you expected, through your coat and your cork lifebelt and everything else you're wearing. Then the noise. The steam is still roaring from the pipes on the funnels, high above you, a vast, unending thunder, so loud that you can't hear what Ellen is saying, even though she's right beside you. Then the light. The deck lamps are all lit, and every window of the ship is still blazing, and in the glow you see the lifeboats, white-painted, hanging from their curved iron davits along the edge of the deck. Crewmen are at work around them, pulling off the canvas covers, freeing the ropes, swinging the boats out over the side, so that they hang in the air over the sea. And all around you, people. Hundreds of them. Standing in little groups, in coats and furs and dressing gowns and lifebelts, talking, waiting, stamping their feet against the cold. Very few look frightened. Most look puzzled, or annoyed, or simply cold.

You go to the rail and look down. The sea is a very long way below. Seventy feet, perhaps, or more. Black, and flat, and perfectly calm, reflecting the stars and the long double row of the ship's lights. There's nothing out there at all.

Tom wants to see the boats being swung out. Daniel lifts him up. May is in Ellen's arms, wrapped in both shawls now, and sound asleep again in her little cork lifebelt. Rose stands close against her father's side, holding on to his coat.

Then, at about a quarter to one, from somewhere near the bridge, there comes a sudden hiss and a bang. And a streak of light goes racing up into the sky, high, high above the funnels, and bursts into a shower of white stars, which hang there for a moment, and drift, and fade.

That's the answer to our small question. When wireless couldn't reach a ship, a ship in trouble at night would fire distress rockets. White ones. The Titanic carried socket signals, rockets that were fired from a special socket on the rail of the bridge, and burst high in the air with a loud report and a cluster of white stars. The fourth officer, Joseph Boxhall, was given the job of firing them, with the help of a quartermaster. And he did so, he testified, because he had seen, or thought he had seen, the lights of another ship, not very far away, apparently off the Titanic's bow. A ship that might see the rockets and come. Through the next hour or more, the Titanic fired rocket after rocket into the night, about eight of them in all, by the usual count.

Nobody aboard the Titanic, from that moment on, could pretend any longer that this was just a precaution. Everyone knows what rockets mean.

Rose looks up at her father. And Daniel looks down at her and smiles, and tells her that the rockets are to call the other ships, and that they'll be here soon. He believes it. He has to.

And he isn't entirely wrong to hope. Up in the wireless room, Phillips and Bride, both of them at work now, have been hearing replies. Ship after ship has picked up the Titanic's call. The Carpathia, as we've seen, is already on her way. But others have heard it too. A German liner, the Frankfurt. The Canadian Pacific steamer Mount Temple. The Titanic's own sister, the Olympic, far away to the west, more than five hundred miles off, too far to be of any use, but listening, and relaying, and asking for news. All through the night, the operators of these ships will pass messages back and forth across the dark Atlantic, a web of tiny clicking voices in the air, all of them trying to help, and almost all of them much too far away.

And there's that other light. The one Boxhall saw.

At the inquiries, Boxhall described seeing, ahead and a little to one side, the lights of a ship. Not very far away, he thought, perhaps five miles or so. He could see her masthead lights, he said, and at one point, he believed, a red sidelight, as though she were turning towards them, or lying still. He watched her through his glasses. He fired rockets towards her. He and others tried calling her with the Morse lamp on the bridge, flashing messages into the dark. Other people on deck saw the light too, and remembered it for the rest of their lives: a faint, steady light, low on the horizon, that never came any closer. Some of the officers even told the people getting into the boats to row towards it, and then come back.

Whose light was it? That question has been argued about for more than a hundred years, and I'll come back to it later in the night, when we're out on the water. For now, it's enough to know that it was there, and that the people on the boat deck saw it, and hoped.

It's worth pausing on that hope for a moment. Because on a night like this, the light on the horizon was everything. It meant that the ocean wasn't empty. It meant that someone, somewhere close, might be looking back.

Rose sees it too. Daniel lifts her up to the rail and points, and she follows his finger out across the black water, and there it is, very faint, a small point of light on the edge of the sea, steadier than a star. She asks if that's the ship that's coming. Daniel says perhaps it is. And Rose watches it, very hard, as if watching could bring it nearer.

Down along the deck, the seamen at the boats are working faster now. The canvas covers are off all the boats. The oars and the masts have been checked. Lanterns are being handed down into some of the boats, small oil lamps that will be all the light their passengers have, once they're down on the sea. Officers are calling out, over the roar of the steam, for women and children to come forward.

And a few at a time, hesitantly, people do.

Now, let's look at the boats themselves, and at what's happening around them.

The lifeboats on the boat deck were numbered, and the British inquiry described the system in its report: the boats in davits were given odd numbers on the starboard side, the right-hand side, and even numbers on the port side, the left. The collapsibles were lettered A to D. So boats one, three, five, seven and so on were on the starboard side, and two, four, six, eight on the port side.

Two officers were in charge of most of the loading. On the starboard side, it was First Officer Murdoch. On the port side, Second Officer Lightoller, who'd been woken in his cabin and come up to help. And here's something that turned out to matter a very great deal. The order had gone out, women and children first. But the two officers understood it differently. Lightoller, on the port side, took it to mean women and children only, and turned men away from his boats, even when there were seats empty and no more women nearby. Murdoch, on the starboard side, took it to mean women and children first, and when there were no more women and children waiting at a boat, he let men get in. So, quite simply, a man's chances tonight could depend on which side of the ship he happened to be standing on.

The first boat to go down, according to most accounts, was lifeboat number seven, on the starboard side, at around a quarter to one. And she went down with far fewer people in her than she could have carried.

And so now, with that first boat on its way to the water, we come to the second of our three mysteries. I asked you at the beginning of the night: when the lifeboats began to go down the side of the ship, why did so many of the first ones leave with empty seats?

There are several answers, and they all fit together.

The first is that nobody believed the ship would sink. Think of it from where you're standing now. Under your feet is the largest and newest ship in the world, warm and solid and brightly lit, hardly moving, hardly even tilting yet. And there, hanging over the edge of the deck, is a small wooden boat, about to be lowered seventy feet into the freezing black sea, in the middle of the night, in the middle of the ocean. Which would you rather be in? To a great many people on the boat deck in this first hour, getting into the lifeboat seemed the more dangerous choice. Many simply refused. Some husbands and wives decided to stay together on the ship. Some people went back inside to keep warm.

The second answer is that nobody had told the passengers which boat was theirs. There'd been no drill. There were no lists. People didn't know where to go, and many of them, especially in third class, deep in the ship, didn't reach the boat deck until much later.

The third answer is about the boats themselves. Several officers, Lightoller among them, said afterwards that they had not been sure the boats could safely be lowered from the davits with a full load of people, for fear that the boats might buckle or the ropes give way. Lightoller described the plan of lowering boats only partly full, with the idea that more people could be taken in from the doors lower down in the ship's side, the gangway doors, once the boats were in the water. I'm paraphrasing his reasoning, because his testimony on this point is long, and has been questioned by later writers. Most later writers believe that the boats and their davits could in fact have taken a full load. And the gangway doors were never used to fill them.

And the fourth answer is simply time. In the first hour, there seemed to be plenty of it. The ship didn't feel as though she was sinking. And so the first boats went quickly, with whoever happened to be standing nearby and willing to get in.

So that's our second mystery. The first boats left half empty because of disbelief, and confusion, and caution, and a feeling, which turned out to be tragically wrong, that there was still plenty of time.

By the usual estimates, that first boat, number seven, took around twenty-eight people. It could have held sixty-five. Emergency boat number one, a little later, is generally said to have left with only twelve people aboard, in a boat built for forty. The later boats went down fuller, much fuller, as the night went on and people understood. But by then, the boats were running out.

And here's where we meet a disagreement among the historians, about exactly that question of the order of the boats. The British inquiry tried to set out the time at which each lifeboat was lowered, and how many people were in it. But it admitted that it could only give rough estimates. Since then, researchers such as Bill Wormstedt and Tad Fitch, who have spent years comparing every surviving account of every boat, have argued that some of the inquiry's times and numbers are wrong. On the port side, for instance, they have found considerable evidence that lifeboat number six was not, as was often said, the first to leave. And people still argue over the occupancy of almost every boat. The truth is that in the noise and the cold and the dark, nobody was counting carefully. And the people who might have counted best, the officers and crew on the deck, were busy saving lives.

Let's set this beside our own world for a moment. Today, if you work in an office or a school, you've probably done a fire drill. You know your nearest exit, and your assembly point, and someone with a clipboard counts everyone out. On a modern cruise ship, by law, every passenger must attend a safety drill before the ship sails, and every passenger is assigned to a particular muster station. All of that, in large part, came about because of this night. The International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea, agreed in London in 1914, required, among many other things, that there be lifeboat places for everyone aboard. On the Titanic, all of that lay in the future.

Through all this, there's music.

The ship's band, the eight musicians led by a young violinist named Wallace Hartley, had come out and begun to play, first, according to the survivors, in the first-class lounge, and then out on the deck near the boats. They played cheerful music, ragtime and popular tunes of the day, and many of the people on the boat deck remembered afterwards how strange and how comforting it was, to stand in the cold, waiting for the boats, with the band playing lively waltzes and dance tunes a little way off. They went on playing for a long, long time.

Joseph Wren appears out of the crowd, red-faced with cold, his white jacket under a borrowed coat. He's been sent up to the boat deck to help with the boats on the starboard side, towards the back, where Mr Murdoch is loading. He finds the Hollis family, and tells Daniel, quietly, that they should come with him, aft, to the boats there, because they'll be going soon. He doesn't say anything else. But Daniel looks at his face, and understands.

So the family follows Joseph along the starboard boat deck, past the funnels, through the crowd, past the band, towards the back of the ship, where boats nine, eleven, thirteen and fifteen are hanging from their davits, swung out over the sea, with officers and seamen working around them. Behind them, another rocket goes up with a hiss and a bang and bursts into white stars, high above the ship.

Daniel feels in his coat, under the lifebelt, for the watch. It's still there, against his chest. He doesn't take it out. He doesn't need to. He knows it must be nearly a quarter past one.

And he notices something he hasn't noticed before. When he looks forward along the deck now, towards the bow, it seems, very slightly, to be lower than where he is standing. As if the long deck were a road going gently downhill.

## Part Eight — Around Half Past One: Boats Eleven and Thirteen

The small question for this hour is a practical one. How, exactly, do you lower a wooden boat full of people seventy feet down the side of a ship, in the dark, and set it gently on the sea?

We're about to watch it done. Twice.

Here is the answer to our small question. A lifeboat on the Titanic hung from two davits, curved iron arms, one at each end of the boat. On the deck, at each davit, seamen held the other end of the falls, wrapped around a cleat or a post, and, on the order to lower, they let the ropes out slowly, hand over hand, a little at a time. The two crews had to work together, very carefully, so that both ends of the boat went down at the same speed. If one end went faster than the other, the boat would tip, and people could fall out. Seventy feet down. Past the rows of lighted portholes of the decks below, so close that you could touch them. Boat eleven, by many later estimates, was one of the most heavily loaded boats of the whole night. It was so full that the people at the oars could hardly swing them. Down the side of the ship they go, as boat eleven did, lurch by lurch, past the lighted portholes, towards the sea. And here, near the bottom, something happens that Beesley describes in detail, and that has made boat thirteen one of the most remembered boats of the night.

As they came down near the water, they found that they were being lowered right into a great stream of water that was pouring out of the ship's side, from a large opening just above the waterline. It was the discharge from the ship's condensers, the water used to cool the steam from the engines, being pumped out into the sea. It was a strong, rushing torrent, and when the boat reached the water, it pushed the boat away from the ship's side, and backwards, towards the stern. Towards the place where boat fifteen, the very next boat, was about to come down.

And as the people in boat thirteen looked up, they saw boat fifteen, full of people, coming down the side of the ship towards them, right above their heads.

They shouted up to the deck to stop lowering. Nobody heard them above the noise. Boat thirteen was still held by its falls, the ropes still hooked on at both ends. And then Fred Barrett, and a man beside him, cut the falls with a knife. Boat thirteen broke free, and was pushed clear by the current, out from beneath the falling boat. And boat fifteen came down into the water just behind them, where they had been a moment before. Nobody in either boat was hurt.

Now here's a small disagreement among the accounts, the gentle kind, about exactly how close it was. Some survivors described the two boats as coming down at almost the same moment. Barrett and some others indicated that boat fifteen started down about thirty seconds after boat thirteen, and that boat thirteen reached the water about a minute or so ahead. In the dark and the noise, half a minute can feel like no time at all. What everyone agrees is that it was close, and that a knife and a quick hand made the difference.

Let's set this beside our own lives. If you take a lift in a tall building today, you step in, press a button, and the car glides smoothly down, controlled by motors and sensors and safety brakes. You hardly feel it move. Daniel and Beesley and the people in boat thirteen went down seventy feet on two ropes, lowered by hand, by men they couldn't see, into a rushing current, beneath a falling boat. And they reached the sea safely.

Boat thirteen pulls away from the ship. Daniel sits on his thwart and looks out across the water, towards the dark shapes of the other lifeboats, scattered across the sea. Somewhere among them is boat eleven. He can't tell which one it is. He puts his hand to his coat, to the inside pocket, out of habit, to feel for the watch. And remembers that it isn't there. It's with Rose.

He smiles. And then he puts his face in his hands, for a moment, and breathes.

## Part Nine — Around Two O'Clock: The Lights on the Water

Here is the small question for this hour. As the great ship went slowly down by the head, why did her lights go on shining, deck after deck, almost to the very end?

We'll watch them from the water. Because now, for the rest of this night, that's where we'll be.

Imagine that you're sitting in boat eleven again, beside Rose, perhaps a few hundred yards from the Titanic now. The seamen have stopped rowing. The boat rocks very gently on the flat black sea. It's bitterly cold. The water around you is close to freezing, and the air above it is colder. Your breath clouds in front of your face, and your feet, on the wet boards of the boat, have gone numb. Rose is pressed against her mother's side, with May asleep across Ellen's lap between them, and Tom squashed in on the other side. Joseph Wren sits at his oar, just behind them, his hands wrapped in his sleeves.

And there, across the water, is the ship.

She's still lit. Every row of portholes, every deck light, every window, still glowing yellow against the dark, so that she looks, as several survivors said, like a great city, or a vast hotel, rising out of the sea. But she's tilted now, quite plainly, down at the front. The bow is low in the water, and the lights at the front are going under the surface, one row after another, shining for a moment beneath the surface, and then fading. The stern is beginning to lift. And from the deck, faint across the water, you can still hear the band.

That's the answer to our small question, and it's one of the quietest, bravest things that happened on this night. The lights stayed on because the ship's engineers stayed below, keeping the generators running, so that there would be light on the decks for loading the boats, and power for the wireless, for as long as possible. They kept the lights burning almost until the very last minutes. Not one of the ship's engineering officers survived the night. They're remembered today by a memorial in Southampton, and in many other places, and every one of the people in the boats who could see that ship lit up against the sky owed something to them.

On the boat deck, the last boats are going.

On the port side, near the front, the last of the lifeboats to be lowered from the davits, collapsible D, went down at about five past two, by the usual reckoning, with the water already very close below it. After that, there were only the two collapsibles on the roof of the officers' quarters, A and B, which the crew were struggling to get down to the deck as the water came up to meet them. They never were properly launched. They floated off the ship as she went down, one of them upside down. We'll come back to them.

In the wireless room, Jack Phillips was still sending. Harold Bride, in the account he gave to the New York Times, published on the nineteenth of April, described how Captain Smith came to the wireless room near the end and told them, in effect, that they had done their duty, and could do no more, and that it was every man for himself now. I'm paraphrasing Bride, because the newspaper versions of his words differ a little. But it's one of the few records we have of the captain in those last minutes, and it shows him, as many survivors remembered him, calm, and releasing his men. Phillips went on working at the key for some minutes more. Then the two operators went out onto the deck. Bride survived. Jack Phillips did not.

Captain Smith, too, was lost with his ship. Survivors gave different accounts of where he was seen last, and what he did. I'm not going to choose among them. It's enough to say that he did not leave her.

And Thomas Andrews, who had built her, was lost too. Nobody knows his last minutes for certain.

And now we come to the band, and to one of the most famous disagreements of this whole night.

The eight musicians played on, as we've seen, for a long time, first in the lounge and then out on deck. Many survivors remembered that, at the very end, they played a hymn. And the hymn most often remembered is "Nearer, My God, to Thee." It was sung at the memorials afterwards, and played at the funeral of the bandleader, Wallace Hartley, and it has become, in films and books, the music of this night.

But Harold Bride, in his newspaper account, said something different. He said the band was playing "Autumn." What did he mean? For years, people assumed he meant a hymn tune of that name. But in the decades after, the historian Walter Lord, who wrote the classic book A Night to Remember in 1955, and who spent the rest of his life collecting survivors' memories, came to suggest that Bride might have meant a popular waltz of the time called "Songe d'Automne," Autumn Dream, by the English composer Archibald Joyce, which was in the repertoire of bands on the White Star liners. Others have pointed out that Bride was in the water, or near it, at the end, and might not have heard the last tune at all. And others again think that the band may have played several things at the end, and that different survivors, in different places, heard different music. We don't know. We'll never know for certain. What we do know is that they played until very near the end, and that every one of the eight men was lost.

All around boat eleven, the other lifeboats are scattered across the water, some near, some far, small dark shapes on the sea, some with a single lamp glimmering in them, some with none. Most of them are rowing, slowly, away from the ship.

Why row away? Because the officers, as they lowered the boats, had told many of the crews to pull clear of the ship's side, and to keep clear. There was a widespread fear, among seamen of the time, that a great ship going down would drag everything near her down with her, by a kind of suction, as the sea rushed in to fill the space where she had been. Modern experts generally think that this fear was much exaggerated, and that the suction of a sinking ship is far less than sailors used to believe. But nobody in the boats tonight could know that. And so the crews rowed away, and then rested on their oars, and waited, and watched.

In boat eleven, Joseph Wren rows with the others for a while, clumsily at first and then better, until a seaman tells them to stop. And then they sit, breathing hard, their breath clouding in the frozen air, and watch.

Ellen speaks to Joseph, quietly, over her shoulder. She asks him if he thinks the men will get away in the last boats. And Joseph, who has spent the last hour on the boat deck and knows how few boats there are left, and how many people, doesn't know what to say. So he tells her the truth, as gently as he can. That he saw her husband standing by boat thirteen when they went down. That thirteen was the very next boat. That Mr Murdoch was letting men in on this side, when there were no women waiting. That there's a chance. A good chance. That's all he says. And it's enough, for now, to hold on to.

Ellen nods. She doesn't say anything else. She holds May a little tighter. And she reaches out in the dark and finds Rose's hand, and the watch inside it, and holds both.

Let me tell you one more small, true thing about this hour, because it's something that every survivor in every boat remembered: the cold. The sea tonight is colder than the freezing point of fresh water. Salt water can stay liquid below the temperature at which fresh water turns to ice, and tonight, out here, close to the ice field, the sea is somewhere around a couple of degrees below that, by the usual accounts. In the boats, people are sitting with wet feet, in evening shoes or slippers or bare stockings, wrapped in whatever they managed to bring. Some wear fur coats over their nightclothes. Some have only a blanket. The crewmen at the oars are the warmest, because they're working. Everyone else simply huddles together and waits.

Now, I'll tell you about the last minutes, as the survivors in the boats saw them. I'll tell it calmly, and simply, and with respect, because these were real people, and real lives.

At about a quarter past two, the bow went fully under. The water came up over the boat deck, and the ship's stern lifted higher and higher into the air, until the propellers, enormous things, rose dripping out of the sea. The lights, which had shone all night, flickered once, and came back, and then went out, all at once, and did not come on again. There was a long, deep, rumbling roar, which many survivors heard, and described in different ways. And the stern, now just a dark shape against the stars, stood up high above the water for what seemed a long time, perhaps a minute or more. Then it settled back a little. And then it slid forward, and down, slowly at first, then faster, into the sea. And it was gone.

It was about twenty past two, ship's time. On Monday morning, the fifteenth of April, 1912.

Beesley watched it all from boat thirteen. He described how the ship stood upright in the water, for some minutes, motionless, against the sky, before she went down, and how the sea closed over her. I'm paraphrasing a passage that many readers have found unforgettable. He wrote as a scientist would, trying to describe exactly what he saw, and leaving out nothing, and adding nothing.

And here we come to a disagreement that lasted seventy-three years, and was settled at the bottom of the sea.

Did the Titanic go down in one piece, or did she break in two? Some survivors in the boats, and some who were in the water, were certain that they had seen her break apart, between the third and fourth funnels, before she sank. A young first-class passenger named Jack Thayer, who was seventeen and who survived by reaching one of the overturned collapsibles, was among those who said so, and a sketch based on his description, drawn aboard the rescue ship, showed her breaking. But other witnesses, including Second Officer Lightoller, insisted that she went down intact. And the inquiries, weighing all this, came down on the side of the ship sinking in one piece.

Then, on the first of September 1985, a joint French and American expedition, led by the oceanographer Robert Ballard and the French scientist Jean-Louis Michel, found the wreck, more than two miles down, on the floor of the Atlantic. And there she was, in two main pieces, the bow and the stern, lying far apart on the seabed. The witnesses who had said she broke had been right.

And still the argument goes on, in a new form. At what angle did she break? Many of the survivors' accounts, and some early studies of the wreck, suggested a high angle, with the stern standing up steeply before she tore apart. But some later researchers, including the naval architect Roger Long, studying pieces of the ship's bottom found on the seabed, have argued that she may have broken at a much lower angle than people once thought, perhaps not much more than ten or fifteen degrees, and that the steep rise of the stern that so many people remembered came after. Others still prefer the higher angle. The debate is still going on among researchers today.

Let's set this beside our own world for a moment, very gently. If you've ever lived through a power cut at night, you'll remember the moment when every light in the house, and in the street, and in all the houses around, went out at once. And how dark it suddenly was. And how quiet. That's what the survivors in the boats remembered, more than anything else. The lights going out. And then the darkness, and the stars.

In boat eleven, Ellen has turned May's face against her shoulder, so that she won't see, though May, mercifully, is asleep. Tom has hidden his face in his mother's coat. Joseph Wren, at his oar, has taken off his cap.

And Rose has watched. All of it. With the silver watch held tightly against her chest, in both hands. She felt it ticking, all through those last minutes, against her fingers, steady, unhurried, the same as it always is. Tick, tick, tick, tick. She does not know if her father is in a boat or on the ship. Nobody in boat eleven knows anything at all.

For a little while, after the ship had gone, there were voices across the water. I won't describe them. The survivors who heard them carried them for the rest of their lives, and it isn't for us to borrow them tonight.

Then, slowly, the sea was quiet. And the people in the lifeboats were alone on the dark, flat ocean, under more stars than they had ever seen.

## Part Ten — Around Half Past Two: The Long Wait

The small question for this hour is a simple one. Out on a dark, flat ocean, in small open boats, with almost no lights, how did the survivors find one another?

Before we answer it, I want to stop for a moment, and be still, and remember.

When the Titanic went down, about fifteen hundred people were lost with her. Passengers and crew. Men, mostly, but women and children too, above all from third class, where many families had been travelling together, a long way from the boat deck. Stewards and firemen and engineers and musicians and postal clerks. Millionaires and emigrants. People from more than a dozen countries. Every one of them with a name, and a family somewhere, and a reason for being on that ship.

And here, even the numbers are something people disagree about, and I think it's right to be honest about that, because it's part of the respect we owe them. The American inquiry put the number of lives lost at one thousand five hundred and seventeen. The British inquiry put it at one thousand five hundred and three. Modern researchers, working slowly through every surviving passenger list and crew list and record, name by name, have arrived at figures of their own, generally a little below fifteen hundred. The differences come from people who booked but didn't sail, from crew who signed on and deserted before the ship left, from people travelling under other names, and from simple mistakes in lists made in a hurry. It's a reminder that behind every one of those figures was a person, and that the work of finding them all, and remembering them, has gone on for more than a century.

I won't say any more about them tonight than that. But I'd like us to keep them in mind, quietly, for the rest of this night.

Now, back to the boats.

Imagine that you're sitting in boat thirteen now, beside Daniel. It's very dark. There's no lamp in this boat, or none that anyone can get to work. The only light comes from the stars, which are so many, and so bright, in the clear frozen air, that you can see the faces of the people around you, faintly, and the shapes of the other boats on the water. Daniel is sitting with his arms around himself, shivering. The young woman beside him, with the baby, has fallen into a kind of doze, and he has put his coat around them both, and sits now in his jacket and his lifebelt, with his hands under his arms.

Lawrence Beesley, in this same boat, writes about the stars. He describes how brilliant they were, how many, and how strange it was that the sky seemed so close, and so full of light, while the sea was so dark. I'm paraphrasing a passage that's long and lovely. He even describes how some of the people in the boats kept mistaking low stars on the horizon for the lights of ships coming to rescue them, and how they'd be disappointed again and again, as the star rose, and became only a star.

And so, our small question. How did the boats find each other?

Partly, they called out, across the water, in the silence. Sound carries a long way on a calm night at sea. Partly, they looked for the lamps that some boats had. And partly, one officer took charge.

Fifth Officer Harold Lowe, a young Welshman in command of boat fourteen, gathered several of the boats together and had them tied to one another, so that they wouldn't drift apart in the dark, and could be seen more easily from a ship. Then he shared out the passengers among them, to make room in his own boat. And then he took boat fourteen back, with a crew of volunteers, towards the place where the ship had gone down, to see if anyone could still be helped. He brought a small number of people out of the water alive. It was one of the few boats that went back, and it's remembered for that.

Then, later, as the sky began very faintly to change, Lowe hoisted a sail on boat fourteen, and went to the help of collapsible A, which had floated off the ship half swamped, with a number of people still in it, and took them off. And Lightoller, on the upturned collapsible B, kept the men standing on it calm and balanced through the long hours, with an officer's whistle, until two of the lifeboats were able to take them off in the morning. Among the men he helped were Harold Bride, the wireless operator, and the young Jack Thayer, and Colonel Gracie, who wrote it all down.

I'll tell no more of that part of the night than that. Those who were on the collapsibles have told it themselves, in their own words, and they told it with great dignity.

And there was one other light out on the sea. Fourth Officer Boxhall, in charge of emergency boat number two, had brought green flares with him, which he lit from time to time through the night, and those green lights, bright and steady, low on the water, were seen by many of the other boats, and gave them something to row towards. And later, they would be seen by someone else, much further away.

Now, there's one more thing that I need to tell you about, before the night turns, because it's the most argued-over question of all. It's about that other light, the one Boxhall saw on the horizon before the boats went down, the one that never came closer.

You'll remember the Californian, the small Leyland liner that had stopped for the night in the ice, and whose wireless operator, Cyril Evans, had gone to bed at around half past eleven. At the British inquiry, officers of the Californian testified that, during the night, they had seen a ship, a few miles away, stop, and that later they had seen her fire a series of white rockets, eight of them, by their count. They reported this to their captain, Stanley Lord, who was resting in the chart room. No one woke the wireless operator. And the Californian did not move until the morning, when her operator came back on duty and heard the news.

The British inquiry concluded that the ship the Californian's officers saw was the Titanic, and that the Californian could have pushed through the ice and reached her, and perhaps saved many, if not all, of the lives that were lost. The American inquiry was severe on Captain Lord too. But Lord himself always denied it. He said that the ship he and his officers had seen was a smaller vessel, not the Titanic, and that the Titanic had been much further away than the inquiries believed. He spent the rest of his life trying to clear his name, and in later years a number of researchers and writers, sometimes called the Lordites, took up his cause, arguing that there was a third ship, a mystery ship, between them, and that the Californian could not have reached the scene in time anyway. In 1992, the British government's Marine Accident Investigation Branch looked at the evidence again, after the discovery of the wreck showed that the Titanic's last position had been somewhat different from the one given in her distress calls. Its report didn't settle the matter. The argument goes on, and it's one of the longest-running in maritime history. I'll leave it there.

Now, let's set all this beside our own world for a moment. Today, if a ship sinks almost anywhere at sea, an emergency beacon sends out a signal automatically to satellites far overhead, which pass on its position to rescue centres within minutes. Aircraft and helicopters can be overhead within hours. And ships at sea must keep a radio watch around the clock. That last rule came directly from this night. Because of the Californian, ships carrying wireless would be required to keep it manned at all hours, so that no call for help would ever again go unheard simply because an operator had gone to bed. In 1912, out here, there was only the sea, and the stars, and the hope that someone would come.

And someone was coming.

Let's go to her now, because for the last two and a half hours, while all of this was happening, the Carpathia has been on her way, steaming north-west through the dark.

Arthur Rostron, her captain, gave a long account at the American inquiry of what he did in those hours. And it's one of the most remarkable pieces of testimony from the whole of that inquiry, because it's so calm, so practical, and so complete. I'll paraphrase it, and gather it together, rather than quote it in pieces.

Once the ship was turned, and on her new course, he began to prepare her to receive survivors. He had no idea how many there would be. Hundreds, perhaps. Perhaps more than two thousand. So he planned for all of them. The Carpathia had three doctors aboard, and he placed one in each of the three dining saloons, first, second and third class, with supplies ready, so that survivors could be cared for wherever they came aboard. He had blankets gathered and brought to the gangways and the public rooms. He had the cooks and stewards make ready hot coffee, and tea, and soup, in great quantities. He had the purser and the chief steward ready to take names. He had his own passengers, as far as possible, kept quietly out of the way, in their cabins.

He had the gangway doors opened in the ship's side, and lights rigged along her hull, so that the boats could see where to come. He had rope ladders hung over the side, and chair slings made ready, to lift up people who couldn't climb, and canvas bags, to haul up small children and babies safely. He had oil made ready, to pour on the sea to calm it, if a swell got up while the boats were alongside. He had extra lookouts posted, at the bow and on the bridge, because he knew that he too was steaming into the same ice field that had stopped the Titanic.

And then, as he described it, he simply drove his ship on, as fast as she would go, through the dark.

Imagine that you're standing beside him on the Carpathia's bridge, in the cold, at about three in the morning. It's a smaller bridge than the Titanic's, an older ship's bridge, with brass instruments and a wooden wheel. All around you, men are watching the sea, ahead and on either side, in silence. Every so often a lookout calls out, and the ship swings, carefully, around an iceberg that looms up out of the dark, grey-white and enormous, and slides past. And Rostron stands there, upright and very still. Later accounts, including those of men who served under him, describe him at this hour standing quietly on his bridge, with his cap raised, praying.

Somewhere ahead, low on the water, a small green light flares up, and fades. Boxhall's flare, in boat number two. Rostron sees it. He steers for it.

It's about three o'clock in the morning now. In boat thirteen, Daniel has stopped shivering, which worries the man beside him, who keeps him talking. They talk about printing. About Reading. About Toronto. About children. The man has two of his own, at home in England. And the young woman with the baby wakes, and thanks Daniel for his coat, and he tells her to keep it.

Tom is asleep again, curled on the bottom boards against his mother's legs, with Joseph's spare jacket over him. And then, at about half past three, somebody in boat eleven sees a flash of light low down on the southern horizon. Then another. A small burst of light, rising into the sky, and fading.

A rocket.

All through the boats, all across the sea, people see it, and sit up, and point, and call out to one another. A rocket. A ship.

And so now, as that little ship comes on through the dark towards them, we come at last to the third of our three mysteries. The answer is steam.

## Part Eleven — Around Ten Past Four: First Light and the Carpathia

The small question for this part of the morning is a gentle one. When a small boat comes alongside a big ship at sea, how do the people in it actually get aboard?

We'll see in a little while. But first, let me finish the answer to our third mystery, because the Carpathia is still coming.

A steamship like the Carpathia makes all of her power from steam, raised in her boilers by coal fires, just as the Titanic did. And that same steam doesn't only drive the engines. It also heats the ship. It warms the cabins and the saloons through pipes and radiators. It heats the water for the baths and the basins and the galleys. On a cold April night in the North Atlantic, a good deal of the steam her boilers make goes into keeping her passengers comfortable.

So when Rostron ordered the heating and the hot water to be cut off, every bit of that steam was sent instead to the engines. And he did one other thing. He had the off-duty firemen and trimmers woken from their bunks and sent down to the stokeholds, so that every furnace could be fed as fast as men could shovel. More coal, burning faster. More steam. And all of it going to the propellers.

That's the answer to our third mystery. A captain racing to save hundreds of strangers turned off the heating in his own ship, on a freezing night, because warmth for his passengers was steam that could be used for speed. His own passengers, asleep in their cabins, grew a little colder as the night went on. Most of them didn't know why until morning.

How fast did she go? The Carpathia was not a fast ship. She'd been built to carry emigrants steadily and economically, and her normal service speed was about fourteen knots. The story that has been told ever since is that, that night, with every ounce of steam her boilers could raise, she reached seventeen knots or more, faster than anyone had thought she could go. It's a wonderful story. And here, gently, is a place where historians disagree about it.

The distance the Carpathia had to cover was worked out from the position the Titanic gave in her distress calls. But since the wreck was found, we've known that the Titanic was not quite where she thought she was. Her last position was some miles out, and the place where she actually sank was nearer to the Carpathia than the distress call said. So the Carpathia's real run that night was shorter than fifty-eight miles. Writers such as the historian Tim Maltin have argued that, if you take the true distance and the time she took, her average speed works out at well under seventeen knots, perhaps somewhere nearer fourteen. Others reply that she may well have touched higher speeds for part of the run, and that she had to slow down and steer carefully around the ice as she came nearer. And Rostron himself, of course, wasn't measuring anything that night except the sea ahead. What nobody disputes is that she came as fast as she possibly could, and that her engineers and stokers gave her everything they had.

And now she's here.

At about four o'clock in the morning, Rostron, seeing ice ahead and the green light of boat two close by, stopped his engines. And a few minutes later, the Carpathia began to take aboard the first of the Titanic's survivors.

At the American inquiry, Rostron described it simply. The first boat, he said, was in the charge of an officer, and he could see that it wasn't fully under control, and the officer called out to him that he had only one seaman with him. So Rostron brought his ship carefully round, close to the boat, to make it easier. That officer was Joseph Boxhall, in boat number two. And by the time its people were aboard, Rostron said, day was breaking, and he could see the other boats, scattered across the sea, within about four miles of him.

Day is breaking. Let's look at it.

Imagine that you're sitting in boat eleven, at this hour, beside Rose. The sky in the east has turned from black to deep blue, and then to grey, and then to a pale, clear gold. The other boats, scattered over the water, some near, some far, with their oars rising and falling. Ice.

Now here's the answer to our small question. When a lifeboat came alongside the Carpathia, the crew made it fast to the ship with ropes, to stop it drifting away. Then the people in the boat had to get up the side of the ship, through the open doors in her hull, a little way above the water. Those who could climb went up rope ladders, the ship's crew reaching down to help them. Those who couldn't, the old, and the injured, and those too cold and stiff to manage a ladder, were hauled up in chair slings, a kind of rope seat that went under the arms. And the smallest children, as Rostron had planned, were lifted up in canvas bags, safely, one by one, while their mothers watched from the boat below and then followed them.

Let's set that beside our own lives. If you've ever stood in the arrivals hall at an airport, waiting for someone you love to come through the doors, you'll know that particular feeling of looking at every face, one after another, and hoping that the next one will be theirs. On the decks of the Carpathia this morning, hundreds of people are doing exactly that. Watching every boat come in. Searching every face.

Aboard the Carpathia herself, all this time, her own passengers have been waking up. Many of them went to bed last night expecting nothing more than another quiet day at sea on the way to Gibraltar. Some woke in the small hours to find their cabins strangely cold, and the radiators silent, and wondered why. Some felt the ship racing, shuddering a little under their bunks with the effort of her engines. And some were woken by the stewards, quietly, and asked to stay in their cabins, or to dress and come and help. By dawn, many of them are up on deck, in their coats, watching the boats come in out of the ice. And in the hours ahead, many will give away their cabins, their clothes, and their own blankets to people they have never met.

There's a lovely detail in the way so many survivors later remembered their first minutes aboard the Carpathia. It isn't the drama. It's the coffee. Again and again, in letters and memoirs, people mention the hot drinks put into their hands as they came through the door. Coffee, and tea, and brandy for the very cold. Beesley mentions it too, as I remember his account, though I'll paraphrase rather than quote him: the warmth of a hot drink, and of a blanket, after so many hours in the cold, and the extraordinary kindness of the Carpathia's people. After a night like that, it's the small, warm, ordinary things that people remember.

Boat thirteen came alongside a little earlier than boat eleven, as it happened, and Daniel went up the rope ladder, slowly, stiffly, his hands so cold he could barely grip the rungs. The young woman with the baby went up before him, the baby lifted up ahead of her in a canvas bag. At the top, a Carpathia steward put a blanket round him and a mug of coffee into his hands, and asked him his name, and wrote it down in a little book. Daniel Hollis, he said. Second class. And then, before he could stop himself, he asked whether a boat with a woman and three children had come aboard yet. The steward said he didn't know. Boats were coming in all the time. If he waited by the door, he'd see.

So he waited by the door.

He watched every boat come in. He watched the faces come up through the door in the ship's side, one after another, in the grey light and then the gold. An old man in a sling. A young woman in an evening gown and a man's overcoat. A boy in a cap. A stewardess. A sailor. Two sisters holding hands. Every time the door darkened, he looked up. And every time it wasn't them, he looked down at his coffee, and waited again.

Now, back to boat eleven, as it comes alongside.

May goes up first, in a canvas bag, wailing with indignation, and is lifted out at the top by a sailor with a huge moustache, who hands her to a stewardess, and she stops crying at once, out of sheer surprise. Then Tom, in a sling, delighted, waving. Then Ellen, up the rope ladder, with Joseph Wren climbing close below her in case she slips. And then Rose.

Rose won't go in the sling. She wants to climb. She puts the silver watch in the pocket of her coat, and buttons it, and checks it twice, and then she climbs, hand over hand, up the swaying rope ladder, with the sea below her and the side of the ship before her, and a sailor's hands reach down and lift her in through the door.

She stands in a passageway that smells of coffee and coal smoke and wet wool. There are blankets. Someone puts one round her shoulders. Someone else puts a cup of something hot into her hands. And she looks around at the faces, as everyone looks at the faces.

And there, by the far door, with a blanket round his shoulders and a mug of coffee in his hand, is a man in a cork lifebelt, without a coat, looking back at her.

He has been standing there, by that door, since boat thirteen came in.

I'm not going to tell you what they said to each other. I think you know.

Ellen comes up behind Rose, with May in her arms, and Tom pulling at her coat, and stops. And then the whole family is together, in the corner of a passageway on the Carpathia, in a heap of blankets and wet coats, all talking at once, and nobody listening, and it doesn't matter.

Joseph Wren stands a little way off, watching them, and then turns away, quietly, to go and help with the next boat.

Out on the water, the sea has begun to ripple under a light morning breeze, and the little waves catch the first sunlight and scatter it, so that the whole ocean seems to be sparkling. The last stars have gone. The thin crescent moon is fading into the blue. And the boats are still coming in, one by one, across the bright water, with their oars rising and falling, slow and steady, towards the waiting ship.

And outside, the sun comes up over the rim of the sea, and the icebergs turn to gold.

## Part Twelve — Around Half Past Eight in the Morning: The Last Boat and the Watch

Here is the last small question of our night. When all the survivors were safely aboard, what became of the little wooden lifeboats that had carried them through the dark?

Let's spend the morning finding out, slowly.

It's a bright, clear, cold morning now, and the sun is well up over the sea. The water is still almost flat, though a light breeze has begun to ruffle it, and small waves are slapping against the Carpathia's side. Out on the sea, one by one, the last of the boats are coming in. The icebergs stand all around, white and blue and shining in the sun. And the decks of the Carpathia are crowded with people in blankets, in borrowed coats, in fur wraps over their nightclothes, standing at the rails, watching.

The last lifeboat to reach the Carpathia was boat number twelve. It came in heavily loaded, because during the morning it had taken off many of the men from the upturned collapsible B, and in charge of it now was Second Officer Lightoller, who had stood on that collapsible all night. Rostron told the American inquiry that by about half past eight, all of the survivors were aboard. Lightoller was the last to climb the ladder. He made sure of that.

Imagine that you're standing at the rail of the Carpathia, in the sunshine, beside the Hollis family. The wind is cold on your face, but the sun is warm on your back, and someone has given you a cup of hot coffee, and you hold it in both hands, for the warmth. Below you, the last boat is alongside. You watch the people climb up the ladder, slowly, stiffly, helped by the Carpathia's sailors. And you watch the tall figure of the officer, last of all, come up after them, and step onto the deck, and stand for a moment, looking back at the sea.

And then, all along the rail, people turn back to one another, and the ship begins, very gently, to come back to life.

The Carpathia's stewards move along the decks with more blankets and more coffee. A stewardess is sitting on a coil of rope with a small child on her knee, singing to him. Two of the Carpathia's own passengers, an elderly couple in thick tweed coats, are leading a young woman in a borrowed shawl inside, out of the wind, one on each side of her, as if she were their own daughter. Somebody has found a box of biscuits and is handing them round. Nobody says very much. But everywhere you look, people are being kind to one another, quietly, without being asked.

Here's the answer to our last small question. Rostron had the Carpathia's crew hoist aboard as many of the Titanic's lifeboats as they could, using the ship's own davits and cargo gear. Thirteen of them were lifted up out of the water, and stowed on the Carpathia's decks and hung from her davits. The others were left to drift. The boats that were taken aboard were carried all the way to New York, where they were handed back to the White Star Line. The little white boats that had gone down the side of the Titanic in the night came home with their passengers.

Before the Carpathia left the place, Rostron did one more thing. He asked for a short service to be held aboard, a service of prayer, of thanksgiving for those who had been saved, and of remembrance for those who had not. People gathered in the saloon. I won't try to give you the words that were spoken, because no exact record of them survives that I can trust. But it was a quiet, simple service, and many of the survivors went, and many of the Carpathia's own passengers went with them.

Then the Carpathia turned, slowly, away from the ice, and began her long voyage back to New York, the way she had come.

Up in the Carpathia's small wireless room, Harold Cottam had been at his instrument almost without a break since he first heard the Titanic's call. And now, over the next days, as the Carpathia steamed towards New York, the most important work of all fell to him: sending the names of the survivors, one by one, to the shore, so that families waiting on both sides of the Atlantic could learn who had been saved. There were hundreds of names, and only one young operator, and soon he was exhausted. And so, in time, he was joined by Harold Bride, the Titanic's surviving operator, who, tired as he was after his long night, was helped up to the wireless room to lend a hand. The two young men worked on, by the accounts of the time, for many hours, sending name after name into the air.

It's a quiet, unglamorous thing, sending a list of names. But for a mother in Southampton, or a brother in Toronto, those names were everything.

And there are other small things travelling to New York aboard the Carpathia this morning, tucked into coat pockets and handbags, things that survived the night along with their owners. A little white toy pig with a music box inside it, in the arms of a fashion writer. A second-class dinner menu card from the night before, printed as a postcard, folded into the bag of a lady called Bertha Marshall, who kept it safe, so that in time it found its way to a museum in Greenwich. I like to think of them both, out here in the morning sunshine, among the icebergs, going home.

And here's a gentle disagreement to end on, about the man at the heart of the rescue. Arthur Rostron told the story of this morning twice. Once in 1912, in his testimony to the inquiries, plainly and carefully, as a ship's captain reports to his superiors. And once, nearly twenty years later, in a memoir called Home from the Sea, published in 1931, the year he retired. Many readers have found the memoir modest and unshowy, as he himself was. But some researchers, comparing the two accounts closely, have argued that a few of the details in the later retellings may have been polished, or misremembered, over the years, as stories so often are, when they're told many times. Others think the differences are small, and natural, and that Rostron's account is, on the whole, remarkably consistent. What nobody has ever questioned is what he did. He heard the call, and he turned his ship, and he came.

Now, I'd like to go back to the family, one last time.

The Carpathia was not a large ship, and she was already carrying her own passengers, bound for the Mediterranean. Suddenly she had more than seven hundred more. So her passengers gave up their cabins, and their spare clothes, and their blankets, and the stewards made up beds in the saloons and the library and the smoking room, and on the floors, and anywhere else there was room. And the Hollis family, with Joseph Wren's help, find themselves a corner of the second-class dining saloon, near a radiator that has come back on now, warm again, with a pile of blankets and two borrowed pillows.

Let's set this beside our own lives, one last time. If you travel across the world today, your phone will set itself to the new time zone the moment you land, without your even noticing. You never have to think about which clock you're living by. Daniel's father's watch has no such cleverness. It only knows what it's told.

May is asleep almost before she's laid down, curled up in a blanket, with her thumb in her mouth. Tom, who has been awake nearly all night, and has climbed into a ship in a sling, and seen the sun come up over icebergs, and been given hot cocoa by a sailor, lies down next to her, and tries very hard to tell his father about all of it at once, and falls asleep in the middle of a sentence.

Ellen sits with her back against the wall, with Daniel beside her, and leans her head against his shoulder, and closes her eyes. She doesn't sleep. Not yet. She just rests there, feeling him breathe.

And Rose, who has been holding something in her pocket all this time, takes it out.

It's the watch. The plain silver watch, a little worn at the edges, with its white face and black hands and its small dial for the seconds. She's kept it all night. She wound it in the boat, a little at a time, in the dark, as her father told her. She held it through everything. And it's still going. Tick, tick, tick, tick.

She holds it out to him, on the palm of her hand. Just as he told her to. In the morning, when he came.

Daniel looks at it for a long moment. And then he takes it, very gently, and holds it to his ear, the way he always does, and listens. And then he looks at Rose, and nods, and doesn't say anything at all, because he can't.

Then he opens the case, with his thumbnail, and looks at the face. And then he looks up, across the saloon, at the clock on the wall.

They don't match.

The watch still says Titanic time. The Carpathia, steaming east and then turning west again, has been keeping her own time, the time of her own patch of ocean, set by her own officers. So her clock is a little different. Not by much. But it's different.

Rose asks him which one is right.

And Daniel smiles, for the first time all morning, and says that they're both right. That's the thing about ships' time. Every ship has her own.

Then he winds the watch. Slowly. Click, click, click. And he sets the hands, carefully, to match the clock on the Carpathia's wall. Ship's time. New time. He snaps the case shut. And then, instead of putting it back in his own pocket, he holds it out to Rose again.

Keep it, he says. You looked after it.

She takes it. And she curls up beside her mother, with the watch held against her cheek, so that she can hear it. Tick, tick, tick, tick. And with the sun coming through the portholes, and the radiator warm beside her, and her whole family around her, Rose, at last, closes her eyes. She is the last of them to fall asleep.

Ellen's eyes close too. Her breathing slows. Daniel sits a little longer, with his arm around her, watching his children sleep, in the warm saloon, with the engines of the Carpathia humming steadily beneath them, as the Titanic's did, all those hours ago. Then his head goes back against the wall, and he sleeps too.

Joseph Wren, passing the doorway with a tray of cups, looks in and sees them, all five, asleep in a heap of blankets in the corner. He doesn't wake them. He just smiles, and goes on with his work.

And in Rose's hand, against her cheek, the watch goes on ticking. It ticked in the warm saloon at dinner, and on the shelf above the washbasin in the dark, and against her father's chest on the boat deck, and in her own hands, all through the long night on the sea. And it's ticking now, steady and unhurried, counting out the first quiet hours of a new day. Tick, tick, tick, tick.

They will reach New York on the evening of Thursday, the eighteenth of April, in the rain, and from there, in time, they'll go on to Toronto, and a house with four rooms and a yard. But that's a story for another night.

Thank you for spending this long night with me, and with Daniel and Ellen, and Rose and Tom and little May, and with Joseph Wren, on the Titanic and in the boats and aboard the Carpathia. And thank you for remembering, with me, the real people of that night, those who were saved and those who were lost. If this story helped you rest, or helped you drift off somewhere along the way, I'm truly glad. And if you'd like to come back for more nights like this one, you might consider liking this video and subscribing to the channel, gently, whenever you're ready. There's no hurry at all.

Next time, we'll stay with the great ocean liners, and with the families who crossed the Atlantic to begin new lives. We'll spend one long day at Ellis Island, in New York Harbour, in the spring of 1907, one of its busiest years ever, and we'll follow a family from the deck of their ship, past the Statue of Liberty, through the great registry hall, to the moment they step out into America. I hope you'll join me there.

Until then, sleep well. Keep warm. And may whatever you hold close tonight still be ticking in the morning.

## How the sources were checked

- **The family and the steward are fictional.** Daniel Hollis (37, a compositor from Reading), Ellen Hollis (34), Rose (11), Tom (7) and May (4), and the second-class steward Joseph Wren (23, from Southampton), are invented. The script says so in Part One. No invented person is placed in any real record, and no real person is given invented dialogue. Every real person in the script (Beesley, Carter, Lightoller, Smith, Murdoch, Fleet, Lee, Hichens, Boxhall, Lowe, Andrews, Phillips, Bride, Cottam, Rostron, Evans, Lord, Barrett, Gracie, Thayer, Hartley, Edith Rosenbaum, Bertha Marshall) does only what the sources describe.
- **The family's survival matches real events.** Ellen and the children leave in starboard boat 11. Daniel leaves soon after in boat 13, which Murdoch's starboard side was loading when no more women were waiting. Lawrence Beesley, a second-class man, was told to get into boat 13 in exactly this way. All 22 second-class children survived; only about 8% of second-class men did (13 of 159, per Encyclopedia Titanica, "Lucky 13"). The fear line in Part One reflects this.
- **How quotes were checked.** Direct page fetches are blocked in this environment, so I verified through web-search result summaries. **About 18 searches ran before the session's shared web-search limit was reached.** After that, no more searches were possible. The items marked **verified** below were confirmed in those results. The items marked **standard (not re-checked)** are well-established facts from the inquiries and the classic literature, written from my own knowledge. Wherever exact wording could not be confirmed, the narration paraphrases and says so out loud ("I'm paraphrasing"). **Before publishing, check every "standard (not re-checked)" row against the Titanic Inquiry Project transcripts (titanicinquiry.org) and Encyclopedia Titanica.**
- **Verbatim quotations in the narration** (all from verified results): "designed to be unsinkable" and "as far as it is possible to do so" (White Star brochure); "practically unsinkable" (1911 press and The Shipbuilder); "No, it is a flat calm as a matter of fact." (Lightoller, British inquiry); "iceberg right ahead" (Fleet, US inquiry); the Mesaba message fragment "saw much heavy pack ice and great number large icebergs, also field ice. Weather good, clear." (British inquiry report); "Come at once. We have struck a berg. It is a C.Q.D." (Cottam, US inquiry, as he recalled it); and "hard a-starboard" (Hichens's helm order, standard and widely attested, **not re-checked this session**).
- **Computed, not quoted.** The sun and moon at the wreck site (41.73°N, 49.95°W) were calculated with PyEphem, converting GMT to ship's time as GMT minus 2 h 58 min, which matches the usual 02:20 ship's time = 05:18 GMT. Results: sunset on 14 April about 6:58 pm; moonset about 4:02 pm (5% lit, new moon on 17 April), so there was no moon during the night; moonrise on 15 April about 5:00 am (a thin crescent); civil dawn about 5:13 am; sunrise about 5:45 am. The narration says twice that these were computed. It also notes that 9:10 pm ship's time was just past midnight in England (GMT, with no summer time in 1912).
- **Cut or softened for lack of verification:** Carter's closing words and the coffee after the hymns; the cabin-blanket design; the exact wording of Smith's "let me know at once" (paraphrased); the exact wording of the "keep out, I am working Cape Race" exchange (paraphrased, with Evans's own remark that he took no offence, which was verified); every line of Bride's NYT account (paraphrased); Beesley's wording throughout (paraphrased); the lowering times of each boat (only the 12:45 first launch and the 2:05 launch of collapsible D are given, both as "by the usual reckoning"); specific death details; the names of survivors in boat 11 other than Edith Rosenbaum.

All the heavy material (the sinking, the loss of about 1,500 lives, the death-toll figures, Lowe going back, the collapsibles, the Californian) is placed before word ~23,200. After word 23,300, Parts 11 and 12 hold only the end of the steam answer, the speed debate, the dawn and the icebergs, the boarding, the reunion, the last boat, the service, the names sent by wireless, and the family falling asleep one by one (May, Tom, Rose, Ellen, Daniel). The recurring object, Daniel's father's silver pocket watch, appears in every part: it is set against the saloon clock, left on the cabin shelf, carried in Daniel's coat, given to Rose in boat 11, and held through the night. The final scene is Rose handing it back, Daniel resetting it to the Carpathia's clock and returning it to her. The like/subscribe request and the Ellis Island teaser follow.
