# How Kings and Queens Slept in Freezing Stone Castles | History for Sleep

## Part One — Around Four in the Afternoon: The Last Light on the West Hill

The light is going now, and on this day of all days, it is going very early.

Over the old city of Winchester, the December sky has turned the soft grey of a pigeon's wing, and a thin band of gold is fading behind the hill to the west. Down in the valley, the river Itchen runs dark and quick between its frozen banks. Smoke rises from a thousand roofs, from hearths and ovens and bakehouses, and hangs above the narrow streets in a long, low haze that smells of woodsmoke and roasting meat. The great church of Saint Swithun, the cathedral, sits heavy and pale at the eastern end of the city, its windows catching the very last of the light. And at the other end, the western end, high on a spur of chalk above the city gate, stands the castle.

From the street, it looks exactly as you would imagine. A curtain of grey flint and stone, thick and high, with towers along its length, a deep ditch in front, a gatehouse with a heavy timber door. Torches have just been lit on either side of the gate, and the flames lean and flutter in the cold air. Soldiers stand by them in long woollen cloaks, stamping their feet. Above the walls, against the darkening sky, you can see the steep roofs of buildings inside, and the long, high ridge of a great hall, and from somewhere within, faint and far off, the sound of music, and voices, and the clatter of a great many people eating.

The date is the twenty-fifth of December. The year is 1252. It is Christmas Day. And the King of England is here.

His name is Henry, the third king of that name since the Conquest. He is forty-five years old. He was born in this very castle, on the first of October in 1207, which is why, in his own lifetime, people sometimes called him Henry of Winchester. He has been king since he was nine years old. And tonight, somewhere inside these walls, he and his household are coming to the end of the great feast of the Nativity.

When most of us picture a medieval king going to bed, we picture something like a fairy tale. A vast, cold stone keep. A king climbing a spiral stair to a bare stone room at the top of a tower, with a great wooden bed in the middle of it, and the wind howling through an arrow slit, and nothing but a guttering torch and a bearskin to keep off the cold.

But that is not quite the picture we are going to find tonight. Not the castle of the fairy tales. The kings and queens of thirteenth-century England almost never stayed long in any one place. They moved across the country with hundreds of people, a great travelling town of carts and horses and servants, and when they arrived, they did not, as a rule, sleep in the cold old stone keep at all. They slept in chambers built inside the walls, painted and panelled and hung with cloth, with fireplaces in the walls and glass in some of the windows. And many of the things that made those rooms warm, including, very often, the bed itself, came with them on the road.

But I must tell you something honestly, before we go in. A castle in midwinter was still a hard place. Its walls were thick, but stone holds the cold as well as it holds out an army. Its doors opened onto frozen courtyards. Its passages were draughty, its stairs were dark, its great hall had a fire in the middle of the floor and a roof open to the rafters. And the warmth of it, the real warmth, was rationed out very carefully, by rank. If you were set down tonight inside these walls, in your nightclothes, with no place in the household, no bed, no blanket, and no idea where to go, you would wander from one cold doorway to another, and on a night like this, you might not have survived until morning.

So tonight, we're going to learn how the people inside these walls, from the king and queen at the very top down to the servants who looked after their beds, made it safely and warmly through the long, dark night of Christmas. Slowly. One room at a time.

And as we go, I'd like you to carry three small, strange questions with you.

The first is this. Why would the King of England, the richest man in his kingdom, in a castle that was his own, in the very city where he was born, go to bed tonight in a bed that had travelled here strapped onto the back of a horse?

The second. Why might a king, lying behind walls many feet thick, guarded by soldiers at every gate, have asked for a painting of armed men to be put up beside his pillow?

And the third. Why would the room where the king slept, on the quietest night of the year, never truly be empty, not even once, from dusk until dawn?

I'll answer each of them in its own time. The first a little before the evening is out. The second in the deep of the night. And the third when the castle is very nearly asleep. There's no hurry. We have the whole night.

Now, before we go through the gate, I want to be clear with you about something, because on this channel, it matters a great deal. The king and queen you'll hear about tonight are real people, and so are many of the others I'll mention. But the family at the centre of our story, the family we're going to follow through this night, is invented. Their names, their ages, their habits and their small kindnesses, I have made up. What I've tried very hard not to make up is everything around them: the castle, the household they serve, the rules of that household, the orders the king sent to his sheriffs about his chambers and his windows and his fireplaces, and the chronicles that describe his Christmases. Those are real, and as we go, I'll tell you the names and dates, so you can look them up for yourself on some quiet afternoon. And wherever the records go silent, I'll tell you that, too. I won't put a single invented word into the mouth of the king or the queen. When they appear tonight, they will be doing only what the records allow us to believe they did.

So. Let's meet our family.

The man hurrying across the outer courtyard of the castle right now, with an armful of folded linen held carefully against his chest, is Hugh. He's thirty-eight. He is a servant of the king's chamber, one of the men whose whole working life is spent among the rooms where the king sleeps, dresses and keeps his private things. He is not a lord. He is not a knight. He would never sit at the high table in the hall. But he has been within a few feet of the king's bed on more nights than he could count, and he knows the way its curtains hang, and the weight of its coverlets, and the particular way the king likes the pillows, better than anyone in this castle.

His wife is Isabel. She's thirty-four, and she works in the queen's household, among the women who look after the queen's linen. Her hands are red and cracked from the washing tubs. She knows every one of the queen's shifts and veils and sheets by sight, and she could tell you, if you asked her, which ones came from Rheims and which from Flanders and which were mended last spring.

They have three children, and all three are here in the castle tonight, because the children of servants often lived in the household, working at small tasks and sleeping wherever there was room. Nicholas is thirteen, and he runs errands for the chamber, carrying wood and water and messages up and down the stairs, and he is very proud of it. Margery is ten, and she helps her mother with the folding and the mending. And Peter is six, and his main work, as far as anyone can tell, is getting underfoot and being sent away again.

Five people. One family. Hundreds of rooms, it can seem, though in truth there are far fewer. And somewhere above them all, one bed.

Here is a small question to begin with, and we'll answer it before this part of the night is over. What, exactly, is this place? Because to understand where the king will sleep tonight, you first need to understand that Winchester Castle is not one building at all.

Imagine that you are walking beside Hugh now, across the frozen ground of the castle's outer bailey. Your breath comes out in white clouds. The ground is rutted and hard, and here and there are puddles iced over, and the straw and dung of horses has frozen into the mud. All around you, the space inside the walls is crowded with buildings, and most of them are not stone at all. There are stables, long and low, with the warm smell of horses drifting out. There are kitchens, with smoke pouring from their louvres and the glow of great fires in their open doors. There's a bakehouse and a brewhouse, storerooms and workshops, lodgings for servants, a chapel with candlelight in its windows. And looming above it all, on your left, is the long dark bulk of the great hall, where the feast is going on, its tall windows glowing faintly gold.

Somewhere at the far end of the castle, on the highest ground, is the great tower, the old stone keep. But Hugh isn't going there. He's going towards a cluster of buildings beside the hall and around a smaller court, where the king's own chambers are, and the queen's, with their chapels and their wardrobes and their private passages.

That's the answer to our first small question. Winchester Castle is not a single fortress. It's a walled town in miniature, a royal house, a barracks, a farmyard and a palace all at once, folded inside a ring of stone.

Now, how do we know so much about this place?

Partly, because a piece of it still stands. If you go to Winchester today, you can walk into the great hall itself, the same hall glowing gold in front of us now. It was built for Henry the Third between about 1222 and 1235, in the years of his youth, and it is one of the finest surviving halls of its age anywhere in England. Almost everything else is gone. In the year 1651, after the Civil War, Parliament ordered the castle to be pulled down, and it was. But the hall survived. And we know so much about the rest of it because of something rather wonderful.

Henry the Third was a king who loved buildings. He loved paint, and glass, and carving, and colour. And he gave orders about them, constantly, in writing. When he wanted something done to one of his houses, a fireplace built, a window glazed, a wall painted, a chamber panelled, he sent a written order to the sheriff of the county, or to the keeper of his works, telling him to do it, and to pay for it out of the king's money. The orders that said, in effect, pay this money, were called writs of liberate, from the Latin word meaning deliver, or pay. They were copied onto long parchment rolls, and the rolls survive today in the National Archives at Kew. Between 1916 and the 1960s, they were printed in English summaries, as the Calendar of the Liberate Rolls, in six fat volumes. And they are among the most detailed records of royal domestic life to survive from the whole of the Middle Ages anywhere in Europe.

Here is one, as a taste. In June of 1252, just six months before our night, the king sent an order to the sheriff of Hampshire about the works at Winchester. Among the items, as the published calendar summarises them, were a plaster fireplace to be made in the great tower, heavy ironwork for the windows of the king's chamber, and a glass window for the gallery of the queen's chapel. A fireplace. Iron for the windows. Glass for the queen. Six months later, Hugh is walking past all three of them in the dark.

And here we meet our first point where historians disagree.

For a long time, the study of castles was dominated by a military way of looking at them. To historians like R. Allen Brown, whose book English Castles first appeared in 1954, a castle was, first and foremost, a fortress: a fortified residence, yes, but above all a military building, designed for defence and control. Its walls and towers and gates told you what it was for. But from the 1970s and 80s onwards, a number of historians and archaeologists began to push back. Charles Coulson, in his book Castles in Medieval Society, published in 2003, argued that castles were just as much about status, lordship, display and comfort, and that a great deal of what looks military about them was really a kind of noble architecture, meant to impress. Robert Liddiard, in Castles in Context, in 2005, made the case that castles must be understood as homes and landscapes and symbols, not just as forts. Other scholars have defended the military view, pointing out that castles really were besieged, and really were defended, and that you cannot simply wish the walls away. The truth, most people now think, is somewhere in the middle. Winchester was a fortress. It would be besieged more than once in its history. But tonight, on Christmas night, it is above all a house. A great house, full of people, getting ready for bed.

Let's look at it with a modern eye, for a moment. You might think of a hotel, a very large one, where hundreds of guests and staff are spread across dozens of buildings, from the grand rooms upstairs to the laundry and kitchens in the basement. And you might think of the way a modern hotel is heated: boilers in a plant room, pipes and radiators in every room, warm air that reaches even the cheapest room under the eaves. Winchester Castle has nothing like that. Each warm room is warm because someone has carried wood to it, lit a fire in it, closed its shutters and hung cloth on its walls. Every bit of warmth in this place was made by hand, and every bit of it had to be earned, or granted.

Hugh has reached a doorway now, at the foot of a stone stair, where a single torch burns in an iron bracket. He shifts the linen higher in his arms, nods to the man standing guard, and starts to climb.

Behind him, across the courtyard, the music in the great hall swells for a moment, as someone opens a door, and then falls quiet again as the door swings shut.

Christmas night has begun.

## Part Two — Around Half Past Four: The Livery of Candles

Here is the small question for this part of the night. The sun has set, a few minutes after four o'clock, and it won't rise again until past eight tomorrow morning. In a castle full of hundreds of people, with no electricity, no gas and no oil lamps worth the name, who decides how much light each person gets? And how is it handed out?

I'll tell you how I know about the sunset, by the way, because nobody wrote it down. I worked it out using a modern astronomical model, for Winchester, on Christmas Day in 1252, in the old Julian calendar that Henry and everyone around him used. The sun set at a little after four in the afternoon, local time, and it will rise again tomorrow at a little after eight. That's sixteen hours of darkness. It is, give or take a day, one of the longest nights of the whole year.

Hugh has climbed the stair, and he's come out into a small, low lobby, lit by a single candle in a niche in the wall. Beyond it is a door, and beyond that door is the outer chamber of the king's rooms. He sets his linen down very carefully on a chest. Then he turns around and goes straight back down the stairs again, because he has forgotten the most important thing of all. He needs light.

Imagine that you are following Hugh now, back across the frozen courtyard, towards a low building near the kitchens with a warm yellow glow at its door. The smell reaches you before you're even inside. It's the smell of hot wax, and of tallow, which is melted animal fat, and of smoke, and of honey. This is the chandlery, the household office that makes and keeps the candles. And at a long board inside, a clerk is sitting with a tally stick and a roll of parchment, and a pile of candles beside him, handing them out, one bundle at a time, to a little queue of servants with their hands held out.

Hugh joins the queue. When his turn comes, the clerk counts out the candles for the king's chamber, and makes a mark, and Hugh gathers them up in a cloth, and goes.

That's the first part of the answer to our small question. In a great medieval household, light was not something you simply took. It was given out, by an office, by a clerk, according to a rule. And the name for this kind of allowance, the regular handing out of food, drink, clothing, candles or fodder to the members of a household, was a livery. It comes from the French word for something delivered. Each person's livery was fixed by their rank and their duties. The higher you were, the more light you got, and the better it was.

And we know this, in astonishing detail, because of a document that was already more than a century old by the time of our night.

It's called the Constitutio Domus Regis, which means the Establishment of the King's Household. It was written in about 1136, at the very beginning of the reign of King Stephen, and it is the oldest surviving description of the English royal household. It lists, office by office, almost every member of the king's household, from the chancellor at the top to the men who looked after the hounds, and it sets out, for each one, what they should be paid each day in money, and what they should receive in bread, in wine, and in candles. It survives because it was copied into two great books of the royal Exchequer, the Black Book and the Red Book, in the thirteenth century, which is to say, in Henry the Third's own lifetime. The standard modern edition is by Emilie Amt and Stephen Church, published by Oxford in 2007.

I'll paraphrase the opening entries, because the exact wording depends on which translation you use. The chancellor, the highest officer listed, was to have five shillings a day, along with fine bread, and wine, and one large wax candle, and forty pieces of candle. Further down the list, the allowances get smaller and smaller. Some officers get a candle-end. Some get none at all. Light, you see, was part of your wages. And how much of it you got told everyone in the household exactly where you stood.

By Henry the Third's time, more than a hundred years later, the household had grown much larger and more complicated. But the principle was exactly the same. Light was rationed. Light was counted. Light was a mark of rank.

So what kind of light is Hugh carrying, tucked in its cloth against the cold?

For the king's chamber, wax. The finest candles were made of beeswax. They burned with a clean, clear, steady flame, and they smelled faintly sweet, like honey and summer. They didn't drip much, and they didn't smoke much, and they were very expensive, because beeswax had to be gathered from hives, a little at a time, and much of what was gathered in England went to the church, to burn at altars. Wax candles were the light of kings and saints. Further down the household, people burned tallow, made from the rendered fat of sheep and cattle. It was cheaper, but it smoked, and spat, and smelled of the kitchen, and it guttered and ran. And at the very bottom of the household, people made do with firelight, or rushlights dipped in grease, or nothing at all.

There were other lights, too. In the great hall tonight, there are torches, long staves bound with tow or rope and soaked in wax or resin, held in iron brackets on the walls or carried by servants. There are tall candles on prickets, iron spikes on stands, at the high table. And in the chapels, there are lamps of oil, and wax tapers burning before images of the saints, and on a night like Christmas, there are far more of them than usual.

Making all this light was a trade in its own right, and a busy one. In London and in the larger towns, there were men who made candles for a living, the chandlers, and in time they divided into two separate crafts, the wax chandlers and the tallow chandlers, each with its own company and its own rules. Both companies still exist in the City of London today. In the countryside, wax was so prized that some rents were paid in it: a pound of wax a year, owed to a church or a lord, in place of money. And beekeepers, who looked after hives made of woven straw, kept a careful eye on their swarms, because a swarm of bees that flew away was a little fortune lost.

Here in the castle, the great household's appetite for light at Christmas was enormous. Every chapel, every altar, every great table, every stair that the king might use, every chamber where a lord was lodged, needed its candles. And when the feast was over, the stubs and ends were gathered up and counted, too. Nothing made of wax was wasted. Whatever could be melted down was melted down again, and made into new candles, for another night.

Hugh is back now, at the top of the stair. He opens the door to the king's outer chamber, and goes in.

It's dark in here, and cold, but not as cold as the stair. The room is long and fairly narrow, with a timber ceiling above, and a fireplace set into the thickness of one wall, where a fire has been laid but not yet lit. There's a smell of fresh rushes on the floor, and of old woodsmoke, and of the cloth hangings on the walls, which are damp from the winter air. Hugh takes one of the candles from his bundle, and lights it at the candle in the lobby, and sets it in an iron holder on a small table. A little circle of warm light opens up around it. And then he kneels at the hearth and, with a taper, lights the fire.

Let's stop here for a moment, and set this beside your own home.

Somewhere on the wall near where you are, there's probably a switch. You press it, and a room is full of light. Not the light of one candle, or a dozen, but something closer to the light of a hundred candles, from a single bulb, clean and steady and silent. And you pay for it, not with your rank, but with a bill that arrives once a month or once a quarter, and it's the same rate for everyone. Nobody in your house has to queue at a chandlery. Nobody has to count. In Hugh's world, the king himself, the richest man in England, could not have lit a room as brightly as your kitchen is lit right now. Not with every candle in Winchester.

Now, here's the first point in tonight's story where historians disagree about a document, and it's about the very one I just described.

The Constitutio Domus Regis was written in about 1136. But whose household does it actually describe? King Henry the First had died at the very end of 1135, and King Stephen had just come to the throne. Some historians have read the document as a description of the household as it had been under Henry the First, perhaps drawn up so that the new king, Stephen, would know how things had been done before him. Others have seen it as a statement of what Stephen's own household was going to be. Amt and Church, in their edition, suggest that it was probably written for the new king, to set out the household he was inheriting. It may be partly a record of how things were, and partly a statement of how they ought to be. That's often the way with medieval rules. They tell you what someone wanted, which is not always quite the same as what happened. But for our purposes, it tells us something that nobody disputes. Every candle in a king's household had a name attached to it.

And that brings us to the second half of our small question. Who decides? The answer is: the household itself, through its offices and its rules, set down in writing, and kept by clerks. The chandlery made and handed out the candles. The clerks of the household counted them. And at the end of each period, the whole thing was accounted for, every pound of wax, and checked against the money spent.

The great households were obsessed with this kind of counting. In the royal accounts of the thirteenth century, there are entries for wax by the hundredweight, and for the wages of the men who made candles, and for the cloth, the wine, the spices, the fish, and the firewood, all written down in careful abbreviated Latin. Historians who study the household accounts of Henry the Third, such as Benjamin Wild, who published a study of the king's wardrobe accounts in 2012, have shown just how much of the king's money flowed through his household every single day, and just how closely it was watched.

The fire in the outer chamber has caught now. It's burning well, in its wall fireplace, and the smoke is going up the flue in the thickness of the wall rather than into the room, which, as we'll see later tonight, was still something of a luxury in thirteenth-century England. Hugh crouches in front of it for a moment, warming his hands.

Then he gets up, and goes to the inner door, the door to the king's own chamber, and opens it, and goes in with his candle.

We'll follow him in, a little later. For now, let's stay in the outer room, and listen.

Down the stair, there are footsteps. Somebody is coming up, quickly and lightly, and a moment later the door opens and in comes Nicholas, Hugh's eldest, thirteen years old, red in the face from the cold, carrying a basket of split logs that is almost too big for him. He sets it down by the hearth with a great sigh, the way boys do when they want you to notice how heavy something was.

Nicholas has spent most of this day running. Up the stairs with wood. Down the stairs with ashes. Across the courtyard with messages. To the kitchen and back. He has been running since before dawn, when the bells of the cathedral rang for the first mass of Christmas, and he will be running, on and off, for a few hours yet. But he doesn't mind, because tonight is Christmas, and the kitchens are full of food, and he has already been given a piece of something sweet and spiced by one of the cooks, which he is not going to tell his mother about.

He looks at the fire, and at the candle, and at the door of the inner chamber, standing half open, with his father's candlelight moving on the other side of it. And he thinks, as he often does, that there is nowhere in the world he would rather be than here, near the king's own bed, in the warm.

Out in the courtyard, the bells of the castle chapel begin to ring. It's the evening office of Christmas Day, vespers, being sung in the king's chapel. The sound comes up the stair, soft and muffled through the stone. And from far away, down in the city, you can hear the deeper bells of the cathedral answering.

In a little while, the king will leave the chapel, and the hall, and come here. And before he does, there is a great deal still to do.

## Part Three — Around Half Past Five: The Great Hall at Christmas

The small question for this hour is a simple one. On Christmas night in 1252, where is the warmest place in Winchester Castle? And the answer, I think, might surprise you.

Let's go and look for it. We're going into the great hall.

Imagine that you are standing at the lower end of the hall now, just inside the great door, with the cold of the courtyard at your back and the noise and heat of the feast in front of you. It's enormous. The hall is a long, tall rectangle, built, as architectural historians like to point out, as a double cube, about twice as long as it is wide, and about as high as it is wide. On either side, rows of slender columns of dark, polished stone, the shafts of a stone called Purbeck marble, rise up to support the tall arches, dividing the hall into a wide central space and two narrower aisles. High up in the walls are tall windows, and tonight their glass is black, reflecting back the light of a hundred flames. And above you, far up in the shadows, is the great timber roof, its beams lost in a haze of smoke.

The hall is full. Long trestle tables run down its length, and every bench is crowded with people: knights and clerks and household officers, guests and their servants, men in fur-lined cloaks and men in plain wool. The air is thick with the smell of roast meat, of spiced wine, of wax and smoke and wet wool drying in the warmth. Servants are moving along the tables with dishes and jugs. A dog is working its way patiently under one of the boards, nose down, hoping for bones. At the far end, raised up on a dais, under a cloth of estate, is the high table.

The order of the hall is the order of the household. The higher your rank, the nearer you sit to the dais, and to the fire, and to the king. The lower your rank, the nearer you sit to the door, and the draught, and the noisy passage at the lower end, behind a wooden screen, where the servants come and go between the hall and the kitchens. Before the meal began, water was brought round in ewers and basins, so that everyone could wash their hands, because most food was eaten with fingers, a knife and a spoon. And through the whole feast, one officer of the household was watching the tables with particular care. That was the almoner, whose job was to gather the leftovers and the broken bread, and see that they went to the poor.

There's a lovely little echo of that in the same order of June 1252 that we heard about earlier. Among the works at Winchester that the king asked the sheriff to carry out, according to the published calendar, was the roofing with slate of a penthouse, a covered lean-to passage, running between the great gate of the castle and the king's almonry, the place from which his alms were given out. So the king's charity had its own building here, by the gate, with its own covered way, so that the poor waiting for bread, and the servants carrying it, could keep a little drier. Tonight, at the end of Christmas Day, I like to think of that covered passage busy in the dark, with baskets of bread and meat from this very feast going out along it, towards the gate.

And there, somewhere at the centre of it, though we're too far away to see him properly through the smoke and the crowd, sits the king.

What did Henry the Third look like? We have one or two hints. A chronicler named Nicholas Trivet, writing some decades after Henry's death, described him as a man of medium height, strongly built, and noted that one of his eyelids drooped, partly covering the eye. His tomb effigy, made of gilt bronze, still lies in Westminster Abbey, though it was made after his death and shows an idealised king. That's about all we can say with confidence. So I won't try to describe his face to you. I'll just tell you he's there, at the high table, on the feast of the Nativity, in the hall of the castle where he was born.

And we know he was here, at this Christmas, because a monk wrote it down.

His name was Matthew Paris, and he was a Benedictine monk of the great abbey of Saint Albans, about twenty miles north of London. He was the finest historian of his age in England. For years, until his death in 1259, he wrote and illustrated an enormous chronicle, the Chronica Majora, the Great Chronicle, filled with the news of England and the world: wars and weather, scandals and saints, comets and taxes and the doings of kings. He knew Henry the Third personally. The king visited Saint Albans, and on at least one famous occasion, at Westminster in 1247, Henry invited Matthew Paris to sit close by so that he could watch a great ceremony and write it down properly.

And in his entry for the thirty-seventh year of Henry's reign, which ran from the end of October 1252 to the end of October 1253, Matthew Paris records that the king kept Christmas at Winchester, and kept it with great magnificence. I'm paraphrasing the old Victorian translation there, rather than quoting it, because I haven't been able to check the Latin myself. But the sense is clear. Henry was here. And it was a splendid feast.

That's one of the joys of this particular Christmas. Most of what we know about medieval royal life comes from orders and accounts, the bare bones of administration. But here, on top of all that, we have a monk who knew the king, telling us where he was, and that he celebrated well.

Now, this is the right place to tell you about our first great scholarly disagreement of the evening, because it's about Matthew Paris himself.

Matthew Paris is wonderful to read. He's lively, opinionated, gossipy, full of colour. But is he reliable? The historian Richard Vaughan, in his classic study Matthew Paris, published in 1958, showed just how much Paris could twist and colour his material. He disliked foreigners at the English court, he resented royal taxes, he took the side of his own abbey and of the barons, and he sometimes rewrote documents to suit his views. Later historians, including David Carpenter, who has written widely about Henry the Third, have found that Paris could be wrong about dates and careless about numbers, but also that he was often remarkably well informed, because he talked to people who had been there, including the king himself. So historians today use him a little like a brilliant but unreliable friend. They listen to everything he says, and then they check it against the records. For our night, the simple fact that Henry kept Christmas at Winchester is the kind of thing Paris was usually right about. Whether the feast was quite as magnificent as he says, we'll have to take on trust.

Where did all this food come from? The rolls of Henry's reign are full of orders, every autumn and winter, to his sheriffs and foresters and bailiffs, to send deer from the royal forests, and wild boar, and fish, and birds, and wine, and wax, to wherever the king would be keeping the great feasts. A royal Christmas didn't simply happen. It was ordered, months ahead, by writ, and gathered in from half the counties of the south of England, and carried to Winchester in carts along frozen roads.

And it wasn't only for the great. Henry the Third was famous, in his own time and since, for his charity, and on the great feasts of the church he regularly ordered large numbers of poor people to be fed at his expense. Somewhere tonight, outside the hall, at the castle gate or in the city, there are poor men and women eating bread and pottage because the king has ordered it. I can't tell you how many, on this particular night. But it would have been very much like him.

Now, let's look up. Because there's something on the wall of this hall that I very much want you to see, and something that isn't.

Historians who have studied the rolls report that, back in 1239, Henry ordered a map of the world, a mappa mundi, to be painted on the wall of the great hall here at Winchester. A medieval world map wasn't quite what we'd think of as a map. It was a great round picture of the whole world as Christians understood it, with Jerusalem at the centre, and the east at the top, where paradise was thought to lie, and the known lands of Europe and Asia and Africa arranged around it, full of rivers and cities and strange beasts and the stories of the Bible. There's a famous surviving example, painted on a single calfskin, at Hereford Cathedral, from about 1300. Winchester's was painted straight onto the wall, and it's long gone. But on this night, if it still survives, it will be up there somewhere in the smoke and the shadows, the whole round world, glowing faintly in the torchlight above the feast.

And here's the thing that isn't on the wall. If you visit Winchester's great hall today, the first thing you'll see, hanging on the west wall, is a huge round table top, painted with a Tudor rose at the centre and the names of King Arthur's knights around the edge, with Arthur himself, enthroned, at the top. For centuries, people believed, or hoped, that it really was King Arthur's Round Table. In 1976, a team led by the archaeologist Martin Biddle took it down and examined it with every scientific method then available, including tree-ring dating of its timbers and radiocarbon dating. They concluded that it was made in the second half of the thirteenth century, roughly between 1250 and 1280, and Biddle suggested that it was most likely made for a great tournament held near Winchester by Henry's son, Edward the First, in 1290. The famous painting on it is later still, added in the reign of Henry the Eighth, in the early sixteenth century.

So, on our night in 1252, the Round Table probably isn't here yet. Probably. Because there's a small disagreement even about that. Tree-ring dates give a range, not a single year, and the earliest part of that range comes uncomfortably close to our Christmas. Most specialists follow Biddle in linking the table to Edward the First, but there's no way to be absolutely certain. It is just possible that the timbers of that table were growing as trees, or even being cut, in the very years when Henry sat at this feast.

So what about our small question? Where is the warmest place in the castle tonight?

You might think it's here. The hall is full of people, and people are warm. In the middle of the floor, there's a great open hearth, a fire of logs blazing on a raised stone platform, with no chimney at all, its smoke rising up into the roof and finding its way out through a louvre, a kind of slatted turret in the roof ridge. Its heat is fierce if you're near it. But the hall is huge. Its roof is high. Its doors are opening and closing all the time, letting in great gusts of freezing air. If you're seated near the fire, your face will burn while your back freezes. If you're at the far end of a bench by the wall, near the door, you'll be cold all night, feast or no feast.

No, the warmest place in Winchester Castle tonight is not the great hall, for all its fire and crowds and food. The warmest place is a small one. It's where Hugh has just lit his candle. It's the king's own chamber, with its fireplace in the wall, its hangings, its shutters, its low ceiling, and its door kept closed. That's the answer to our small question. In a medieval castle, warmth didn't live in the big rooms. It lived in the small ones.

Let's set that beside a modern home for a moment. If you've ever been to a big wedding reception in a grand old hall, or a works Christmas party in a cavernous room, you may have noticed the same thing. The big room is exciting, and noisy, and full of life. But it's never quite warm. And at the end of the night, you're glad to go home to a small room, with a door that shuts. The people of Henry's household felt exactly that, every winter evening.

Back in the hall, the minstrels have started up again, with pipe and drum and something stringed. The noise rises. More wine goes round. A burst of laughter breaks out at one of the lower tables. Somebody spills something. A great platter is carried in through the screens at the lower end, from the passage that leads to the kitchens, held high by two men, and everybody turns to look.

And at the edge of it all, by the screens, carrying a jug of hot water up from the kitchen on her way to the queen's rooms, is Isabel, Hugh's wife. She doesn't stop to look at the platter. She has seen a great many platters. She has somewhere she needs to be.

## Part Four — Around Half Past Six: The Bed That Came on a Horse

Here is this hour's small question. When Hugh opens the door of the king's chamber, and lifts his candle, what does he actually see?

Let's go in with him.

Imagine that you're standing just behind Hugh, in the doorway, with the warmth of the outer chamber's fire at your back. The room in front of you is not large. It is perhaps a little bigger than a generous modern living room, with a timber ceiling not far above your head, and walls that are plastered smooth and painted. In the candlelight, you can't see much of the painting, only a glimpse of colour here and there, a deep green, a gleam of something gold, the edge of a painted border of leaves. There are shutters across the windows, closed and barred, with a strip of cloth stuffed along the bottom where the draught comes in. There's a second fireplace in this room, smaller than the one outside, already laid with wood. The floor is covered with fresh rushes, mixed with something sweet-smelling, dried herbs perhaps, and there are woven mats over them near the bed. There's a chest against one wall, iron-bound and locked. A bench, with a cushion. A small table with a basin and a ewer. A perch for a hawk, empty tonight. A crucifix on the wall, and beneath it a little prie-dieu, a kneeling desk for prayer.

It's worth noticing what isn't here. There's no wardrobe cupboard, in our sense, because clothes are kept folded in chests, or hung on pegs in the separate room next door. There are no chairs, except perhaps one, because a chair with a back and arms was a seat of honour, not an everyday thing. There are no pictures in frames. There's no mirror on the wall, though there may be a small one of polished metal in the chest, and there's certainly no clock. And there are no books on shelves, though the king's chapel nearby has its service books, bound in leather and boards, and the queen, as we'll hear, loved a good book. A medieval great chamber was sparsely furnished, by our standards. What made it rich was not the number of things in it, but the colour on its walls, the cloth on its bed, and the warmth.

And there, taking up most of one end of the room, is the bed.

It's big, though not as big as you might imagine. It stands on a low wooden frame, with a framework of posts and rails above it, from which hang curtains, falling all the way to the floor on three sides and drawn back on the fourth. Across the top, there's a canopy of cloth, stretched flat, which later inventories would call a tester or a celure. In the candlelight, the curtains look dark, a deep green, the colour Henry the Third loved more than any other. I should tell you that I have chosen that colour for tonight. We don't have a description of the curtains of this particular bed on this particular night. But we do know that Henry asked for green again and again, in the painting of his chambers, and that he loved green and gold together, and so, in this story, the curtains are green.

Hugh sets his candle down on the table, and stands for a moment, looking at the bed, the way a craftsman looks at something he made.

That's our small question answered. A small, painted, panelled room, with a fire, shuttered windows, a chest, a basin, a place to pray. And a bed with curtains, which is the heart of everything.

And now, a little before the evening is out, just as I promised, we come to the first of our three mysteries.

I asked you, at the very beginning of the night: why would the King of England, the richest man in his kingdom, in a castle that was his own, in the very city where he was born, go to bed tonight in a bed that had travelled here strapped onto the back of a horse?

The answer begins with one simple fact about medieval kings. They hardly ever stayed still.

Henry the Third had dozens of houses: castles, palaces, manors, hunting lodges, scattered across the south and middle of England. Westminster, beside the Thames, was his favourite, and he spent more time there than anywhere else. But he also stayed, again and again, at Windsor, and Winchester, and Clarendon in Wiltshire, and Woodstock in Oxfordshire, and Marlborough, and the Tower of London, and many more. The court moved from one to another, sometimes after a few days, sometimes after a few weeks, following the seasons, and the hunting, and the great feasts of the church, and the business of the kingdom. Historians reconstruct these journeys from the places and dates on which the king's letters were issued, and the map they make is a web of roads, crossed and recrossed, year after year.

And when the king left a house, a great deal of what made it comfortable left with him.

Most of the time, when the court was elsewhere, a royal house like Winchester was kept by a small staff: a constable, a keeper, a few servants. The great rooms were bare. The wall paintings stayed, of course, and the glass, and the fireplaces, because they were part of the building. But the hangings, the cushions, the best linen, the silver, the wardrobe of the king's clothes, the books and the relics of his chapel, and very often the king's own bed, travelled with the household from house to house. That's why the French word for furniture is meubles, and the old English word for one's household goods was movables. A great deal of a medieval nobleman's furniture was designed to be taken apart, packed into chests, loaded onto carts and horses, and set up again somewhere else.

Think of what it meant, when the court was about to move. Some days ahead, officers of the household would ride on to the next house, to make sure it was ready: that the roofs were sound, the rooms swept, the stables cleared, the fuel cut, and lodgings found for everybody who couldn't fit inside. In the later Middle Ages, the men who rode ahead to arrange lodgings were called herbergers, from an old word for an inn or a shelter, and it's from them that we get our word harbinger, a sign of something that's about to come. Behind them came the carts and the packhorses, loaded with chests and bundles, the kitchen gear, the chapel goods, the wardrobe, and the bed. And behind those, at last, came the king and queen and their great household, riding through the winter countryside, with the villagers coming out of their doors to watch them pass.

And there was a man whose job it was to take care of exactly this.

Remember the Constitutio Domus Regis, the description of the king's household written in about 1136? In its list of the officers of the king's chamber, there is an entry for the bearer of the king's bed. I'll paraphrase the entry, because I'm relying on the translations rather than the Latin: he was to eat in the household, he had a small daily allowance for a man to help him, and he had a packhorse, with its fodder. That's all. One man, one helper, one horse, whose whole duty was to carry the king's bed from place to place. The same document gives the usher of the king's chamber fourpence for each day that the king was on a journey, for what the translation I've seen calls the king's litter, a kind of travelling couch. Even the king's resting place, it seems, had a daily allowance for the road.

By Henry the Third's time, the household was much bigger, and the king's baggage needed carts as well as horses. But the idea was the same. Wherever the king went, his bed went too.

So that's our first mystery solved. Why does the king sleep in a bed that came on a horse? Because the court of a medieval king was always moving, and the comforts of a royal chamber, the curtains and the coverlets and the mattresses and very often the bed itself, moved with it. A castle, most of the time, was an empty shell. It became a royal house only when the household arrived and unpacked. The richest man in England slept, in a sense, like a traveller. Like a very grand camper, carrying his own bedroll from place to place.

Now let me be careful here. Not every royal bed was a travelling bed. Some of the king's houses had fixed beds, built for a particular room. At Westminster, in the room that would come to be called the Painted Chamber, Henry had a great state bed that stood against the wall, framed by paintings, and the historians who write the UK Parliament's own heritage pages describe its posts as painted green and spangled with gold stars. That bed wasn't going anywhere. But many beds were made to come apart, and the most valuable part of a medieval bed, as we'll see in a moment, wasn't really the wood at all.

Let's think about that in modern terms. When you travel, you probably stay in a hotel, or rent a room, and you expect to find a bed made up and waiting for you, with clean sheets and pillows, and you'd be astonished if the hotel asked you to bring your own mattress. But think of the way some people travel even now. Musicians on tour who carry their own pillows. Families who take a travel cot for the baby. Anyone who has ever gone camping, and unrolled a sleeping bag in a tent, and felt at once a little more at home. Henry's bed was all of those things at once. It was the one familiar place in a life that was always on the road.

And here we come to the evening's next disagreement among historians. It's about beds themselves.

How big were medieval royal beds, really? And how many did a king own? You'll sometimes read about vast medieval beds, big enough for half a dozen people, and it's true that a few enormous beds survive from later centuries. The most famous, the Great Bed of Ware, now in the Victoria and Albert Museum, is about eleven feet wide. But it was made in the 1590s, more than three hundred years after our night, and it was a curiosity even then, a bed for an inn, famous precisely because it was so absurdly big. For the thirteenth century, almost no beds survive at all. Historians have to piece them together from documents, pictures and fragments.

The furniture historian Penelope Eames, in a great study of medieval furniture published in 1977, argued that we should think of the medieval great bed not mainly as a piece of carpentry, but as an arrangement of textiles. The wooden frame might be quite simple. What made a bed grand, and costly, and fit for a king, were the hangings: the canopy, the curtains, the coverlets, the cushions, the furs, all of which could be changed for different seasons and occasions, packed up and carried, and displayed. In great households of the later Middle Ages, inventories list whole sets of bed hangings, matched in colour and pattern, valued far more highly than the frame. Other scholars have emphasised that we know much less about the beds of the thirteenth century than about those of the fifteenth, and that it's dangerous to read the splendour of later inventories back into Henry's time. And as for how many beds a king owned, the honest answer is that nobody knows exactly. Certainly more than one. Certainly some fixed and some travelling. Beyond that, the records are patchy.

Hugh, I think, would agree with Penelope Eames. To him, the bed is not the frame. The bed is everything he puts on it.

He's turned now to the pile of linen he carried up the stair, and he begins.

First, he checks the bed itself. On the wooden frame, there's a lattice of cords or webbing, stretched tight, to take the weight of what goes on top. Over that, a mattress, a great flat bag of canvas stuffed with straw or wool, firm and heavy. Over that, a second, softer one, a featherbed, a sack of fine ticking filled with feathers and down, which sinks a little when he presses his hand into it. He shakes it, and pummels it, and turns it, to break up the lumps that formed when it was packed for the journey, and he smooths it flat again. Then he lays the sheets: fine linen, bleached white, smelling of cold air, because his wife and the other washerwomen dried them outside on frosty grass and then in front of a fire. One sheet below, one above. Then the coverlets, one of wool, and one of something richer, lined with fur, perhaps squirrel or rabbit, soft and grey in the candlelight. Then the pillows, two or three of them, stuffed with down, in linen covers. And over them, folded back, the great outer coverlet, embroidered at its edges.

He does it all without hurrying, and without a single wasted movement. He has done it a thousand times, in a dozen houses. He could do it in the dark.

When he's finished, he stands back again, and then he does one more thing. He takes the edge of the green curtain, and draws it a little way along its rail, so that it hangs closer around the head of the bed, where the draught from the window would reach the pillow. He looks at it. Moves it an inch. Looks again.

Then he nods.

Down in the outer chamber, Nicholas has fallen silent. He has crept to the inner door, and is watching his father through the gap, with the kind of attention that boys give only to things they really want to learn. Hugh sees him, and doesn't send him away. In a few years, Nicholas may be the one making this bed. And it's better, Hugh thinks, if he learns it properly.

## Part Five — Around Half Past Seven: The Queen's Rooms and the Washerwomen

The small question for this part of the evening is one that people very often ask about medieval kings and queens. Did they share a bedroom?

Let's follow Isabel, and find out.

She has climbed a different stair from her husband's, on the other side of a little court, and she is walking now along a narrow passage, a gallery with a wooden floor and small windows looking out over the dark courtyard. The jug of hot water is wrapped in a cloth against her hip. Her footsteps are quick and quiet. At the end of the passage there's a door, and a woman standing beside it, one of the queen's damsels, wrapped in a fur-lined mantle, who nods to her and lets her in.

These are the queen's rooms. And they are, quite clearly, a separate world from the king's.

The Queen of England is Eleanor. She was born in Provence, in the far south of what is now France, probably in about 1223, so she is twenty-nine or so on this Christmas night. She was the second of the four daughters of Raymond Berengar, Count of Provence, and his wife, Beatrice of Savoy, and every one of those four daughters became a queen. Her elder sister Margaret was married to King Louis the Ninth of France. Her younger sister Sanchia had married Henry's brother, Richard, Earl of Cornwall, who would one day be elected King of the Romans. And the youngest, Beatrice, was married to Louis's brother, Charles of Anjou, who would one day be King of Sicily. Eleanor came to England as a girl of about twelve, in the winter of 1236, and she was married to Henry at Canterbury Cathedral on the fourteenth of January that year, and crowned at Westminster a few days later. Tonight, she has been queen for very nearly seventeen years.

She came from one of the most cultured courts in Europe. Provence was the land of the troubadours, the poets who sang of love in the language of the south, and her family were great patrons of poetry and music. In England, Eleanor kept up a love of books and stories. Matthew Paris himself, the monk whose chronicle tells us about tonight's Christmas, wrote a life of King Edward the Confessor in French verse, with pictures, in these very years, and it's generally thought to have been made for Queen Eleanor, and perhaps presented to her. So, as far as we can tell, the same monk who grumbled about the queen in his Latin chronicle also wrote a beautiful book for her in French. Medieval people, like us, could hold two opinions at once.

So, did Henry and Eleanor share a bedroom?

The answer, in most of their houses, is that each of them had their own. In the great royal houses of the thirteenth century, the queen usually had her own suite of rooms: her own chamber, with its own bed, her own wardrobe, her own chapel, her own household of ladies and damsels and servants, often in a separate range of buildings, connected to the king's by a passage or a gallery or a stair. At Winchester, as we heard earlier, there was a queen's chapel with a gallery, for which the king ordered a glass window in the summer of 1252. At the Tower of London, the queen's lodgings were in a separate tower beside the king's, which survives today, rebuilt, as the Lanthorn Tower. At Clarendon, in Wiltshire, the queen had a whole range of rooms of her own.

And those rooms were not lonely. A queen of the thirteenth century was surrounded, from morning to night, by her own household: ladies of noble birth who were her companions, damsels who attended her, a confessor, clerks to keep her accounts, a steward, ushers and grooms and pages, cooks, a tailor, and the washerwomen like Isabel. The queen's household had its own officers, its own money and its own records, and when the court travelled, it travelled as a separate column within the great procession, with its own carts and its own beds. In later centuries, some of the queen's household accounts survive, roll by roll, recording what was bought for her chamber and her chapel and her table. They show a world as busy, and as carefully counted, as the king's.

That doesn't mean they never slept together. Of course they did. By this Christmas they had four living children, and a fifth, a daughter, was yet to come. The king could go to the queen's chamber, or she to his, by the private passages. But it does mean that the queen had a place that was hers, where she could withdraw with her own people. That's the answer to our small question. A king and queen had separate chambers, and separate beds, and met in one or the other. On which bed, on which nights, the records almost never say, and I'm certainly not going to guess at it on this one.

Now, the rooms. I'd like to tell you what the queen's chambers looked like, and here, the records are generous.

Henry took enormous care over Eleanor's rooms, in house after house. At the Tower of London, in 1238, two years after their marriage, he ordered her chamber there to be decorated, and the order, as Historic Royal Palaces quotes it, was for the chamber to be "painted with points and to cause flowers to be painted below the points." The points were a kind of false masonry, thin painted lines that made a plastered wall look as though it were built of neat, square blocks of stone. And below them, flowers. Two years after that, in 1240, the same chamber was whitewashed and painted all over with roses. Imagine that. A young queen, seventeen years old, from the sunny south, waking up in a room in a grey stone castle on the Thames, and seeing roses painted all around her walls.

Isabel isn't looking at the walls tonight, though. She has put her jug of hot water down by the fire in the outer chamber, where one of the queen's women will use it for the queen's washing later, and now she's at the chest in the corner, counting.

Because Isabel's work is linen. She is a washerwoman of the queen's household, one of the women who look after the queen's shifts, her veils and wimples, her towels, her sheets and her pillow covers. In the royal households, laundresses were among the very few women whose work turns up in the records at all, named or unnamed, paid for washing the linen of the king's or queen's chamber. Isabel is not grand. She doesn't attend the queen. She would never presume to speak to her. But she knows the queen's linen better than the queen does.

Imagine you are with her now, kneeling beside the chest in the light of a single candle. She lifts out the sheets, one at a time, and holds each up to the light, checking it. They're fine, smooth, cool to the touch, much finer than anything you'd find in an ordinary house. Some of this linen has come from as far away as Flanders and northern France, from towns famous for their weaving. She runs her thumb along a hem, looking for a fray. She sniffs a pillow cover, to make sure it smells of nothing but clean cloth and cold air. She lays out a fresh linen shift on top, folded small, the shift the queen will put on in the morning. Then she closes the chest.

How did a washerwoman in 1252 get linen this clean, with no soap powder and no hot tap?

With a great deal of work. The linen was soaked, and then washed in lye, a strong alkaline liquid made by pouring water through wood ashes and letting it drip through, which loosens dirt and grease. It could be washed with soap, too, a soft soap made from fat and lye, though the best hard soaps came from the south, from Castile and the Mediterranean, and were costly. The linen was beaten, with wooden bats, on boards or stones, and rinsed in great tubs, and wrung out between two people twisting in opposite directions. And then it was spread out to dry, on grass or hedges in summer, and on frosty days like this, near the fires of the laundry, and then smoothed with heavy, flat stones or glass slickers, or pressed. White linen was a sign of wealth, and keeping it white was a never-ending task.

And for all that work, the laundry was one of the warmest places in the castle to be on a winter day. There were fires to heat the water, and great coppers steaming, and the air was thick with damp warmth, so that the women worked with their sleeves rolled up while, outside the door, the courtyard froze. Isabel's hands may be red and sore, but on a morning like the one that's coming, she'll be warmer at her tubs than the soldiers on the walls.

Let's set that beside your own home. Somewhere in your house, perhaps under a kitchen counter or in a cupboard, there's a washing machine. You put the clothes in, you press a button, and an hour or so later they come out clean. You might have a dryer, too. The whole thing, from start to finish, takes perhaps a few minutes of your time. A single wash day for Isabel, with the soaking and the lye and the beating and the rinsing and the wringing and the drying, might take her and her companions from dawn to dusk, and leave their hands red and cracked for days.

Now, here's our disagreement for this part of the night. And it's about the queen herself.

Eleanor of Provence was not popular in England, not in her own lifetime, and certainly not with the chroniclers. Matthew Paris disliked her, and disliked even more the crowd of relatives and followers who came with her from Provence and Savoy, and who were given English lands, marriages and offices. Her uncles, in particular, rose high: one of them, Boniface of Savoy, became Archbishop of Canterbury, and another, Peter of Savoy, was given the land beside the Thames where his palace, the Savoy, would later stand. Paris and other writers accused the queen of greed, of influence over the king, of favouring foreigners. Some years after our night, during the great crisis of Henry's reign, Londoners would show their hostility to her openly. For a long time, historians largely followed the chroniclers, and Eleanor came down to us as a grasping, unpopular foreign queen.

But in 1998, the historian Margaret Howell published a full study of her life, called Eleanor of Provence: Queenship in Thirteenth-Century England. Howell argued that Eleanor should be seen not as a villain but as a capable and determined woman doing what queens were expected to do: building a network of support, looking after her children's interests, managing her own lands and household, and playing a serious part in politics. She showed that much of the hostility towards Eleanor came from writers with their own reasons for disliking her. Other historians have taken different positions, some more sympathetic still, some insisting that Eleanor's ambitions really did help drive England towards civil war. Howell also reminds us, by the way, that the king and queen seem to have quarrelled earlier in 1252, and to have been reconciled by the end of the year. So, on this Christmas night, as far as the record can tell us, they were on good terms again.

What both sides would agree on is that Eleanor was deeply devoted to her children. And tonight, I imagine, she is thinking about one of them in particular.

Because exactly one year ago, at Christmas in 1251, the royal family had been far away in the north, at York. And there, on the day after Christmas, the twenty-sixth of December, Eleanor's eldest daughter, Margaret, then eleven years old, was married to the young King of Scots, Alexander the Third, who was ten. It was a famous occasion, with a great gathering of English and Scottish nobles, and Matthew Paris wrote about it at length. And then the little girl went north to Scotland, to live as a queen, among strangers. Tonight, she is twelve, and far away, in a Scottish castle. Whatever Eleanor is feeling about that, no one wrote it down. But every mother can guess.

The other royal children are not here, as far as anyone can tell. The lord Edward, the heir, is thirteen, the same age as our Nicholas. There's Beatrice, who is ten, and Edmund, who is nearly eight. Royal children in these years were often brought up at Windsor, in their own household, with their own nurses and tutors, and visited by their parents when the court passed that way. Whether any of them were at Winchester for this Christmas, I can't tell you. So tonight, I'll leave them at Windsor, asleep.

Isabel, meanwhile, has finished at the chest. She stands, and straightens her back, which aches, and pushes a strand of hair under her linen headcloth. One of the queen's damsels, passing, gives her a small nod of thanks. And Isabel goes out, along the cold gallery, and down the stair, to find her children.

She finds Margery first, waiting for her at the foot of the stair, with a basket of folded towels, and her nose red from the cold. And behind her, as usual, Peter, who has a smear of something on his face that might be honey, and is pretending he doesn't.

Isabel wipes his face with the corner of her apron, and takes the basket from Margery, and the three of them go back together across the frozen court, towards the warmth.

## Part Six — Around Half Past Eight: Fire in the Wall, Glass in the Window

Here is this hour's small question. On a freezing December night, inside walls of stone and flint, how do you actually keep a king warm?

Hugh is about to show us. He's back in the inner chamber, kneeling at the small fireplace near the bed, and he's lighting it.

Imagine you are kneeling beside him on the rushes. The fireplace in front of you is set right into the thickness of the wall. It has a flat back of stone and tiles, blackened by many fires, and on either side, short stone jambs, and above, projecting out into the room, a hood, shaped a little like an upturned funnel, made of stone and plaster, that catches the smoke and guides it up into a flue running inside the wall, and out into the night air through a vent high above. On the hearth, there are two iron firedogs, holding up the logs, so that the air can get underneath them. Hugh lays a little kindling of dry twigs and splinters between them, and touches his taper to it, and blows, very gently, and the flames run up through the wood, and in a moment there's a fire, crackling, filling that small square of the wall with orange light.

And the smoke goes up. Not into the room. Up, into the hood, and away.

That might seem like nothing to you. But in the England of 1252, it was something quite special.

Most people in the kingdom, in their own homes, still lived around an open hearth in the middle of the floor, with no chimney at all, the smoke drifting up into the rafters and finding its way out through the thatch or a hole in the roof. Even in the great hall of this castle, as we saw, the main fire is a central hearth. But for smaller rooms, and especially for the private chambers of great people, there was another way. The fire could be moved to the side of the room, into the wall, with a flue to carry the smoke away. And this was not a new idea. Some of the great stone keeps built in England after the Norman Conquest, more than a hundred and fifty years before our night, already had fireplaces built into their walls, with short flues that let the smoke out through small vents in the outer face of the wall. You can still see examples at Rochester Castle, in Kent, and at Colchester, in Essex.

But Henry the Third was a great builder of fireplaces. In order after order in the Liberate Rolls, he asked for fireplaces to be made in his chambers and his wife's, in his chapels and wardrobes, at house after house. We met one of them earlier tonight: the plaster fireplace in the great tower here at Winchester, ordered in June of this very year. The rolls also show him asking for fireplaces with hoods, and for their chimneys to be carried up properly, so that they would draw. He was a king who disliked being cold, and who disliked smoke even more. And he was rich enough to do something about it.

How do you make a fireplace like this? In stone, if you can afford it, with dressed blocks for the jambs and the hood. But many of Henry's fireplaces, like the one ordered for Winchester's great tower, were made of plaster, laid over a framework and smoothed, and then painted, so that the hood itself could carry colour and pattern. The back of the hearth was protected with tiles, or with stones set on edge, because a fire burning against bare wall would crack it in a single winter. And there was a craft to the flue, too. Too wide, and it would let in the rain and the cold. Too narrow, and it would choke and smoke. The masons who built them learned by doing, and by failing, and by being sent back to do it again when the king complained.

That's the first part of the answer to our small question. You keep a king warm with fire, of course. But with a particular kind of fire. A small one, in a small room, in a fireplace built into the wall, which throws its heat out into the room and sends its smoke away.

What does he burn? Wood, mostly. Great quantities of it, cut in the royal forests and carried in by cart. There's a little charcoal, perhaps, for braziers. And there was coal, too, in thirteenth-century England, though not much of it here in the south. It came by sea from the north-east, from around Newcastle, which is why people called it sea-coal, and its smoke was thick and sulphurous and unpleasant. There's a story often told about Queen Eleanor and that smoke: that a few years after our night, in 1257, she left Nottingham Castle because the smoke of sea-coal burning in the town below was so bad. The story is repeated in many books, and it may well be true, but I haven't been able to check its original source for myself, so please hold it lightly. What's certain is that tonight, in this chamber, the fire is of good, dry wood, and it smells of oak and beech and of the forest.

And there's no shortage of forest around Winchester. Hampshire was a county of royal forests. To the south-west lay the greatest of them all, the New Forest, which William the Conqueror had set aside for his hunting nearly two centuries before. To the south-east lay the Forest of Bere. In a royal forest, the deer and the wood belonged, in law, to the king, and special forest courts punished anyone who took them without leave. So when the king needed fuel for a Christmas at Winchester, it could come from his own woods, cut by his own men, carried in by cart along the frozen tracks. Somewhere in the woodpile by Hugh's fireplace tonight, there may well be a log of oak that grew in the New Forest, in the shade of trees where William Rufus, the Conqueror's son, was killed by an arrow while hunting, back in the year 1100.

Every log, in other words, has a long road behind it. Cut in the autumn, stacked and dried, loaded onto a cart, hauled to the castle, unloaded into the woodstore, carried across the courtyard by a boy like Nicholas, up the stair, and laid at last on the king's hearth. And in a few hours, it will be ash.

Now, the second part of the answer. Heat is only half the battle. You also have to keep it in.

Look around the room again, now that the fire is burning. The walls are plastered and painted, and in many of Henry's chambers, the lower part of the walls were also lined with wooden panels, wainscot, which kept the cold of the stone away from the room. Henry ordered it often. In one order addressed to the sheriff of Wiltshire, which John Ruskin quoted from the rolls in the nineteenth century, the king asks for the wainscot of one of his chambers to be painted green, and in another, for a wainscot of green colour, spotted with gold. And one of the nineteenth-century books on English domestic architecture that gathered these orders together records that at Winchester itself, a royal chamber was painted green, with stars of gold. That's Henry's colour scheme again: a green room, like the inside of a wood, with golden stars scattered across it.

Imagine waking in a room like that, on a winter morning. The firelight on the green walls. The gold stars catching the light as the flames move. A painted night sky, indoors, above your head. Henry seems to have liked his rooms to feel like a garden or a starlit wood, and in the depths of an English winter, I think I understand why.

And over the walls, at least in winter, there were hangings. Cloth, of wool or linen, sometimes painted, sometimes embroidered, hung from hooks or rails, which kept out the draughts, softened the echo of the stone, and added a layer of warmth between the people and the wall. The floor is covered with rushes and mats. The ceiling is boarded timber, which holds the heat far better than a vault of bare stone. And the windows.

The windows are the hardest part.

In this chamber, there are two of them, set deep into the thickness of the wall, with stone seats on either side, so that in summer you could sit there in the light. Tonight, they are dark. The lower part of each window is closed by wooden shutters, barred from the inside. The upper part is glazed, with small pieces of greenish-white glass, set in lead, in a pattern of lozenges. And outside the glass, as we heard, there's heavy ironwork, the iron bars that the king ordered in June, to make the windows strong.

Glass, in the thirteenth century, was still a costly thing, and not every royal window had it. Many had only shutters, or panels of oiled linen, or thin sheets of horn. But Henry ordered glass for his chambers and his chapels again and again, and he liked it to be white, which is to say clear, or as clear as the glassmakers of his time could make it, so that the light would come through. Glass doesn't keep out the cold as well as a modern double-glazed window would. It's thin, and the lead between the pieces lets in the draught. But it keeps out the wind and the rain, and it lets in the light, and with a wooden shutter closed behind it on a winter night, it does a decent job.

Imagine for a moment how one of those windows was made. The glass itself came from glasshouses, often deep in woodland, where there was plenty of timber to fuel the furnaces, and it was blown into great bubbles or cylinders, and opened out, and flattened into sheets while it was still soft. The sheets were never perfectly even. They were thicker in some places and thinner in others, and full of tiny bubbles, and slightly green or yellow from the iron in the sand. The glazier cut them into small pieces, not with a diamond, but by drawing a line with a hot iron and then nibbling away the edge with a notched tool called a grozing iron. Then he fitted the pieces into strips of lead, grooved on both sides, and soldered the joints, and pressed a kind of putty into the gaps to keep out the wind. It was slow, careful, skilled work, and every little lozenge of greenish light in the king's window had been held, at some point, between a glazier's fingers.

So that's our full answer. You keep a king warm with a fire in the wall, a small room, wooden panels and painted plaster, cloth on the walls, rushes on the floor, shutters on the windows, glass where you can afford it, a door that closes, and a curtained bed. None of them, on its own, is enough. But together, on a night like this, they make a warm, close, glowing little box of a room, in the heart of the cold castle.

Let's set that beside your own home. Your house is probably heated by a system you never see. A boiler, or a furnace, or a heat pump, somewhere out of sight, sends warmth through pipes or ducts to every room, and a thermostat on the wall turns it up and down by itself. Your windows are double- or triple-glazed, sealed in their frames. Your walls and loft are full of insulation. You can set the whole house to twenty degrees and forget about it. The king of England, in his own castle, had none of that. He had Hugh, kneeling at a fireplace with a taper. And Hugh would have to come back, again and again, through the night, to keep it going.

Now, here is our disagreement for this hour. And it's about chimneys.

When did chimneys become common in England? There's a famous passage, written in 1577 by a clergyman named William Harrison, in his Description of England. Harrison wrote, and I'll paraphrase him, that old men in his village had noticed great changes in their lifetimes, and that one of them was the great number of chimneys that had lately been built, whereas when they were young, there had been hardly two or three chimneys in most country towns, and people had made their fires against a reredos in the hall. That passage has been quoted again and again as evidence that chimneys spread across England only in the sixteenth century.

But architectural historians have long pointed out that the story is more complicated. Margaret Wood, in her great survey The English Mediaeval House, published in 1965, gathered examples of fireplaces and chimneys in stone houses, castles and manor houses going back to the twelfth century. In great houses, wall fireplaces were well known for centuries before Harrison's old men were born. And yet, in ordinary homes, and even in the great halls of rich men, the open central hearth went on being used for a very long time, sometimes by choice, because a central fire heats a big room more evenly and gives everyone a place around it. So both things are true at once. The chimney was old. And the chimney was new. It all depends on whose house you're standing in. Tonight, in the king's chamber, it's old. A mile away, in a cottage beyond the city walls, it's still a thing of the future.

Hugh sits back on his heels, and watches the fire for a while. It's catching well. The logs are beginning to glow along their undersides. He adds one more, carefully, crosswise, so it won't roll. Then he gets up and goes to the shutters, and runs his hand along their edges, feeling for draughts. There's one, at the bottom of the left-hand window, a thin, steady thread of cold air. He tucks the strip of cloth more firmly into the gap. The thread of cold goes away.

There's a sound at the outer door. It's Nicholas again, with another basket of wood, and this time, behind him, Margery, carrying a pile of towels, and Peter, carrying nothing at all, but very determined to be part of whatever's happening. Hugh lets them come as far as the inner door, and no further. The children stand in a little row on the threshold, looking in. The firelight is on their faces. They can see the green curtains, and the white pillows, and the gleam of fur on the coverlet. Peter's mouth is open.

Hugh lets them look for a moment. Then he puts a finger to his lips, and shoos them gently back into the outer room, where there's work to do. The king will be coming soon.

## Part Seven — Around Half Past Nine: Guards Beside the Pillow

The small question for this hour is this. When a medieval king finally goes to bed, what actually happens? Who does what, and in what order?

I have to be honest with you before we begin, because this is a place where it would be very easy to make things up. Nobody, as far as I know, wrote down exactly how Henry the Third went to bed on Christmas night in 1252. The rolls tell us about his rooms. The chronicle tells us where he kept his feast. But the ordinary little ritual of a king's bedtime, the order of things, the who and the how, comes to us mostly from household books written later in the Middle Ages, in the fourteen hundreds, by men who served in great households and wanted to teach others how it was done. So what I'm going to describe now is drawn from those books. It's the kind of thing that happened. It is not a record of this night.

The most charming of those books is called the Boke of Nurture. It was written in English verse, probably in the middle of the fifteenth century, by a man named John Russell, who tells us that he had served as usher and marshal in the household of Humphrey, Duke of Gloucester, the youngest brother of King Henry the Fifth. It was printed in 1868 by the scholar Frederick Furnivall, in a collection he called Early English Meals and Manners. And among its instructions for the butler and the carver and the usher, there's a section on the duties of a chamberlain, the servant who looks after his lord's chamber and his person. I'll paraphrase what he says, rather than quote, because Russell's Middle English spelling is wonderfully wild, and I'd rather give you the sense than stumble over the letters.

The chamberlain, Russell says, should be diligent and clean. In the evening, he should be ready when his lord comes to bed. He should help him off with his gown and his outer clothes, and lay them carefully aside. He should have a stool or a cushion ready, and help his lord off with his shoes and hose. He should comb his lord's hair, and put on his nightcap or kerchief. He should have the bed made ready, the sheets and pillows laid, and the curtains drawn. He should set out the basin and the towel, and see that there's water. And then he should see that the necessary things for the night are in their places, including the chamber pot, and he should make sure that the fire and the light are safe. And then, when all is done, he should draw the curtains, and bow, and go.

In Henry's household, the men who did this kind of work belonged to the king's chamber, a world of its own within the household. At the top of it were great men, officers with titles and lands, who had the right to be close to the king. But the daily work of the chamber, the fires and the linen and the candles and the basins, was done by men like Hugh, the ushers and servants and valets, whose names appear now and then in the accounts when they are paid their wages or given a gift of cloth. They were trusted, because they had to be. They saw the king tired, ill, cross, unwell, half dressed, asleep. They knew things about him that no chronicler ever wrote down. And they kept those things to themselves.

Tonight, there are two of them on duty in the chamber: Hugh, and an older man who has served the king since before Hugh was born, and who moves a little stiffly now, but whose hands are as sure as ever. They don't need to speak. They've done this together so many times that each knows exactly where the other will be. One takes the gown. One kneels for the shoes. One holds the basin. One has the towel. It's like watching two people dance a dance they've known all their lives.

Imagine you're standing in the shadows of the outer chamber now, by the wall, out of the way, the way a servant learns to stand. The door from the stair opens. There are voices, low and quiet. A knight of the household comes in first, and then two clerks, and then, among them, a man of about forty-five, of middling height and solid build, in a long dark gown trimmed with fur, his face tired from a long day of feasting and prayer. He's speaking quietly to the man beside him. He doesn't look at you. Why would he? You are a servant, standing by a wall.

That's all I'll give you of the king himself. A tired man, at the end of Christmas Day, coming to his rooms.

And then the little ritual begins, much as Russell describes it. Hugh comes forward, with another of the chamber servants, and they help the king out of his gown. They lay it over a perch. They kneel to take off his shoes. A basin is held, and he washes his hands and face. Hugh has his nightcap ready, warmed by the fire. The clerks bow and go. The knight of the household stays, at the door. And the king, in his linen shirt and his warm robe, goes to the little desk below the crucifix, and kneels.

That's our small question answered. A king goes to bed the way a great many people of his time did, only with more help. He's undressed. He washes. He prays. And then he lies down, and somebody draws the curtains around him.

Henry the Third was known, all his life, for his piety. He heard mass, often several times a day. He was devoted, above all, to the memory of King Edward the Confessor, the saintly English king who had died nearly two hundred years before, and whose abbey at Westminster Henry was rebuilding, at enormous cost, in this very decade. Two and a half years before our night, in 1250, Henry had taken the cross, vowing to go on crusade. Whatever else people said about him, and they said a great deal, no one doubted that he prayed.

And that, at last, brings us to the second of our three mysteries.

I asked you, at the very beginning of the night: why might a king, lying behind walls many feet thick, guarded by soldiers at every gate, have asked for a painting of armed men to be put up beside his pillow?

The answer starts with an order in the rolls. Among the orders that John Ruskin and the nineteenth-century antiquaries copied out of Henry's records, there's one addressed to the sheriff of Southampton, which in the thirteenth century meant the county we now call Hampshire, the county of Winchester. It tells him to have painted, on a tablet beside the king's bed, the guards of Solomon's bed. I should tell you plainly that the version I've been able to see doesn't make it absolutely clear which of the king's houses the order was meant for, so I can't promise you that the painting hung in this room. But it was ordered for a royal bed, in Hampshire's account, and it tells us something precious about what a king like Henry wanted beside him as he slept.

What were the guards of Solomon's bed?

They come from the Bible, from the Song of Songs, the great love poem of the Old Testament, which every educated person in Henry's world knew well, in Latin. In the third chapter, there's a passage describing the bed of King Solomon. In the King James Version, the English translation of 1611, the passage reads like this:

"Behold his bed, which is Solomon's; threescore valiant men are about it, of the valiant of Israel. They all hold swords, being expert in war: every man hath his sword upon his thigh because of fear in the night."

Because of fear in the night. In the Latin Bible that Henry knew, the Vulgate, those last words are propter timores nocturnos: because of the fears of the night.

So that's our answer. The king wanted a picture of armed men beside his bed because of fear in the night. Not, I think, because he really expected an enemy to come through the walls of his own castle on Christmas night. There were real guards for that, at the gate, on the stair, at his door. But the night, in the medieval imagination, was full of other dangers too. Bad dreams. Temptations. Sudden death in sleep, without a priest, without confession. Evil spirits, which, in the beliefs of the time, were thought to be most active in the dark. The painted guards were a prayer in paint. They said: this bed, like Solomon's, is protected. The king is a king like Solomon, wise and just and favoured by God, and around his sleep stand sixty valiant men with swords on their thighs, keeping off the fears of the night.

It fits so well with the prayers that Henry would have heard, night after night, at the last service of the day, compline, sung just before bed in every monastery and royal chapel. Compline had a hymn, Te lucis ante terminum, To thee before the close of day, which asked God to keep watch through the night, and its second verse asks that dreams and the phantoms of the night may flee far away: procul recedant somnia, et noctium phantasmata. Far off let dreams depart, and the phantoms of the nights. The painted guards beside the pillow were saying the same thing, in colour, in the candlelight, all night long.

Let's set that beside our own lives. Many of us have our own small rituals against the fears of the night. A child may sleep with a night light, or a favourite toy, or the door left open a crack. Plenty of grown-ups check the locks twice before bed, or set an alarm system, or keep a phone on the bedside table, just in case. Some people still say a prayer. The fears may be different, but the wish behind them is old. We want something standing guard while we sleep. Henry asked for sixty painted swordsmen.

Now, here's our disagreement for this hour. And it's about what a royal bedchamber was for.

You might assume that a king's bedroom was a private room. But think about Henry's great chamber at Westminster, the one that came to be known as the Painted Chamber, with its state bed against the wall and its enormous painting of the coronation of Edward the Confessor beside it. The art historian Paul Binski, in his study The Painted Chamber at Westminster, published in 1986, showed how that room worked as both a bedchamber and a place of royal business and ceremony, where the king might receive important visitors, and where its paintings, of kings and virtues and biblical battles, sent out messages about what kind of king Henry wished to be. In that reading, the bed itself was partly a symbol, an image of kingship, as much as a place to sleep. Other historians have stressed that kings also needed real, private rooms, and that as the thirteenth century went on, they built more and more of them, smaller and further from the public spaces, so that the great chamber became more public while the king slept somewhere quieter. It's likely that both things were happening at once. A royal bed was a place to sleep, and a royal bed was a statement. The painting of Solomon's guards, beside a royal pillow, is both. It's a private prayer, and it's a public claim.

There's another small clue to what Henry wanted his chamber to say, and it comes from Westminster. In his great chamber there, he had a motto painted, in French, in large letters. It's usually given as Ke ne dune ke tine ne prent ke desire, and it means, more or less: he who does not give what he holds does not get what he wants. A strange thing, you might think, to have written up near your bed. But it was a motto about generosity, the great virtue of a medieval king, who was expected to give freely, to the poor, to the church, to his followers. Every morning, Henry woke up to a reminder to be generous. Whatever his critics said about his spending, he seems to have taken it to heart.

The king rises from his prayer desk. Hugh is waiting by the bed, holding back the curtain. The king climbs in, onto the deep featherbed, under the linen sheets and the fur-lined coverlet, and lies back against the warm pillows. Hugh draws the coverlet up. He checks that the candle on the table is safe, standing in its holder, away from the curtains. He banks the small fire a little, so it will burn slowly. He bows. Then he draws the green curtains closed, all the way round, so that the bed becomes a small dark room within the room, warm and close and still.

And in the candlelight, beside it, if the order was for this room, stand the painted guards of Solomon's bed, with their swords upon their thighs, because of fear in the night.

Hugh goes out into the outer chamber, and pulls the inner door almost closed, leaving it open a hand's width, so that he can hear. The knight of the household nods to him, and goes out to the stair.

Nicholas is sitting by the outer fire, his knees drawn up, looking at his father with shining eyes. He saw the king. He saw his father help the king into bed. He is going to remember this night for the rest of his life.

Hugh sits down on the bench beside him, and lets out a long, slow breath. Then he puts his hand on his son's shoulder, and squeezes it, and says nothing at all.

## Part Eight — Around Half Past Ten: Where Everyone Else Lies Down

Here is the small question for this hour of the night. The king is in his bed, behind his curtains. The queen is in hers. But there are hundreds of other people inside these walls tonight. So where, exactly, do they all sleep?

Let's go and see. Quietly, now.

Imagine that you have slipped out of the king's outer chamber, and down the dark stair, and across the frozen court, and in through the screens at the lower end of the great hall. The feast is long over. The high table has been cleared, and the cloth of estate has been taken down. The trestle tables have been lifted off their legs and stacked against the walls. The great central fire has burned down to a broad bed of red embers, glowing on its hearth, giving off a deep, steady heat. And all around it, on the floor, on the benches along the walls, in the aisles between the marble columns, there are people. Lying down. Wrapped in cloaks and blankets. Some on straw pallets, some on nothing but a layer of rushes and their own mantle. A few are still talking, in low murmurs. Most are already asleep. Somebody, somewhere, is snoring, with great confidence.

This is the first part of the answer to our small question. In a great medieval household, a hall was not only a place for eating. At night, for many of the lower servants, and for travellers and guests of lesser rank, it was a bedroom too. A huge, shared, smoky, snoring bedroom, with the fire in the middle.

But not everybody sleeps here. Not by any means.

The great officers of the household, and the most important guests, will have lodgings of their own: chambers inside the castle, or houses in the city. The knights and clerks of the household may share rooms, two or three to a bed, in lodgings around the courts. The grooms and the boys who look after the horses will sleep in the stables, in the hay, close to the animals, where it's warm, and where they can keep an eye on the most valuable horses in England. The cooks and scullions sleep near the kitchens, by the warmth of the great ovens. The men of the watch don't sleep at all, or not much. And many of the people who came with the court for Christmas, the hangers-on and petitioners and merchants and minstrels, are lodged out in the city, in inns and houses and the guest-halls of the religious houses, because the castle simply cannot hold them all. When the court came to a town like Winchester, the whole town filled up.

And where there were beds, they were shared. It was perfectly ordinary, in the Middle Ages, for two or three people of the same sex and the same rank to share a bed, whether they were brothers, or friends, or fellow servants, or travellers who'd met that very evening at an inn. Your bedfellow was simply the person you slept beside, and on a winter night, a bedfellow was a blessing, because two people under one blanket are warmer than one. Knights of the household might share a bed in their lodgings. Clerks might share. Pages and boys certainly did. Only the very greatest people expected to sleep in a bed alone, and even they, as we'll see, were rarely truly alone in the room.

There's a gentle reminder of this in the very word we use for the people we share our lives with. Long after the Middle Ages, people still used the word bedfellow for a close companion, and Shakespeare's line about misery acquainting a man with strange bedfellows is still quoted today. In Henry's world, it wasn't a figure of speech. It was simply true. Travellers on the road, arriving late at a crowded inn, might well find themselves sharing a bed with a stranger, and think nothing of it, except to hope that he didn't snore, or steal the blankets.

Some of the beds in the castle tonight are proper bedsteads, with frames. Many are not. A great household carried a great many simple beds that could be rolled up or folded: a mattress, or a sack of straw, a bolster, a pair of sheets and a blanket, tied up in a bundle in the morning and laid out again at night. In some of the later household books, there are low beds on small wheels, which could be pushed under a bigger bed during the day, and pulled out at night for a servant to sleep on. Later centuries would call them truckle beds. Wherever the household went, the bundles of bedding went too, piled high on the carts.

So that's our answer. They sleep everywhere. In chambers and halls, in lodgings and stables, in kitchens and inns. Each according to his rank, and her work, and the warmth that was granted to them.

Now, let's go back up the stair, to our family. Because tonight, Hugh has done something a little special.

Normally, Isabel and the children would sleep with the other servants of the queen's household, in a crowded room near the laundry. But tonight is Christmas, and the king's wardrobe, a small room opening off the outer chamber, where the king's clothes and linen and chests are kept, has a little space on its floor, and it's warm, because it shares a wall with the chamber fireplace. And so Hugh has laid out two straw pallets in there, between the chests, with woollen blankets, and his family has come up the stair to sleep near him.

Imagine you're in the wardrobe with them now. It's a narrow room, smelling of cedar and wool and lavender, lined with chests and with wooden pegs from which hang the king's robes, in their linen covers, like a row of sleeping ghosts. There's a little window, shuttered. There's no fire in here, but the wall that backs onto the chamber fireplace is warm to the touch, and Isabel has put the children's pallet right up against it. Margery and Peter are on it already, wrapped in a blanket together, whispering. Nicholas is still sitting with his father, by the outer fire, refusing to admit that he's tired.

I want to tell you something about that word, wardrobe, because it's one of the most interesting words in the castle.

Today, a wardrobe is a piece of furniture where you hang your clothes. But in the thirteenth century, it meant a room, a place where clothes and other things were guarded, warded, kept. And it meant something else too. Henry the Third's Wardrobe, with a capital W, was one of the great departments of royal government, which handled a large part of the king's money, buying everything from cloth and spices to wax and armour, and travelling with him wherever he went. Its clerks kept accounts that survive in the National Archives. So the word could mean a cupboard, a room, or a whole office of state. And there's a third meaning, too, which I'll come to gently. In castles, the small private rooms set into the walls, with a seat over a shaft that dropped down to a pit or the moat below, were also called wardrobes, or in the French form, garderobes. A medieval privy, in other words.

Henry cared a great deal about those, too. His orders to his keepers of works often concern the cleaning, the rebuilding and the improving of the privies in his chambers and his queen's, because a badly placed or badly built one could make a whole set of rooms smell. In a royal chamber, the privy was usually just a few steps away, through a little door, in the thickness of the wall. And for the night itself, there were chamber pots, of metal or earthenware, kept close by the bed, so that nobody had to go wandering cold passages in the dark.

Let's set all this beside a modern home. Most of us sleep in a bedroom of our own, or with a partner, and the idea of sleeping on the floor of a great hall with sixty strangers would horrify us. The nearest we come to it might be a youth hostel, or a sleeper train, or a festival campsite, or a long-haul flight, with rows of people dozing under thin blankets in the dark. And most of us take for granted a bathroom down the hall, with hot water from a tap, and a toilet that flushes. In Winchester Castle tonight, the king himself has nothing like that. He has a pot, a privy in the wall, and a basin of water that Hugh warmed by the fire.

Now, a word about washing, since we're here. Remember the Constitutio Domus Regis, the household list from about 1136? Among the officers of the chamber, there's one called the ewerer, the man in charge of water and basins and towels. I'll paraphrase his entry, from memory of the translations, and please treat the details as something to check: the ewerer was to have double food, and a penny, when the king was travelling, for drying the king's clothes, and fourpence whenever the king took a bath, except on the three great feasts of the year. Think about that for a moment. Some seven centuries after the Romans left Britain, taking their great bathhouses with them, the king's household still had a man whose duties included drying the king's clothes on the road and preparing his bath. Kings did bathe, in great wooden tubs lined with cloth, filled with water heated in the kitchens and carried up in buckets. It wasn't something you'd do every day. But it was done.

And here is our disagreement for this hour. It's about privacy.

How private, really, was royal sleeping? For a long time, the standard story, told by historians like Mark Girouard in his book Life in the English Country House, published in 1978, was that privacy grew slowly over the centuries. In the early Middle Ages, he suggested, great lords lived largely in public, in the hall, among their households, and only gradually withdrew into smaller private chambers, until by the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the great hall had become a grand entrance and the family lived in their own apartments. It's a beautiful story, and there's a lot of truth in it.

But other historians have complicated it. Christopher Woolgar, in The Great Household in Late Medieval England, published in 1999, showed how carefully great households of the later Middle Ages controlled who could go where, with chambers set aside for the lord and his family, and access to them limited and guarded, which suggests that privacy, of a kind, was valued much earlier than the old story allowed. Amanda Richardson, studying the royal palaces, and especially the queen's lodgings at Clarendon, has argued that the separate spaces of kings and queens in the thirteenth century tell us a great deal about rank, about gender and about the wish for withdrawal. And yet, as we'll see before the night is out, even the most private royal chamber was rarely truly empty. So was a medieval king's sleep private? Yes, by the standards of his time. And no, by ours. It depends on what you mean by alone.

The rushes on the floor of the chambers are worth a moment too, since everyone is lying so close to them tonight. Fresh rushes, cut from riversides and marshes, were strewn across the floors of great houses, and sweet-smelling herbs and flowers were often mixed in with them, to freshen the air. In the summer, they'd be changed often. In the depths of winter, it was harder, and the rushes might stay down longer, with fresh ones laid on top of the old. Here in the king's rooms, for the feast, they're fresh, and they crunch softly underfoot, and they smell faintly green, like a riverbank in summer.

Back in the wardrobe, Isabel has lain down beside her younger children, still in her kirtle and her headcloth, with her cloak spread over all three of them. Margery has stopped whispering. Peter has not. He is asking his mother whether the king has a pillow, and whether it's soft, and whether the king's dog sleeps on the bed, and whether the king is cold. Isabel tells him, very quietly, that the king is as warm as a loaf in an oven, and that if Peter doesn't stop talking, he'll wake him, and then they'll all be in trouble. Peter considers this, and stops talking.

Out in the outer chamber, Hugh has laid his own pallet across the floor. Not just anywhere. He has laid it right in front of the inner door, the door to the king's chamber, so close that anyone wanting to go in would have to step over him.

Nicholas watches him do it, and asks him why.

Hugh tells him he'll understand, before the night is through.

Then he tells Nicholas to go to bed. And this time, Nicholas goes, and lies down at the foot of his mother's pallet in the wardrobe, curled up against the warm wall, and pulls the edge of the blanket over himself.

The castle is very nearly quiet now. In the hall, the embers glow. In the stables, the horses shift and sigh. In the chamber, behind the green curtains, the king lies still. And high on the walls, the watchmen pace along the wall-walk, wrapped against the frost, looking out over the sleeping city.

## Part Nine — Around Midnight: The Watch on the Walls

The small question for this part of the night is this. It's midnight, or near enough, on Christmas night. The king is asleep. The household is asleep. So who, in all of Winchester, is still awake?

More people than you might think.

Let's start up on the walls. Imagine that you have climbed a narrow stone stair inside one of the towers, feeling your way in the dark with one hand on the cold stone, and come out onto the wall-walk at the top of the curtain wall. The cold hits you at once. Up here, there's nothing between you and the sky but a low parapet of stone, notched with gaps. The wind is light, but it's bitter, and it finds every gap in your clothes. Below you, on one side, is the dark huddle of the castle's roofs, with a faint glow at one or two shuttered windows. On the other side, beyond the ditch, the whole city of Winchester lies spread out below you in the dark, its streets marked only by a scattering of tiny lights, a lantern here, a glowing window there, and the long, pale shape of the cathedral to the east.

That cathedral is worth looking at, even in the dark. It's enormous. The church that the Normans began here in 1079 has the longest nave of any medieval cathedral in Europe, and its shape against the sky, long and low and immensely solid, dominates the eastern end of the city. It's named for Saint Swithun, a bishop of Winchester in the ninth century, who asked, according to the old story, to be buried humbly outside the church, where the rain could fall on his grave. When his bones were moved inside, in the year 971, it was said to have rained for forty days, which is why, in England, people still say that if it rains on Saint Swithun's day, the fifteenth of July, it will rain for forty days more. Tonight, there's no rain at all. Only frost, and starlight, and the long black roof of Swithun's church.

And below you, on this side, just beyond the castle ditch, you can see the dark shape of the city's west gate, the Westgate, with its arch and its tower, where the road runs out of Winchester towards the west. A part of that gate still stands today, at the top of the high street, and you can go inside it. Tonight, its great doors are shut, and barred, and there's a lantern burning above the arch.

Down there among the sleeping streets lived all kinds of people, and some of them we know by name. One of them, living in Winchester in these years, was a Jewish woman named Licoricia, who became one of the most important moneylenders in England, and who did business with the king himself. In 2022, a bronze statue of her and her son was unveiled in Winchester, not far from where she once lived. On this Christmas night, the Jewish families of the city are not keeping the feast, of course. But they're sleeping in the same frost, under the same stars, in the same city, behind their own shutters.

And beside you, on the wall-walk, a man is standing, wrapped in a heavy cloak with the hood pulled up, his breath steaming, a spear leaning against the parapet beside him. He's one of the watch. He nods to you. He doesn't say anything. He's been up here for hours, and he'll be up here for hours more, and talking, he has found, only lets the cold in.

He's dressed for it, as well as he can be. A thick linen shirt, a woollen tunic, a padded jacket over that, the kind of quilted garment soldiers wore under or instead of armour, a hood that covers his head and neck and shoulders, woollen hose, leather boots stuffed with straw or wool, and over everything, the great cloak. Even so, the cold gets in. His feet went numb an hour ago. At the end of his watch, he'll go down to the guardroom, where there's a brazier of glowing charcoal, and stand over it until the feeling comes back, and drink something hot, and sleep for a few hours on a bench, and then do it all again tomorrow.

That's the first part of the answer to our small question. Somebody always watched. In a royal castle, with the king in residence, there were men at the gates, men on the walls, men on the stairs to the royal chambers, all through the night, taking it in turns.

And down in the city, there were watchmen, too. Keeping watch at night was an old duty, owed by the townsmen themselves, and in the years around our night, Henry's government was already sending out orders about how it should be done. Thirty-three years after our night, in 1285, Henry's son, Edward the First, would issue a great law about keeping the peace, which takes its name from this very city. It's called the Statute of Winchester. Among other things, it ordered that in walled towns, the gates should be closed from sunset until sunrise, and that watches should be kept in cities and boroughs and towns, as one translation puts it, "from the sun setting unto the sun rising." And if a stranger passed by in the night, the watchmen were to arrest him and hold him until morning. But, as I like to point out, the statute only required these night watches in the summer half of the year, from Ascension Day until Michaelmas. In the depths of winter, it seems, the law assumed that very few people would be out in the dark, and that the cold itself would keep the streets empty.

Tonight, in the castle, it's different. The king is here. So the watch goes on, winter or not.

What does the watchman actually watch for? Not, on a night like this, an army. There's no war in England this Christmas, and no enemy expected at the gate. He watches for fire, above all, because a fire in a crowded castle full of timber buildings, on a night when every hearth is burning, is the danger everyone fears most. He watches the city below for a glow where there shouldn't be one. He watches the gate, and the road, for any traveller arriving late. And he watches the sky, because a watchman learns the stars, and can tell the hours by them, roughly, as they wheel across the south.

Who else is awake? Look east, towards the cathedral.

The great church of Saint Swithun was not only a cathedral. It was also a monastery, the home of a community of Benedictine monks, and they followed the Rule of Saint Benedict, written in the sixth century. In its eighth chapter, the Rule says, and I'll paraphrase, that in wintertime, from the first of November until Easter, the brothers should rise at the eighth hour of the night, when they have slept for a reasonable part of the night, and have digested their food. The hours of the night in Benedict's reckoning were not our hours. They divided the time from dusk to dawn into twelve equal parts, so in winter, each hour of the night was long. But it means that, some time in the small hours, the monks of Saint Swithun's will rise from their beds in their dormitory, and file in the dark down the night stair into the cold choir of their church, by the light of a lantern, to sing the night office. Tonight, at the end of Christmas Day, they'll be singing the night office for the feast of Saint Stephen, the first martyr, whose day the church keeps tomorrow, the day after Christmas.

In the castle chapel, there may be a priest awake, too, keeping a lamp burning before the altar, as lamps burned in churches all night long.

And in the kitchens, a boy is awake, tending the great fires, so that they never quite go out. In the stables, a groom is awake, because one of the horses is restless. In the queen's rooms, one of her damsels is awake, sitting by the fire in the outer chamber, because the queen's women took it in turns to watch. And in the king's outer chamber, on his pallet across the inner door, Hugh is awake.

He didn't mean to be. He was asleep, an hour ago, deeply. But something woke him, the way something always does, at about this time of night. He's lying on his back now, in the dark, listening.

He can hear the fire in the outer chamber, burned down to a soft, ticking glow. He can hear Isabel breathing in the wardrobe, and Peter's little snuffling snores. He can hear the wind, faintly, outside the shutters. And from the inner chamber, through the gap in the door, he can hear the king. Not much. Just the slow, deep breathing of a sleeping man, behind his curtains, and once, the creak of the bed as he turns over.

Hugh gets up, very quietly, and goes to the door, and looks through. The candle on the table is burning low, but it's still burning, steady in its holder. The fire in the small fireplace has sunk to a bed of embers. He goes in, on bare feet, across the rushes, and kneels at the hearth, and lays one small log on the embers, very gently, so that it won't crackle, and watches it catch. Then he goes out again, and lies back down, across the door.

That's the rest of the answer to our small question. At midnight, in Winchester, the watchmen are awake on the walls, the monks are about to rise in their church, the kitchen boys are tending the ovens, the queen's damsel is keeping watch by her fire, and a servant of the king's chamber is kneeling at the hearth, keeping the king warm.

Some of the household books of later centuries speak of a special night-light for the lord's chamber, a squat candle of wax burning in a bowl, which some of them call a mortar, set somewhere safe to burn quietly all night. I'm not sure that name was used in Henry's day, and I haven't been able to check the passages myself, so I'll say only this: a small light was kept burning in a great man's chamber through the night, so that if he woke, or if anyone needed to come in, they would not be stumbling in the pitch dark.

Let's set this beside our own world for a moment. If you look out of your window tonight, wherever you are, you'll probably see streetlights, and the lights of other houses, and perhaps the glow of a city on the clouds. Somewhere nearby, there's a police station with officers on duty, and a fire station with a crew asleep in their bunks, ready, and a hospital with nurses walking the corridors. There may be cameras watching the street. A whole invisible army keeps watch over a modern town at night, mostly unseen. In Winchester in 1252, the army was smaller, and colder, and it carried spears and lanterns. But it was there.

And now, it's time for a disagreement among historians, and scientists, about this very hour of the night.

You may have heard that people in the past didn't sleep through the night in one block, as many of us try to do, but in two: a first sleep, from not long after dark until around midnight, and then a period of quiet wakefulness, and then a second sleep until morning. The idea was put forward by the historian A. Roger Ekirch, of Virginia Tech, in an article in the American Historical Review in 2001, called Sleep We Have Lost, and in his book of 2005, At Day's Close: Night in Times Past. He found hundreds of references, and later, he says, thousands, in diaries, letters, court records and literature, to people's first sleep and second sleep, as if everyone knew that the night came in two parts.

He found support in an experiment from the early 1990s, in which a psychiatrist at the United States National Institutes of Health, Thomas Wehr, kept volunteers in rooms that were dark for fourteen hours a night, like a winter night, and found that after a few weeks many of them began to sleep in two blocks, with a quiet waking period in between.

But in 2015, a team led by the sleep researcher Jerome Siegel, of the University of California, Los Angeles, studied three peoples who still live without electricity, the Hadza of Tanzania, the San of Namibia, and the Tsimane of Bolivia, and found that they generally did not sleep in two distinct blocks. Ekirch replied that he had only ever claimed that the pattern was common in pre-industrial Europe, with its long, cold nights. And in 2023, the writer Niall Boyce published a careful critique in the journal Medical History, arguing that the phrases first sleep and second sleep might be read in other ways, and Ekirch replied in turn, in 2024. The debate goes on.

So is Hugh, lying awake at midnight across the king's door, having his own quiet hour between two sleeps, as Ekirch would say? Or is he just a man who's been woken by a creak? I can't tell you. But on a night of sixteen hours' darkness, with a fire to tend and a king to listen for, I don't think Hugh would find it strange to be awake for a while.

Out on the walls, the watchman stamps his feet, and blows into his hands, and looks out towards the east. And there, over the low hills beyond the city, something is beginning to happen. A soft, pale glow is rising in the sky. In a little while, the moon will come up.

## Part Ten — Around One in the Morning: The Moon, the White Bear and the Man at the Door

Here is the small question for this hour. When the moon rises over Winchester tonight, what kind of moon will it be?

Let's go back up onto the wall-walk for a moment, beside the watchman, and wait for it.

Imagine you are standing there now, with your hands tucked under your arms, and your cloak pulled tight, looking east. Over the dark hills beyond the river, the glow in the sky has been growing for some time. And now, at last, a little after half past midnight, the edge of the moon slides up over the horizon, large and orange-gold, low and slightly squashed, the way the moon always looks when it first rises. It climbs, slowly. And as it climbs, you see that it isn't full. It's a half moon, waning, its bright side turned towards the west, towards the castle, as if it were looking back over its shoulder at the city.

That's the answer to our small question. It's a waning half moon, rising a little after midnight. And I should tell you how I know, because nobody in Winchester wrote it down. I worked it out with the same modern astronomical model I used for the sunset, for this place and this night. The full moon had come about a week before Christmas, and the new moon will come a week after. Tonight, the moon is exactly halfway between, and it will climb slowly across the southern sky through the rest of the night, and still be up, pale and high, when the sun rises.

There's an old word for this kind of moon, by the way, that sailors and farmers used for centuries: the last quarter. It rises around midnight, and sets around midday, and it gives a softer, later light than the full moon, coming up just as the household has gone to sleep. To the watchman on the wall, it's a friend. It means the second half of his long night won't be spent in the pitch dark.

As it climbs, its cold silver light begins to fall across the city. It touches the roof of the cathedral, and the frosted thatch and tiles of the houses, and the frozen surface of the water meadows along the river. It lays the long shadows of the castle towers across the courtyard. And it finds its way, through a crack between a shutter and a wall, into the king's wardrobe, where it lies across the floor in a thin, bright line, a hand's width from Nicholas's face.

Nicholas is awake.

He's been awake for a little while, lying on his side against the warm wall, listening to the castle, and watching the line of moonlight creep across the floor. He doesn't know what woke him. He isn't worried. He's simply awake, in the middle of the night, as if it were the most natural thing in the world.

Imagine that you're lying there beside him, on the edge of the straw pallet, under the corner of the blanket. The air on your face is cold, but the wall behind you is warm, and the blanket is warm, and the bodies of his mother and brother and sister are warm, a little nest of warmth in the dark. You can hear his father moving very quietly in the outer chamber, and the faint tick of the dying fire. You can smell the wool of the robes hanging on their pegs above you. And far off, outside, very faint, you can hear a bell. The monks of Saint Swithun's are rising for the night office.

Nicholas creeps to the door of the wardrobe, and looks out into the outer chamber, and sees his father, sitting up on his pallet across the inner door, wrapped in his blanket. Hugh looks up, and sees him, and beckons, and Nicholas pads across the cold floor and sits down beside him, and Hugh puts the blanket around both their shoulders.

And they talk. Very, very quietly, in whispers, so as not to wake anyone in either room.

What do they talk about? About the king, of course. About the feast. About the platter that came in at the end, and whether the cooks will let Nicholas have what's left of it in the morning. And then Hugh, because it's Christmas, and because his son is awake beside him in the moonlight, tells him a story. A true one, as it happens. A story that has been going round the whole household this autumn.

The king, Hugh tells him, has been given a bear. A white bear. A bear from the far, frozen north, sent by the King of Norway. And it's being kept at the Tower of London, by the river.

This, I'm happy to say, is true. In 1252, the year of our story, King Haakon the Fourth of Norway sent Henry the Third a white bear, almost certainly a polar bear, and Henry had it kept at the Tower, along with the other exotic animals that kings sent to one another as gifts. And the orders about it survive. The king ordered the sheriffs of London to pay for its keep, a few pence a day. And in a writ that was transcribed and published in the nineteenth century, he told them to provide, and I'll quote that published translation, "one muzzle and one iron chain, to hold that bear without the water; and one long and strong cord to hold the same bear when fishing in the river of Thames."

So, a few miles from Westminster, in the years after our night, a white bear from the Arctic was led out on a long rope to swim in the Thames and catch fish, while Londoners gathered on the bank to watch. The records disagree a little about the exact year of the muzzle order, and about whether the sheriffs paid fourpence a day or sixpence, so I won't press those details. But the bear was real. And I like to think of Nicholas, lying awake in the moonlight, imagining it: a great white bear, fishing in a grey river, on the end of a rope.

The white bear wasn't alone at the Tower. Almost twenty years before our night, in 1235, the Emperor Frederick the Second had sent Henry three leopards, a gift with a meaning, because leopards, or lions as we'd call them now, were the beasts on the royal arms of England. And a few years after our night, in 1255, the King of France, Louis the Ninth, Eleanor's brother-in-law, would send Henry an elephant, the first ever seen in England, and the king would order a special house built for it at the Tower. Matthew Paris went to see it, and drew it, from life, in the pages of his chronicle, and his drawing survives, a great grey creature with a long trunk and small, patient eyes, and a keeper standing beside it to show how big it was. People came from all over to look at it.

Nicholas, of course, has never seen any of these animals. He has only heard of them, the way children hear of wonders, in whispers, in the night. But he's heard of the leopards, and he's heard of the bear, and he's going to ask his father, one day, whether the king might take him to the Tower. His father will tell him that the king doesn't take servants' boys to see bears. And Nicholas will go on hoping anyway.

Boys like Nicholas, growing up in great households, were expected to learn manners as well as tasks, and later in the Middle Ages, little books of advice were written for them. One of the best known is called The Babees Book, written in English verse in the fifteenth century and printed by Frederick Furnivall in 1868, in the same collection as Russell's Boke of Nurture. I'll paraphrase its spirit, rather than quote it: be gentle and courteous, stand still when your lord speaks to you, look at him when he talks, keep your hands and your nails clean, don't fidget, don't lean against the wall, don't interrupt, and do what you're told quickly and quietly. It was written two centuries after our night, for boys of a higher rank than Nicholas. But I suspect Hugh has said a good many of the same things to his son, on a good many evenings, on the stairs of a good many castles.

There's another story about Henry the Third that I'll tell you now, because it's one of the few times a chronicler tells us something the king said, and I want to be careful with it. Matthew Paris records that in the summer of 1258, some years after our night, the king was on the Thames in his barge when a terrible thunderstorm broke, and he put in to shelter at the bishop of Durham's house beside the river, where Simon de Montfort, Earl of Leicester, was staying. Simon was his brother-in-law, and the two men had a stormy relationship of a different kind. And according to Paris, when the earl told him not to be afraid, because the storm was passing, the king replied that he feared thunder and lightning beyond measure, but that he feared the earl more than all the thunder and lightning in the world. I'm paraphrasing Paris rather than quoting him, and remember, Paris could embroider. But it's a lovely, human glimpse. The King of England was afraid of thunderstorms.

And that brings us to our disagreement for this hour. It's about Henry himself.

What kind of king was Henry the Third? For a long time, historians weren't very kind to him. In the great Victorian histories, and for much of the twentieth century, he often appeared as a weak, pious, extravagant and rather foolish king, easily led by favourites, always short of money, bad at war, and lucky to keep his throne. There's some truth in each of those charges. But the historian David Carpenter, in a long series of studies, including The Reign of Henry III, published in 1996, and a great biography, the first volume of which appeared in 2020, has painted a much richer picture. Carpenter's Henry is a king of genuine piety and real kindness, who kept the peace in England for long stretches, who poured his money into beauty and charity, who was devoted to his wife and children, and who believed, in his own way, in ruling well, even if he often failed at it. Other historians, such as Björn Weiler, have explored Henry's kingship in its European setting, and found him a more thoughtful and more ambitious ruler than the old picture allowed. The argument goes on, and it's a gentle one, as these things go. But nobody, on either side, would deny that Henry loved his chambers, and his paintings, and his feasts, and his prayers.

Nicholas's whispers are slowing down now. His head is against his father's shoulder. He asks one more question, and it's the question Hugh has been waiting for. He asks why his father always sleeps across the door.

And so, very nearly at the end of our night's waking, here is the third of our three mysteries.

I asked you, at the very beginning: why would the room where the king slept, on the quietest night of the year, never truly be empty, not even once, from dusk until dawn?

There are three answers, and they're all true at once.

The first is safety. A king asleep is a king at his most helpless. There were guards at the gates and on the stairs, but the last guard of all, the closest, was the man who slept at the king's door. In the great households of the Middle Ages, it was usual for servants of the chamber, and sometimes knights or squires of the body, to sleep in the king's chamber or across its door, so that no one could come in without stepping over them. The household books of the fifteenth century, such as the Black Book of the household of King Edward the Fourth, written in the 1470s and edited by the historian A. R. Myers in 1959, set out exactly who among the king's servants was to be near him at night. I'll paraphrase rather than quote it, but the principle is plain: the king was never left without someone close by.

The second answer is service. A king who wakes in the night might be thirsty, or cold, or ill, or afraid, or simply want something. And so there had to be someone there to bring it. Somebody to mend the fire, and keep the light burning, and fetch the water, and bring the pot. Even the fears of the night, which the painted guards of Solomon's bed were meant to keep away, were easier to bear if you knew that a living person was lying just beyond the door, awake at a word.

And the third answer is the one we keep meeting all night. Warmth. A fire needs a person. In a room with no thermostat, the warmth lasts only as long as somebody is there to feed the flames. So, the room of a king could never really be empty, because the king's own comfort depended on someone being there, through every hour of the dark.

That's our third mystery solved. The king's chamber was never truly empty because it was never meant to be. Safety, service and warmth all needed a person. And that person, on this night, is Hugh.

Let's set that beside our own lives, for a moment. Most of us, when we go to bed, leave nobody on duty at all. The heating looks after itself. The smoke alarm on the ceiling keeps watch for fire. The lock on the door keeps watch for strangers. If a baby wakes, a monitor carries the sound to the parents' room. If someone is ill, a phone on the bedside table can bring help within minutes. All the jobs that Hugh does in person, lying across the king's door, we have handed over to machines, quietly, without ever really noticing. The richest king in Christendom needed a man on the floor. We have a little blinking light.

Hugh tells his son a little of this, in a whisper. Not in those words. In the words of a man who has slept across doors for twenty years. And Nicholas listens, and nods against his shoulder, and understands.

## Part Eleven — Around Two in the Morning: The Castle Falls Asleep

Here is a small, gentle question for this hour of the night. Now that the waking is over, and the castle is settling again, who will be the last to fall asleep?

Let's find out together, slowly.

Imagine that you're lying back down now, on the edge of the children's pallet in the king's wardrobe, against the warm wall. The blanket is heavy over you. The straw beneath you crackles softly as you settle, and then is still. The line of moonlight on the floor has moved a little, and grown fainter, as the moon climbs higher into the southern sky. Above you, the king's robes hang on their pegs in their linen covers, pale and still. The wardrobe smells of wool, and cedar, and faintly of lavender, and of the woodsmoke that gets into everything in a medieval house, even a king's.

And everything is very, very quiet.

Not silent. Never quite silent. If you listen, you can hear the old timbers of the roof above the wardrobe ticking softly as the frost tightens its grip on them. You can hear a mouse, somewhere far off, behind the panelling, going about its own small business. You can hear the faint, slow breath of the wind along the shutters, rising and falling, like someone sighing in their sleep. And under it all, so faint that you can hardly be sure it's there, the voices of the monks, down in the city, singing the night office, carried on the cold air.

Peter, of course, never woke at all. He has slept through the whole night so far, through the bells and the moonrise and the whispering, curled up tight against his mother's side, with one small hand holding on to a fold of her kirtle, as if he were afraid she might get up and go without him. He's six. When you're six, and warm, and fed with Christmas sweets, sleep is easy. So Peter is first, and he is very far away, dreaming, I should think, of white bears.

Margery is next. She stirred, a little while ago, when her brother crept out to his father, and she lay awake for a few minutes, listening to the whispers through the door, not quite able to hear the words. She thought about the queen's linen, and the hem she'd mended yesterday, and whether the damsel who nodded to her mother had noticed how neat it was. She thought about the green curtains she'd glimpsed through the door, and the white pillows, and the fur. And then her thoughts began to slow, and tangle, the way thoughts do at the edge of sleep, until the hem and the curtains and the fur were all one soft, warm thing that didn't make sense any more. Her eyes closed. Her breathing deepened.

Margery is asleep.

Her hand has come to rest on the edge of the blanket, close to her brother's, and one small finger is still curled around a loose thread of the wool, the way she curls her finger around the thread when she's helping her mother wind it. In the morning, she won't remember falling asleep at all. She'll only remember that she was warm.

Let's set this beside our own world, very gently. Many people today fall asleep to some kind of sound: a fan humming, or rain on a window, or a recording of waves on a shore, or a calm voice telling a long, slow story about a castle on a winter night. We do it because silence can feel too empty, and because a steady sound tells some old part of us that everything is all right. In the king's wardrobe tonight, the sounds are older. Breathing. The tick of a fire through a wall. A bell, far away. The murmur of a father and son in the next room. Those are the oldest sleep sounds there are.

Out in the outer chamber, Nicholas has fallen asleep sitting up, with his head on his father's shoulder and the blanket around them both. Hugh doesn't move, for a long time. He sits there, holding very still, so as not to wake him, feeling the weight of his son's head grow heavier and heavier. Then, very carefully, he lifts the boy, the way he lifted him when he was small, though he's much heavier now, all long legs and elbows, and carries him back into the wardrobe, and lays him down at the foot of the pallet, against the warm wall. Nicholas mumbles something about bears, and turns over, and is gone.

Nicholas is asleep.

Isabel stirred when Hugh came in. She opened her eyes, and saw him, and saw Nicholas, and smiled, in the dark, the small, tired smile of a mother who knows exactly where all her children are. Hugh bent down, and tucked the blanket over the boy's feet. And then he went back to his post, across the door.

I'd like to describe something to you now, while the castle goes quiet, because I think it belongs to this hour. It's the great hall, at two in the morning.

Imagine that you're standing once more just inside the great door, but now it's dark, and still. The central fire is a low, broad glow of red, pulsing very slowly, like something breathing. The sleepers lie all around it, in their cloaks and blankets, dark shapes on the rushes. The tall windows are pale grey now, with moonlight, and that light falls in long, slanting bars across the floor, and across the sleeping bodies, and up the slender marble columns. High above, the roof is lost in shadow. And somewhere up there, on the wall, painted in the dark, is the great round map of the world, with Jerusalem at its centre, and paradise in the east, and all the cities and rivers and seas of the earth, sleeping too, until the morning light comes back to find them.

It's one of those buildings that has seen a great many nights. Seven hundred and seventy-odd years of them, since our Christmas, and it's still standing. If you go there today, and stand in the same place, on a quiet afternoon in winter, you can still feel a little of the hush of this one.

There's a gentle question that people like to argue about in Winchester, and I'll share it with you now, because it's the comfortable kind of argument, the kind you can have by a fire. Was Winchester ever the capital of England? In the days of Alfred the Great, and of the kings who came after him, it was the chief city of the kingdom of Wessex, and Alfred himself was buried here. After the Norman Conquest, the royal treasury was kept here, and the great survey of 1086 that we call Domesday Book was kept here for a time, which is why it was sometimes called the Book of Winchester. So, for a while, it was something very like a capital. But medieval kings didn't really have capitals in our sense, because, as we've heard all night, they never stayed still. Historians who have studied the city, like Martin Biddle, who led the great excavations in Winchester in the 1960s and 70s, tend to talk about it as a royal city, a seat of the kings, rather than a capital. By Henry's day, Westminster had become the centre of government. But Winchester was where he was born. And tonight, it's where he sleeps.

Isabel goes next. She's lying on her side, facing her children, with one arm over Peter and her cheek on the edge of the pallet. She is thinking about the washing she'll have to do after the feast, the mountains of table linen, the stains of wine and grease. She's thinking that tomorrow is Saint Stephen's day, and that there will be another feast, and more linen. And then she isn't thinking about linen at all. She's thinking about her own mother, long ago, in a village outside the city, singing to her by a fire. And then about nothing. Her arm grows heavy over Peter. Her breathing slows, and matches his.

Isabel is asleep.

Now, all through the castle, the same thing is happening. In the queen's rooms, the damsel by the fire has nodded off in her chair, her chin on her chest, and the queen, behind her own curtains, is sleeping. In the hall, the last of the murmuring has stopped. In the stables, the restless horse has finally settled, and the groom has curled up in the straw beside it. In the kitchen, the boy minding the fires has wedged himself into the warm corner by the bread oven, and his eyes are closing. Only the watchmen are awake, up on the walls, and the monks in their church, singing softly in the dark, and one man, lying across a door.

Down in the city, the same quiet has settled over every street. The inns are dark, their guests asleep two and three to a bed. The bakers won't be up for a while yet. The river runs on under its fringe of ice, past the mills and the water meadows, where the frost lies white on the grass and a heron stands motionless at the edge of the water, waiting for the light. In the cathedral close, the monks' singing ends, and they file back up the night stair to their dormitory, and lie down again in their beds for a little more rest before the morning. And over all of it, the half moon hangs in the southern sky, laying its pale light on the roofs, and the towers, and the long, white, frosted slopes of the downs that roll away from Winchester on every side.

And the king? Behind his green curtains, in his deep featherbed, under the fur-lined coverlet, beside the painted guards of Solomon's bed, Henry the Third, King of England, is asleep. We can't know what he dreamed. We can't know whether he slept well. The records don't follow kings that far. So let's just leave him there, in the warm, in the dark, on the night of the feast of his Saviour's birth, in the castle where he himself was born, forty-five years ago.

Let me tell you one small, true, comfortable thing about feathers, while the castle sleeps. A feather bed is warm because it is mostly air. Each feather, and even more so each tiny fluffy cluster of down, holds pockets of still air among its fine branches, and still air is one of the best insulators there is. That's why birds fluff up their feathers on cold mornings. And it's why, all those centuries ago, the richest people in Europe slept on bags of feathers and under coverlets of fur and wool, trapping their own warmth around them all night long. It's exactly what a modern down duvet does. Hugh made the king's bed tonight with the same science that you'll use when you pull your own covers up.

Hugh is the last.

He's lying on his pallet across the inner door, on his back, with his hands folded on his chest, the way his father taught him to lie, so that he can be up in an instant if he's needed. He's listening, as he always does. He can hear the king's breathing, slow and deep, through the gap in the door. He can hear his family's breathing, behind him, in the wardrobe. He can hear the fire ticking, and the faint sigh of the wind at the shutters, and far off, the monks.

He counts them, the way he always does. Isabel. Nicholas. Margery. Peter. The king. All here. All warm. All safe.

Imagine you're lying there too, a guest in this castle on this one night of all the nights in history, listening with him. The blanket is warm. The wall behind you is warm. The air on your face is cool. Your breathing slows, and begins to match his. In, and out. In, and out.

Hugh's eyes close.

His hands, folded on his chest, loosen a little. His breathing deepens. Somewhere far down in the city, a dog barks once, and is quiet. In the outer chamber, the fire settles, with a soft sound, like a small sigh, as a log breaks and sinks into the embers, and a few sparks rise up the flue and are gone.

And Hugh is asleep.

Outside, the night goes on, in its own slow way. The moon climbs towards the south. The frost thickens on the roofs and on the grass of the castle ditch. The watchman on the wall pulls his hood further down and leans on his spear and looks out over a city of sleeping people, lit silver by the moon. And in the king's chamber, behind the green curtains, the king sleeps on, in his travelling bed, in the warm.

## Part Twelve — Around Half Past Seven in the Morning: Drawing Back the Curtains

The last small question of our night is the simplest of all. On the morning after Christmas, in a king's castle, what is the very first thing that anybody does?

Let's wait and see.

The long dark is ending. It's around half past seven in the morning, and the sun won't rise over the hills to the east until a little after eight. But already, something has changed. The cracks around the shutters, which were black all night, and then silver with moonlight, have turned a soft, pale grey. The moon itself is still up, high in the south-western sky, white and fading, like a thumbprint on glass. Down in the city, the bells of the cathedral are ringing. The monks are going in to sing the first office of the morning. It is the twenty-sixth of December, the feast of Saint Stephen.

Outside, the whole world is white with frost. The roofs of the castle are white, and the grass in the ditch, and the tops of the walls where the watchmen walked all night. On the hills to the east, the sky is turning from grey to the palest blue, and then, along the very edge of the world, to a thin line of rose and gold. The watchmen of the night are being relieved now by the men of the day, stamping and grumbling and blowing into their hands as they climb the tower stairs. In the kitchens, the fires are being built up again, and the first loaves of the day are going into the ovens, and the smell of baking bread is drifting across the courtyard.

In the king's outer chamber, the fire has burned down to a low glow of embers. In the wardrobe, the children are still asleep, in a tangle of blankets and arms and legs. Peter is sprawled across his mother's feet. Margery has her face pressed into Nicholas's back. Isabel has one hand out of the blanket, resting on the floor, as if she'd reached for something in the night and forgotten what.

And Hugh opens his eyes.

He doesn't need the bells to wake him. He has woken at this grey hour almost every morning of his working life. He lies still for a moment, listening, as he did last night. The king's breathing, through the door, slow and even. His family's breathing, behind him. Everyone here. Then he gets up, quietly, folds his blanket, and goes, barefoot, to the outer hearth.

That's the answer to our last small question. The very first thing anybody does, in the king's chamber, on a winter morning, is mend the fire.

Imagine you're kneeling beside him on the hearth now, in the grey half-light. The stones are still warm under your knees. Hugh rakes the embers together, gently, into a heap, and lays a handful of dry kindling on top, and leans down, and blows. A long, slow, steady breath. The embers brighten. The kindling smokes, and catches, and crackles. He lays on small sticks, and then a log, and then another, and in a minute or two the fire is burning again, low and orange and steady, and its light is moving on the walls.

Then he goes into the inner chamber, very softly, and does the same at the small fireplace there, by the bed, so quietly that the king doesn't stir.

And then he does something that I find rather lovely. He takes the king's clean linen shirt for the day, which Isabel's companions washed and pressed and which he carried up the stair at dusk last night, and he holds it up in front of the fire, turning it slowly, so that it warms through. John Russell, in his Boke of Nurture, tells the chamberlain to do exactly this, and I'll paraphrase him: in the morning, have the fire ready, and warm your lord's shirt and his clothes before he puts them on, and have a cushion and a footsheet ready by the fire for him to stand on, so that his bare feet don't touch the cold floor. Russell wrote two hundred years after our night, but the cold of a winter morning had not changed, and I imagine the kindness hadn't either.

When the shirt is warm, Hugh folds it over his arm, and sets it near the fire, and waits.

While he waits, he looks around the two rooms, the way he does every morning, checking. The candle on the table in the inner chamber has burned right down to a stub in its holder, but it lasted the night, and it never once came near the curtains. The fire is well. The shutters are sound. The basin is ready, with water warming beside the hearth. The king's robe is on its perch, and his shoes are on their cushion, and the towels Margery carried up last night are folded on the bench. Everything is where it should be. It's a small, quiet satisfaction, the kind that people who look after others know well: the night has passed, and nothing went wrong.

Let's set this beside your own home, one last time. Perhaps your heating comes on by itself before you wake, on a timer, so that the house is warm when you get up. Perhaps you have a heated towel rail in the bathroom, or slippers by the bed, or a dressing gown hanging on the door. You probably don't think of any of them as remarkable. But each one does, without anyone's help, what Hugh is doing now with his hands, his breath, and his patience. In a medieval castle, warmth in the morning was a gift, given by a person, every single day.

Out in the wardrobe, the children are waking up. Peter first, who sits bolt upright and asks, much too loudly, whether it's morning, and is hushed by three people at once. Then Margery, who rubs her eyes and starts folding the blanket before she's properly awake, because that's what her mother does. Then Nicholas, who groans, and pulls the blanket back over his head, and is pulled out from under it by his sister. And then Isabel, who sits up, and smooths her headcloth, and looks at her husband, standing in the doorway in the firelight with the king's warm shirt over his arm, and smiles. There. Another night. They've done it again.

Isabel gathers the children and the blankets, and slips out, down the stair, back to the queen's side of the castle, where there will be linen waiting, mountains of it, after the feast. On her way, she'll pass the laundry, already steaming, and the well, where the water has a skin of ice that has to be broken with a bucket, and the great hall, where the sleepers are stirring on the rushes, and sitting up, and rubbing their eyes, and pulling on their shoes, while servants come in with baskets of bread and jugs of ale, and somebody throws fresh wood onto the embers of the great fire. Nicholas goes down to the kitchens for wood, and perhaps, if he's lucky, for whatever is left of that platter. And Hugh waits by the inner door.

There's a gentle disagreement among historians that I'll leave you with, since we're talking about mornings and shirts. People often ask whether medieval people slept naked. You'll read in some books that almost everyone did, and that a nightgown was a much later invention. And it's true that some medieval pictures show people in bed with nothing on, under the covers, and that some writers speak of undressing completely before sleep. But other historians of dress point out that pictures can follow artistic conventions, and that plenty of people, especially in cold weather, seem to have kept their linen shirt or shift on, and a cap or kerchief on their heads, as Russell's chamberlain provides. The honest answer seems to be that it depended on the person, the place, and the weather. On a night like this one, I suspect a good many people in Winchester Castle kept their shirts on.

The light in the room is growing. Through the shutters, the grey is turning to a faint, pale gold. And down in the courtyard, there are footsteps, and voices, and the creak of a door. The castle is waking up. Somewhere in the chapel, a priest is preparing for the morning mass of Saint Stephen. The king will hear it, as he hears mass every morning.

From behind the green curtains, there's a stir. A man waking, turning over, and lying still for a moment, the way people do when they first come up out of sleep. And then, a word, or a sound, that Hugh has been waiting for.

He goes to the bed, and with the same careful hand that drew them closed last night, he draws back the curtains.

I'll leave the king there, at the edge of his morning, in the warm and the firelight. What he said, if he said anything, nobody wrote down, and I'm not going to invent it. In a little while, he'll be dressed in his warmed shirt, and his robe, and he'll go out of this room, and down the stair, to the chapel, and to the business of the day, and to the second day of Christmas. The household will go with him. And the chamber will be quiet.

So let's stay, just for a moment, with Hugh, and the bed.

The room is empty now, except for him. The fires are burning in both rooms. The shutters have been opened, and the morning light is coming in through the little panes of greenish-white glass, through the heavy iron bars the king ordered last summer, falling in soft, pale squares across the rushes, and across the painted walls, the green and the gold, and across the bed.

Hugh stands in front of it, the way he stood last night. The pillows still hold the shape of a head. The coverlet is thrown back. The sheets are rumpled and warm. And he begins, again, as he has a thousand times, in a dozen houses. He lifts the pillows, and shakes them, and lays them by the fire to air. He folds back the coverlet and the furs. He pulls back the sheets and shakes them out and lays them over the bench. He pummels the featherbed, and turns it, and smooths it flat. And then, when it's all fresh again, he makes it up, layer by layer, linen and wool and fur and down, just as he did at dusk, without hurrying, and without a single wasted movement.

When it's done, he stands back, and looks at it. The bed that came on a horse. The bed that will, one of these days soon, be taken apart again, and packed into its chests, and strapped onto the back of a packhorse, and carried away down the frozen road to the next house, and the next, wherever the king goes. The bed that, for one long Christmas night in 1252, kept the King of England warm.

Then Hugh takes the edge of the green curtain, and draws it gently along its rail, all the way round, until the bed is closed again, a small, quiet, green-curtained room within the room, waiting for the night.

He smooths the edge of the curtain with his hand, once, where it meets the post. He looks at it. Moves it an inch. Looks again. And then he nods, just as he did last night, the small private nod of a man who knows that something has been done properly.

And in the morning light, beside it, the fire burns on.

Thank you for spending this long Christmas night with me, in Winchester Castle, with Hugh and Isabel, and Nicholas and Margery and little Peter, and with a king and queen who really lived, and really slept, somewhere inside these walls. If this story helped you rest, or helped you drift off somewhere along the way, I'm truly glad. And if you'd like to come back for more nights like this one, you might consider liking this video and subscribing to the channel, gently, whenever you're ready. There's no hurry at all.

Next time, we'll go a little way down the hill, to the great church of Saint Swithun, and spend a whole winter night with the monks of a medieval cathedral priory: rising in the dark at the sound of a bell, walking the night stair to a freezing choir by the light of a single lantern, and singing through the small hours while the world sleeps. We'll find out how they kept warm, how they kept awake, and how they kept time. I hope you'll join me there.

Until then, sleep well. Keep warm. And may someone, somewhere, always draw your curtains closed at the end of the day.

## How the sources were checked

- **The family is fictional.** Hugh (servant of the king's chamber), Isabel (washerwoman in the queen's household), Nicholas (13), Margery (10) and Peter (6) are invented, and so is the older chamber servant and the watchman. The script says so in Part One. No invented person is presented as appearing in any record.
- **Real people only do what records support.** Henry III and Eleanor of Provence are never given dialogue. The script says plainly that no record describes Henry's bedtime on this night (the ritual in Part Seven is taken from later household books and labelled that way), that no record shows which bed the king and queen slept in, and that Eleanor's presence at this Christmas is assumed rather than recorded: Matthew Paris places the king at Winchester, and Margaret Howell's biography says the couple had made up their quarrel by the end of 1252. The colour of the bed curtains (green) is chosen and labelled as a choice in Part Four. The one recorded speech of Henry's (the 1258 thunderstorm) is paraphrased from Matthew Paris and flagged as possibly embroidered.
- **The setting is real.** Winchester Castle, Hampshire; the night of Christmas Day, Wednesday 25 December 1252 (Julian calendar), into St Stephen's Day. The great hall (1222–35) survives; the rest of the castle was slighted in 1651.
- **Limits of verification in this session.** Page fetches were blocked, and the web-search budget for the session ran out after 13 searches (a shared per-turn cap). Items marked **[S]** below were confirmed in search results this session. Items marked **[K]** are standard, well-known facts from established scholarship that could not be re-checked by search once the budget ran out. They should be checked against the named edition before publication. Wherever a [K] item involves wording, the narration paraphrases it and says so.
- **Computed, not quoted.** Sunset (about 16:11 UT, so a little after 4 pm local solar time), sunrise on 26 December (about 08:10 UT, a little after 8 am local), moonrise (about 00:38 UT on 26 December) and the moon's phase (waning, about 48% lit, last quarter; previous full moon about 18 December, next new moon about 2 January) for Winchester (51.063° N, 1.321° W) were calculated with the PyEphem library. The weekday (Wednesday) was computed from the Julian Day number. The narration says that the times and the moon were worked out with a modern model.
- **Dropped or softened for lack of verification.** These were researched but left out: the often-quoted letter about a foul-smelling royal privy (only a blog source was found; the script speaks only generally about Henry's interest in privies); a 1240 Westminster "chimney with the twelve months painted on it" (not found); any claim about a specific Christmas feeding of a stated number of paupers; Eleanor of Castile and Edward I material; and exact sums for the white bear's keep (sources give 4d and 6d, and the script says so).
