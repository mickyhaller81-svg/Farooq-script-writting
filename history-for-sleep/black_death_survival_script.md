# How You Could Have Survived the Black Death | History for Sleep

## Part One — Around Half Past Seven in the Evening: The City Below

The heat of the day is going now, slowly, the way heat goes in Tuscany in July.

On the southern slope of the hill below Fiesole, among olive trees silvered by the evening light, there is a farmhouse with a square stone tower, a walled garden, a loggia of three arches, and a gate that has been shut for many weeks. The cicadas are still singing in the pines, a dry and endless shimmer of sound. Swallows are cutting through the air above the roof. And from the loggia, if you stood there now, you could look straight down the long green slope, past the vines and the cypresses, to the valley of the Arno, where the city of Florence lies in a golden haze, its towers and its red roofs and the unfinished bulk of its cathedral glowing in the last of the sun.

It is the evening of Monday, the fourteenth of July, in the year 1348.

When most of us think of the plague, we picture a figure in a long black coat and a wide hat, with a white mask shaped like the beak of a great bird, walking through a dark street. We've seen it on posters, in films, in shop windows every October.

But that figure does not belong to tonight. Not the plague doctor of the Halloween costumes. That beaked costume comes from the seventeenth century, nearly three hundred years after this summer. It is usually credited to a French royal physician named Charles de Lorme, who is believed to have introduced it in 1619. In 1348, no one in Florence has ever seen such a thing. The people we are going to meet tonight faced the great mortality with very different tools: with distance, with patience, with clean water, with smoke and herbs, with rules and habits, and with a great deal of luck.

I should be honest with you before we go in. If you were set down in Florence this spring, with all your modern knowledge and none of their habits, without their choices and without their luck, you would very possibly not have survived until the autumn. A great many people did not.

But some did. Many did. And tonight, slowly, we are going to learn how.

While we do, I'd like you to carry three small mysteries with you, the way you might carry three cool pebbles in a pocket on a hot day.

The first. On one of the hottest nights of the year, why would a careful mother keep a fire burning in her house all night long, and feed it, not with logs, but with herbs?

The second. Why would a town that was losing so many of its people pass a law forbidding its church bells to ring for the dead?

And the third. Why does the English word for keeping travellers apart, to keep a sickness out, come from the Italian word for the number forty?

I'll answer each of them in its own time. The first before the evening is out. The second in the deep of the night. And the third in the small hours, when the house is nearly asleep. There's no hurry. We have the whole night.

Now, before we go through the gate, I want to be clear with you about something, because it matters on this channel. The family you are about to meet is invented. Their names, their ages, their habits and their kindnesses, I have made up. But almost everything around them is real. The hills are real. The city below is real. And the letters, the chronicles, the laws and the doctors' advice that I'll share with you tonight are real, written by people who lived through this year, and I'll tell you their names and their dates as we go, so that you can look them up yourself, some quiet afternoon. Where I can't be sure of the exact words, I'll tell you that I'm paraphrasing.

So. Let's meet them.

The man standing at the edge of the loggia, looking down at the city with his hands behind his back, is Niccolò. He is forty-two years old, a merchant of the wool guild of Florence, the great Arte della Lana, which in this century is one of the richest trade guilds in Europe. He buys English and Spanish wool, has it washed and combed and spun and woven and dyed by dozens of workers in the city, and sells the finished cloth as far away as Naples and Avignon. Until April, he went every morning to his workshop near the river. He has not been there for three months.

Sitting on the stone bench behind him, a basket of mending at her feet, is his wife, Lapa. She's thirty-five. She runs this household the way Niccolò runs his ledgers, carefully, and in her head. It was Lapa, as much as Niccolò, who decided in the first week of April that they would leave the city, and leave at once.

Inside, in the cool of the great room on the ground floor, is Niccolò's mother, Monna Tessa. She is sixty-four, a widow, small and upright, with a black veil and a sharp tongue and a memory that goes back to the days of Dante. She was a young woman when the poet was sent into exile from Florence, in 1302, and she likes to say that she remembers the shouting in the street, though nobody quite believes her.

Then the children. Bartolomeo, whom everyone calls Meo, is fourteen, and already keeps a little account book of his own. Caterina is eleven, dark-eyed and serious. Piero is seven and has spent the whole summer trying to catch lizards on the garden wall. And Nanni, which is what Florentines call a little boy named Giovanni, is three, and is at this moment asleep on a mattress in the shade of the tower, with one arm flung over his face.

There is also Nuta, the household servant, who is nineteen and came with the family from the city. And in the farmhouse buildings across the yard, on the other side of a low wall, live the people who actually farm this land, the sharecroppers Cecco and his wife Bice, with their two grown sons. They are not servants. In the old Tuscan arrangement called mezzadria, a farming family works the land and keeps a share of what it grows, often half, and the landowner keeps the rest. Cecco has worked this hillside for twenty years. He knows every olive tree on it by its shape.

That's everyone. Seven in the villa, a family of four across the yard, and a thin brown dog called Moro who belongs to nobody and to everybody.

Here is a small question to begin with, one that we'll answer before this part is done. Where, exactly, did they flee to, and why here?

Imagine that you are standing beside Niccolò now at the edge of the loggia. The stone is still warm under your hand from the day's sun. The air smells of dry grass, of hot resin from the pines, of the herbs in the garden, rosemary and sage and lavender, and faintly, from the kitchen behind you, of woodsmoke and bread. Below you the slope falls away in terraces, olive trees and then vines and then fields of stubble where the wheat was cut a few weeks ago. A path winds down between walls toward the road to the city. You can see the walls of Florence from here, and the gates, and the long thin bridge of the Ponte Vecchio across the river, though at this distance it looks no bigger than a twig.

Beside the cathedral, a little apart from it, you can make out a tall, slender tower, still unfinished, its upper part wrapped in wooden scaffolding. That's the new bell tower, the campanile, which was begun in 1334 to a design by the painter Giotto, who was made master of the cathedral works that year. Giotto died in 1337, with only the lowest part built, and others have carried the work on since. It will not be finished for another decade or so. Tonight, its scaffolding is empty. Nobody is working on it this summer.

And you notice something. The road is empty.

On an ordinary July evening, there would be carts on it, mules, peasants walking home from the city markets, a rider or two. Tonight there is nothing. Not a single figure moves on the whole long road between the hills and the city gate.

Niccolò has looked at that road every evening since April. He doesn't say anything about it. Neither do you.

That's the answer to our small question, at least the first half. They fled here, to the hill below Fiesole, because it was close enough to reach in a morning's walk, and far enough to be away from the crowded streets, and because they owned it. Many well-off Florentine families had a country house and a farm like this one in the hills around the city, the contado, where their food and wine and oil came from. When a great sickness came to the city, the countryside was the obvious place to go. And here, a family could close its gate.

The second half of the answer is in a book.

In the years just after this summer, a Florentine writer named Giovanni Boccaccio, who was about thirty-five years old in 1348, wrote a great collection of a hundred stories called the Decameron, which he finished around 1353. And he began it with one of the most famous descriptions of the plague ever written, describing what he had seen in his own city. In one English translation, it opens like this: "In the year then of our Lord 1348, there happened at Florence, the finest city in all Italy, a most terrible plague." And the frame of his book, the story that holds all the other stories, is about exactly what our family has done. Seven young women and three young men meet in a church in the city, decide that they will not stay to watch their world fall apart, and leave together for a villa in the hills, where they spend their days walking in gardens, eating simply, singing, and telling stories.

Boccaccio placed their villa somewhere in these hills, close to the city. For centuries, people have tried to say exactly which villa he meant, and we'll come back to that friendly puzzle later in the night. But when I picture Niccolò's house tonight, I picture it as a neighbour of theirs, a stone's throw, perhaps, from the place where Boccaccio's storytellers sat in the shade.

Now, I said that I'd acknowledge the scale of what is happening down there, in the city, and I will, gently, once, and then we'll let it rest. The plague had come into Italy at the end of 1347, by ship, and it reached Florence in the spring of 1348. Boccaccio wrote that between March and July of this year, more than a hundred thousand people died within the walls of Florence. Modern historians think that figure is too high, simply because the city may not have held many more people than that to begin with. But no one doubts that a very large part of the city died. Perhaps half. Perhaps more.

And here is our first place where historians disagree, and it's about that very question. How many people, across the whole of Europe, did the Black Death kill? For a long time, the most commonly repeated answer was about a quarter to a third of the population. Then, in 2004, the Norwegian historian Ole Benedictow published a large study, The Black Death 1346 to 1353: The Complete History, drawing on records from all over the continent, and argued that the true figure was far higher, around sixty per cent. In a later edition, he raised it further. Other scholars have come down in between. In 2019, the economists Remi Jedwab, Noel Johnson and Mark Koyama, working city by city, estimated an average of a little under forty per cent for Western Europe. And some recent studies of ancient pollen, which show where farmland went out of use and where it didn't, suggest that some regions suffered very much worse than others. The honest answer is that the toll varied hugely from place to place, and that the overall figure is still argued over. For Tuscany, though, nearly every estimate is high.

That is enough of numbers for now. Let's set this beside our own world, the way we will all night. Many of us, in 2020, lived through something that in a small, faint way echoes what this family is doing. We stayed at home. We kept away from crowds. Some of us left the cities, if we could. We washed our hands and aired our rooms and waited for news. It is a strange feeling, isn't it, to look at Niccolò standing at his loggia, looking down at an empty road, and recognise him.

But the differences are enormous, too. We had a germ theory of disease, which no one would put forward in a clear form for another five hundred years. We had telephones and screens, so that even apart, we could see the faces of the people we loved. Niccolò has no way of knowing who is alive in Florence tonight, except by letters, which come rarely, and are left at the gate.

Lapa has put down her mending. She stands, and comes to her husband's side, and the two of them look down at the city together for a while, without speaking.

Then she touches his arm and says that the light is going, and that she wants the gate seen to before dark. Niccolò nods. He looks at the road one last time.

Far below, in the city, a few lights are beginning to show. Up here, in the garden, the first bat of the evening flickers past the tower. The cicadas are slowing. The swallows have gone. And from the kitchen behind them comes the faint, sweet, resinous smell of something being laid on the hearth, which is not wood, and which we will come back to before the evening is done.

## Part Two — Around Eight O'Clock: The Gate Is Shut

Here is the small question for this part of the evening. If you have shut your gate against the world, and you mean to keep it shut all summer, how does the bread get in?

Because that is the puzzle at the heart of this household. Behind the gate, there are seven people who must eat every day. They have a well, a garden, a kitchen, a cellar of wine and oil, and a farm all around them. But a family cannot live on a hillside entirely alone for long. Salt, for example, cannot be grown. Neither can candles, or medicine, or news.

Let's go and see how they manage it.

Imagine that you are walking down the garden path behind Niccolò now, in the warm blue dusk. The gravel crunches under your feet. On either side, there are beds of herbs, edged with low clipped box: sage, rosemary, rue, mint, marjoram, and a row of tall white lilies past their best. The lilies are not only there for their beauty. The lily is the emblem of Florence itself, the giglio, a red lily on a white ground, which flies on the city's banners and is stamped on the city's famous gold coin, the florin, first struck in 1252 and trusted by merchants all over Europe. Lapa planted these when she was first married. There's a fig tree against the wall, heavy with small green fruit that won't ripen for a few weeks yet. A lizard, very late, flicks off the top of the wall as you pass. Ahead of you is the gate, a pair of heavy wooden doors set in an arch in the wall, with an iron bar across them on the inside.

Beside the gate, built into the wall, there is something you might not notice at first. A low stone ledge, a sort of shelf, open to the outside, with a little wooden hatch above it.

Niccolò goes to the hatch, slides it open, and looks out. On the ledge outside, someone has left a covered basket, a stoppered jug, and a folded paper weighted with a stone.

That's how the bread gets in. That's the answer to our small question. Twice a week, Cecco's eldest son walks down to a village on the road, buys what is needed, and brings it back, and leaves it on the ledge outside the gate. He doesn't come in. Nobody from the villa goes out. The basket stays on the ledge in the open air for a while before anyone touches it, because Lapa insists on it, and then Nuta brings it in. The coins to pay for the next lot go out the same way, left on the ledge, and Lapa washes them first in a bowl of vinegar, which is a habit she has picked up from somewhere and will not give up.

I should be careful here, and honest. That little ledge and hatch belong to our invented house, and Lapa's vinegar is my own touch, a plausible one, since vinegar was used for cleansing and was much recommended by the physicians of the time in food and in washing. But the principle behind them is something that we can see in the real records of this year, written by real governments, in towns very close to here.

Twenty miles or so to the north-west of Florence, as the crow flies, there is a smaller city called Pistoia. And in May of 1348, as the plague closed in around them, the governing council of Pistoia issued a set of health ordinances, rules for the city to live by in the time of the mortality. They survive in the city's records, and they were published by the historian Alberto Chiappelli in 1887, and they've been translated into English several times since, including by Rosemary Horrox, in her collection of sources called The Black Death, published in 1994.

The very first thing the ordinances try to do is close the roads. Citizens of Pistoia were forbidden to travel to the cities of Pisa or Lucca, where the plague already was, and no one from those places was to come into Pistoia. There were rules about bringing in old cloth, and about funerals, and about how the butchers were to work, because the smells of their trade were thought to corrupt the air. We'll come back to these ordinances later in the night, because one of them holds the answer to our second mystery.

But the idea behind the first rules is the same idea behind Niccolò's gate. If the sickness comes from somewhere else, then keep the somewhere else away.

Florence, too, tried to protect itself. According to modern summaries of the city's records, in the spring of 1348 Florence cleaned its streets, tried to keep sick travellers out, and kept animals out of the city. None of it was enough. Florence was too big, too busy, too connected to the whole Mediterranean by its trade. But a single house on a single hillside, with a wall and a gate and a family willing to stay behind it, was a very different thing from a city.

Niccolò lifts the basket in through the hatch. Inside, under a cloth, there are two round loaves, a little bag of salt, a piece of cheese wrapped in leaves, and a bundle of something green and pungent that he holds up to his nose: fresh rue, and a handful of juniper twigs, which Lapa asked for. The jug holds vinegar. He leaves them on the inner ledge for Nuta, as Lapa has told him to, and picks up the folded paper, and turns it over in his hands, and doesn't open it yet. It's a letter. He knows the hand. It's from his factor in the city, the man who has been looking after his business since April. He puts it inside his tunic.

The business that letter is about is not a small one. A decade before this summer, around 1338, the Florentine chronicler Giovanni Villani, a merchant himself, wrote a famous survey of his city's wealth, and in it he reckoned, and I'll give you his figures as round numbers, because the manuscripts and editions differ slightly, that Florence had something like two hundred workshops of the wool guild, making seventy or eighty thousand pieces of cloth a year, and that more than thirty thousand people lived from that trade. Villani himself, I should tell you gently, did not live to see this autumn. He died in the plague this very year, and his brother Matteo took up the chronicle where he left it. But his numbers give us a sense of what Niccolò walked away from in April: a share in one of the greatest industries in Europe, a workshop full of people, bales of wool from England and Spain stacked in his warehouse, all of it left behind in the hands of a factor and a ledger.

He doesn't regret it. Not tonight. Bales of wool and bolts of cloth come from everywhere, pass through dozens of hands, and travel in the holds of ships and in the packs of mules from ports where the sickness already was. Nobody in 1348 knows exactly why it seemed safer to be away from them. But a great many merchants had the same instinct as Niccolò, and some of them, as we'll see much later tonight, were closer to the truth than they knew.

Across the low wall, in the farmhouse, you can hear Cecco's family settling down for the evening. A door bangs. One of the sons laughs. Bice calls something to her husband about the hens. They keep to their side, and the villa keeps to its side, and they talk across the wall, cheerfully, a dozen times a day, at a distance of a few yards. When Cecco needs to tell Niccolò something about the vines, he stands in his own yard and shouts. It's a strange way to live, and they've grown used to it. Bice says it's like living next to the nuns.

Then he slides the bolt on the hatch, and checks the bar on the gate, and the two of you walk back up the path in the darkening garden.

Now let's go back to Boccaccio, because he describes, better than anyone, the choices that people in Florence made this spring. He says, and I'll paraphrase most of this, that people behaved in several different ways. Some decided that the best defence was to live moderately and keep apart. They gathered in small groups, shut themselves away in houses where no one was sick, ate delicate food and drank fine wine in moderation, avoided all excess, refused to hear any news from outside, and passed the time with music and other pleasures. Others did the very opposite, and decided that the cure was to drink and make merry and go from tavern to tavern and laugh at it all. Others took a middle way. And here I can give you his words, in an older English translation that's been widely reproduced. They "walked abroad, carrying in the hands flowers or fragrant herbs or divers sorts of spices, which they frequently raised to their noses." The air, they believed, was tainted, and a sweet smell would guard against it. And finally, Boccaccio says, there were those who thought the safest thing of all was to leave. They abandoned the city, their houses, their goods, and went out into the countryside.

Our family has done two of those things at once. They have fled, and they are living moderately, behind a gate. And Lapa, in her garden, grows the flowers and herbs that the third group carried in their hands.

Let's set this beside your own world. If you've ever had groceries delivered and left on the doorstep, and waited until the driver had gone before opening the door, then you have done, almost exactly, what Niccolò has just done at his gate. In 2020, millions of people around the world did it every week. We wiped down our shopping. Some of us left parcels in the hallway for a day before opening them. The reasons we gave ourselves were more precise than Lapa's. But the instinct, to let the outside world touch your threshold and no further, is the same.

And here we reach the next point where scholars have disagreed, and it's about Boccaccio himself.

How far should we trust his description of the plague? For a long time it was read simply as an eyewitness report, the account of a man who had walked through the stricken city and written down what he saw. He was certainly in a position to know. His father, a merchant and banker, is thought to have died in the plague of these years, and Boccaccio lived in Florence through much of the outbreak. But scholars who have studied the Decameron closely have pointed out that its description of the plague is also a carefully made piece of literature, shaped by older models. Some have noticed that parts of it echo earlier Latin descriptions of great plagues, including the account of a sixth-century plague in Italy in the history of the Lombards written by Paul the Deacon in the eighth century. So is the introduction a report, or a work of art? Most scholars now would say that it is both. Boccaccio saw terrible things, and he also wrote about them like the great writer he was, with older words echoing in his ears. And when his account agrees with the plain records of the time, as it often does, we can trust it a little more.

Back in the house, Nuta has lit a lamp. It's a small clay oil lamp with a floating wick, and it sits on the table in the great room, making a soft yellow pool of light. Olive oil, here, is the common fuel for light, pressed from the trees outside the window. Monna Tessa is sitting beside it, her rosary of wooden beads in her lap, watching the door. She doesn't approve of the gate. She doesn't approve of very much. But she was the first to agree, in April, when Lapa said they must go.

She asks Niccolò if there's news. Niccolò says there's a letter. She asks what it says. He says he hasn't read it yet. She looks at him for a long moment and says nothing more, which for Monna Tessa is a great kindness.

Caterina is at the table too, with a slate and a piece of chalk, practising her letters, because Lapa believes a merchant's daughter should be able to read a contract. Piero is under the table with the dog. Meo is sitting on the stairs that lead up to the tower rooms, with his own little account book open on his knees, writing something in it by the light from the doorway.

I'll tell you one more true thing about this house before this part of the night is over. The farmhouses of the Tuscan countryside in this century were usually built of stone, the local grey sandstone of these hills, or of rough stone and brick, with roofs of terracotta tiles, the same curved, overlapping red tiles that you can still see on farmhouses here today. Many had a square tower, called a colombaia, where pigeons were kept for their meat and their droppings, which made good fertiliser. The villas of the wealthy grew out of these farms, slowly, over the next century, into the grand country houses that tourists visit now. In 1348, our house is still mostly a farm, with a few good rooms, a walled garden, and a view.

Outside, the dusk has deepened into a soft violet dark. The first stars are coming out above Fiesole. Down in the valley, the city has almost disappeared, just a few pinpricks of light inside the walls. The cicadas have stopped. The crickets have begun.

And Lapa, in the kitchen, is untying the bundle of juniper twigs from the basket, and laying them, carefully, one by one, beside the hearth.

## Part Three — Around Half Past Eight: A Moderate Supper

The small question for this hour is a kitchen question. In a summer like this one, when everyone in Tuscany is afraid, what does a careful family eat for supper? And why does it matter, to them, that they eat so little?

Let's go into the kitchen and find out.

Imagine that you are stepping down two worn stone steps from the great room into the kitchen. It is the warmest room in the house, even now, after sunset, and it smells wonderful. It smells of bread and onions and olive oil and something green simmering in a pot. The floor is of beaten earth and old brick. The walls are rough plaster, smoke-darkened near the ceiling. There's a long wooden table, scrubbed pale, with a bench on either side. And along one wall, raised up a little off the floor, there is a broad stone hearth, with an iron chain hanging down from a beam above it, and on the chain, a round-bellied pot of copper.

Nuta is stirring the pot. Lapa is at the table, cutting bread.

Tonight's supper is simple. There is a minestra, a thick soup of beans and greens from the garden, with onion and a little garlic and a good pour of this year's oil. There is the bread that came through the hatch, cut into thick slices. There is a little of the cheese, and some new figs from a tree on the south wall that ripens early, and a bowl of small yellow plums. And there is wine, the red wine of the hillside, pressed last autumn from Cecco's vines, mixed with a good deal of water from the well, the way most Italians in this century drank it every day.

It's worth pausing over that pot for a moment, because it holds a small surprise. When I say beans, I don't mean the beans you might picture, the haricot or kidney beans that Tuscan cooking is famous for today. Those came from the Americas, and no one in Europe will see one for another century and a half. The beans in Nuta's pot are broad beans, fava beans, dried last summer, and perhaps a handful of chickpeas, or lentils. There are no tomatoes in this kitchen either, and no potatoes, and no maize for polenta, and no peppers. All of those are still growing, unknown, on the far side of an ocean that, as far as anyone in Florence knows, has no far side at all. The Tuscan kitchen of 1348 is built on bread, oil, wine, beans, greens, onions, garlic, cheese, fruit and herbs, and on whatever meat or fish the season and the purse allow. It is, if you like, the ancestor of the food we know, before half of its ingredients had arrived.

The oil itself is precious and everywhere. It goes into the soup, onto the bread, into the lamps, onto cuts and sore hands. Cecco's family pressed it last November from the olives on these terraces, crushing the fruit under a great stone wheel turned by a mule, and the jars of it stand in a cool storeroom under the tower, sealed with wax. In a good year, the hillside gives more than the household can use, and the surplus goes down to the city to be sold. This year, Niccolò thinks, it will stay where it is.

No meat tonight. No rich sauces. No great feast. Nothing heavy, nothing fried, nothing to excess.

That's the first half of the answer to our small question. A careful family in the summer of 1348 eats moderately, and simply, and drinks its wine well watered. And they do it on purpose.

To understand why, you need to know what the learned doctors of this century believed about the body. They followed a tradition of medicine that went back more than a thousand years, to the Greek physician Hippocrates and above all to Galen, who practised in Rome in the second century. In that tradition, the body was governed by four humours, four fluids: blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile. Health was a matter of keeping them in balance. And the balance could be upset by many things, by what you ate and drank, by how much you slept, by exercise, by the passions of the mind, and, above all, by the air you breathed. A body that was overfull, overheated, overfed, was thought to be far more open to corruption. A body that was moderate and clean and calm was thought to resist it.

So when Boccaccio, in his introduction, describes the people who shut themselves away and lived temperately, eating delicate food and drinking the best wine in moderation, and avoiding every kind of excess, he's describing people who were following the best medical advice of their age. The doctors called it a regimen, a rule of life, and in the plague years they wrote a great many of them.

The most famous of all was written a few months after our night, and I want to tell you about it now, though I'll be careful about the date. In October of 1348, the king of France, Philip the Sixth, asked the medical faculty of the University of Paris, the most prestigious body of physicians in northern Europe, to explain the causes of the pestilence and to say what could be done about it. Forty-nine masters of medicine put their names to the answer. It is usually called the Paris consilium, which simply means a piece of formal advice. Its first part sets out the causes, and its second part, in several chapters, sets out remedies and a regimen for living. And the passages on causes, which have been translated into English many times, are some of the strangest and most fascinating pages of the whole century.

The masters wrote that the distant, universal cause of the pestilence lay in the heavens. They pointed to a conjunction of three planets, Saturn, Jupiter and Mars, in the sign of Aquarius, which they said had taken place on the twentieth of March in 1345, at one hour after noon. Such a meeting of the planets, they believed, had corrupted the air. And the near, immediate cause was that corrupted air itself, breathed in, reaching the heart and the lungs. They even admitted, with a sort of scholarly honesty, that some causes of the sickness might always remain hidden, even from the most learned men.

Now, that conjunction of the planets really did happen, as it happens. Saturn, Jupiter and Mars did draw close together in the sky in the spring of 1345. Modern astronomical software can show it. But what we'd say now is that the planets had nothing whatsoever to do with what followed. The doctors of Paris were reasoning carefully, from the best knowledge they had. It simply wasn't the right knowledge.

That's the second half of our small question's answer. They eat moderately because their doctors told them that a moderate body, in a time of corrupted air, would be the safest body to have.

And here's the gentle surprise. Some of that advice was useless, and some of it was not. Avoiding excess, keeping clean, keeping away from crowds, eating plainly from your own garden rather than buying food that had passed through many hands: we'd recognise some sense in those even today. The reasons were wrong. Some of the habits were good.

Let's set this beside your own world for a moment. You probably have a sense, today, of what health advice looks like. A government website. A leaflet at the doctor's surgery. A label on the back of a cereal box. Advice to eat your vegetables, to sleep well, to stay calm, to wash your hands. Much of it comes from vast studies involving thousands of people. In 1348, health advice came from learned men in universities, reasoning from ancient books. And yet, if you were to read the regimens of the plague years, you might be surprised how much of the tone is familiar. Eat moderately. Rest. Don't panic. Keep your house clean and aired. Avoid crowds. It is very nearly what our own grandparents were told.

Before anyone sits, there is a small ritual, and it's a ritual of cleanliness. Nuta goes round the table with a brass basin and a jug, and each person holds out their hands in turn, and she pours a little water over them, and they dry them on a linen towel that hangs over her arm. Washing the hands before a meal, with water poured from a ewer, was a custom of polite households all over medieval Europe, described in books of manners and shown in paintings of feasts. It was partly courtesy and partly sense, since people ate a good deal with their fingers, from shared dishes. In a summer like this one, Lapa has made it a rule that nobody, not even Piero, not even the baby, comes to the table without it. She would not be able to tell you about germs. But she knows that clean hands at a clean table, in a clean house, feel safer than dirty ones, and on that, at least, she is right.

The family gathers at the table. Monna Tessa says a short blessing, in Latin, and everyone crosses themselves, and then there is the soft clatter of spoons in wooden bowls.

Piero wants to know why there's no meat. Lapa says because it's summer, and because the doctors say so. Piero says that the doctors don't have to eat beans every night. Monna Tessa laughs before she can stop herself, and then pretends she was coughing.

Meo, who is fourteen and has been reading, asks his father whether it's true that the doctors in the city say the plague comes from the stars. Niccolò says some of them do. Meo asks whether that means there's nothing anyone can do. And Niccolò, who has thought about this a great deal, says no. It means that the stars may have made the air bad, but a man can still choose what air he breathes. That's why they're here. That's why the gate is shut. Meo thinks about that, and writes something in his little book, and goes back to his soup.

Nanni, who woke up when the lamp was lit, sits on his mother's lap and is fed small pieces of bread dipped in the soup. He gets most of it on his face.

And here we reach the next point where historians, and scientists, have disagreed, and this one is a big one. It's about the disease itself.

For most of the twentieth century, it was simply assumed that the Black Death was bubonic plague, the disease caused by a bacterium called Yersinia pestis, which was identified in Hong Kong in 1894 during a later plague outbreak. But some scholars began to doubt it. In 1984, the zoologist Graham Twigg argued that the Black Death spread too fast, and in the wrong places, to be the plague that modern doctors knew, and he suggested anthrax instead. In 2002, the historian Samuel Cohn, in a book called The Black Death Transformed, argued at length that the medieval disease behaved so differently from modern plague, in its seasons, its speed, and its death rates, that it could not have been the same illness at all. It was a serious argument, made by serious scholars, and for some years it was genuinely open.

Then the question was settled, in an unexpected way. By science, from teeth.

In 2010, a team led by Stephanie Haensch tested bone and dental samples from skeletons in plague burial grounds in several European countries and found the genetic traces of Yersinia pestis. And in 2011, a team led by Kirsten Bos, with Hendrik Poinar and Johannes Krause, reconstructed a draft of the whole genome of the bacterium, from the teeth of people buried in the Black Death cemetery at East Smithfield, in London, dug in the very year 1348 or 1349, on the site later occupied by the Royal Mint. They found that the medieval bacterium didn't differ greatly from strains of plague that exist today. The disease of 1348 was plague. The doubters had asked the right questions, and science had answered them.

What still remains open, as we'll see much later tonight, is exactly how it travelled. But the name of the killer, at least, is no longer in doubt.

Supper is finished. The bowls are scraped and wiped, and the bread crusts go to Moro, who has been waiting by the door with tremendous patience. Nuta takes the pot off the chain and sets it on the hearthstones to cool. The fire beneath it has burned down to a heap of glowing coals.

And now, Lapa does something that she does every evening, and which you might find very strange, if you'd just walked in out of a warm July night.

She kneels by the hearth, and she takes the juniper twigs that came through the hatch in the basket, and she lays them on the coals. And then she reaches into a little earthenware jar on the shelf beside the hearth and brings out a handful of dried rosemary, and a pinch of something else, a few small, pale, glassy grains, and she scatters them over the top.

At once, a thick, white, fragrant smoke begins to rise, and curl up toward the blackened beams, and drift out through the kitchen door into the rest of the house. It smells of resin and pine forests and something sweet and almost churchlike.

Piero, under the table, sneezes. Moro sneezes too. And Caterina, who is eleven and notices everything, asks her mother the question that you might be asking yourself. Why are you lighting a fire, Mamma, when it's so hot?

Lapa smiles at her, and wipes her hands on her apron, and says that she'll tell her in a little while, when the dishes are done.

## Part Four — Around Nine O'Clock: Juniper, Rosemary and Smoke

Here is this hour's small question, and it's a small one indeed. What were those pale, glassy grains that Lapa scattered on the coals?

Let's kneel down beside her and see.

Imagine that you are kneeling on the warm brick at the edge of the hearth, with Caterina beside you. The dishes are done. The kitchen is dim, lit only by the little oil lamp on the table and the red glow of the coals. The smoke is rising in slow white ribbons, folding and unfolding in the air above the hearth, and drifting toward the open door. Your eyes sting a little. Your nose is full of it. Juniper, sharp and clean, like crushing a pine needle between your fingers. Rosemary, warm and green and slightly bitter. And under both, something richer and sweeter, the smell of a church on a feast day, of Mass and candles and old stone.

Lapa opens the little earthenware jar and tips it toward you so that you can see inside. At the bottom are a few small lumps of resin, pale yellow, almost translucent, like drops of honey that have hardened. She takes one between her finger and thumb and holds it up to the lamp. The light shines through it.

It is frankincense.

That's the answer to our small question. Frankincense is the dried resin of a small, gnarled tree that grows in the dry lands of southern Arabia and the Horn of Africa, and it has been burned as incense, in temples and churches, for thousands of years. It came to Florence the way pepper and cinnamon and silk did, through long chains of merchants, by caravan and by ship, to the great ports of Venice and Genoa and Pisa, and from there to the spice-sellers of the city. It was expensive. Most families would only smell it in church. But a wool merchant with a good year behind him could buy a little jar of it, and in the spring of 1348, a great many people who could afford it did.

Lapa bought this jar in April, from an apothecary near the Old Market, on the last morning before they left the city. It has to last. So she uses only a few grains a night, and the rest of the smoke comes from the juniper and the rosemary and the other herbs of her own garden: sage, bay, rue, and sometimes the dried flowers of lavender.

There's a lovely detail about the shop where she bought it. In Florence, the apothecaries, the spice-sellers who made up medicines and sold incense and sugar and wax and pigments for painters, belonged to one of the great guilds of the city, the Arte dei Medici e Speziali, the guild of physicians and apothecaries. Doctors and spice-merchants were members of the same guild, because the doctors prescribed and the apothecaries supplied, and both dealt in the same precious substances from far away. Its most famous member had died a quarter of a century before this summer. In the 1290s, a young Florentine who wanted to hold public office had to be enrolled in a guild, and so he joined this one, the guild of the doctors and the spice-sellers. His name was Dante Alighieri. So when Lapa walked into that little shop near the Old Market in April, she was walking into the world of the guild that had once counted the greatest poet of Italy among its members.

The juniper, by contrast, cost her nothing. It grows wild on the dry, stony slopes of these hills, a prickly, low evergreen shrub with blue-black berries, and Cecco's sons cut it for her from the scrub above the olive terraces, where the land is too steep to farm. The rosemary and sage and bay come from her own garden. The rue, with its strange, bitter, almost oily smell, grows in a corner by the well. Every one of these plants was thought, in the medicine of this century, to be warm and dry in its nature, and so to be good against the cold, damp corruption of plague air. There was a whole science behind the choice, written down in herbals and books of remedies, and Lapa, who has never read one, knows most of it by heart, from her mother and her grandmother.

There's one more thing she knows, and it's a practical thing. A fire of green herbs alone smokes too much and burns badly. So she lays the herbs on a bed of good charcoal, or on the dying embers of the cooking fire, which burn hot and steady with very little smoke of their own. The coals do the burning. The herbs do the smelling. It's a small art, and she has practised it every night for three months.

Now Caterina asks her question again. Why light a fire on such a hot night? And this time, Lapa answers her.

She says that the air is sick. That down there, in the city, the air has gone bad, the way water goes bad in a stagnant ditch, and that the bad air is what makes people ill. She says that a fire cleans the air, and that sweet smoke drives the bad air away, the way a breeze clears a room. She says that's why the doctors tell people to burn sweet woods and herbs in their houses, and to carry flowers. And she says that as long as this fire is burning, and the smoke is sweet, the air in their house will be clean.

Caterina thinks about this, very seriously, and says that she will help keep it burning.

So here, at last, is the first of the three questions I asked you at the very beginning of the night. On one of the hottest nights of the year, why would a careful mother keep a fire burning in her house all night long, and feed it, not with logs, but with herbs?

The answer is that she believed she was cleaning the air.

To the people of 1348, the most convincing explanation of the plague was that it lived in corrupted air, which learned physicians called a miasma. As we heard at supper, the masters of Paris believed that a meeting of the planets had poisoned the air, and that people fell ill by breathing it in. Other writers blamed vapours rising out of the earth, or from swamps, or from rotting things, or winds blowing from the south. But whatever the source, the danger was the same. Bad air. And the remedy followed naturally from it. You kept away from places where the air was foul. You closed your house against it. And you fought it with fire and sweet smoke. Fire was thought to purify the air, to dry it and correct it. Fragrant smoke, from woods and herbs and resins that were themselves thought to be warm and dry and wholesome, would overcome the corruption. That's why Boccaccio's Florentines walked through the streets holding flowers and spices to their noses. And that's why, in houses all over Europe this year, people burned juniper, rosemary, bay, pine, and, if they could afford it, frankincense, and myrrh, and aloe wood.

And it wasn't only ordinary people. The most famous fire of the whole plague burned in the palace of the Pope.

In 1348, the Pope was not living in Rome but in Avignon, in the south of France, where the papal court had settled earlier in the century. The Pope was Clement the Sixth. And his physician was a man named Guy de Chauliac, one of the greatest surgeons of the Middle Ages, who later wrote a vast textbook of surgery, the Chirurgia Magna, finished in 1363, in which he described the plague at Avignon as he'd seen it himself, from January of 1348, lasting about seven months. Later historians, retelling the story, report that on Guy's advice the Pope kept to his rooms through the worst of it, with great fires burning, and admitted almost no one. I should tell you that the exact details of that story come to us mostly through later writers, and the image you'll sometimes read, of the Pope sitting all summer between two roaring fires, may be a little embroidered. But the heart of it, a Pope shut away in his palace with fires kept burning to purify the air, is told consistently. And Clement survived.

So the first mystery is solved. The careful mother keeps a fire on the hottest night of the year, and feeds it with herbs, because she believes, as the Pope's own doctor believed, that the sweet smoke is cleaning the air that her children breathe.

Was she right? That's the interesting part. And it brings us to this part's disagreement, which is not between modern historians, but between people living in the fourteenth century itself.

Not everyone who lived through the Black Death believed that bad air was the whole story. Many ordinary people saw, with their own eyes, that the sickness seemed to pass from person to person, from a sick man to those who nursed him, from a house to its neighbour, from a ship to a port. Boccaccio himself says that to talk with the sick, or even to touch their clothes or anything they had touched, seemed to carry the sickness to the healthy. That is an idea of contagion, of something being passed on, and it sits rather awkwardly beside the idea of a poisoned atmosphere. Some writers held both ideas together, saying that the sick gave off corrupted vapours that then infected those nearby. And a few, writing a little later, came down firmly on the side of contagion from experience. The best known of them is the Andalusian scholar and statesman Ibn al-Khatib, of Granada, who argued in a treatise written in the years after the Black Death, and I'll paraphrase him, that contagion was proven by experience, by observation, and by the reports of trustworthy people, and that anyone who ignored such evidence was mistaken. It was a quarrel between theory and observation, and the observers, in the end, turned out to be closer to the truth.

As for Lapa's fire, the honest modern answer is that it almost certainly did not clean the air in any way that mattered. Plague is not carried by bad smells. And yet, here and there, writers have wondered whether smoke in a house might sometimes have made it a less welcoming place for fleas. I'd treat that as no more than a possibility. Nobody has shown it. What the fire certainly did was something else. It gave a frightened family something to do, every night, with their own hands, against something they could not see. And that, as we'll see later tonight, was medicine of a kind too.

Let's set this beside your own home. You might have a scented candle on a shelf somewhere, or a little diffuser of essential oils, or an air purifier humming in a corner, with a filter that catches particles from the air. Some of those are for pleasure, and some are for health. And in 2020 and the years after, we talked endlessly about air: about ventilation, about opening windows, about filters in classrooms and offices, about what a room full of people breathes. We were thinking about the air in our houses, the way Lapa is. We had better reasons. But the feeling, of watching the air in your own house, is not new at all.

The juniper has caught properly now. Small blue flames run along the twigs, and they crackle and pop, and the smoke thins a little and turns grey and fragrant. Lapa lays a few more sprigs of rosemary on top, and the green needles curl and blacken and send up a fresh white plume.

Caterina takes a sprig from the bundle and adds it herself, very carefully, the way she's been shown. Then she sits back on her heels and watches it burn. She looks, for a moment, very like her grandmother.

Monna Tessa, in the doorway, is watching too. And she says, in her dry way, that in her day they burned the same herbs for the same reason, in the year when the fever came from the marshes, and nobody bought frankincense from an apothecary, because nobody had any money. Lapa says that's because nobody in Monna Tessa's day had a husband in the wool guild. Monna Tessa says that her husband was in the wool guild for thirty years, thank you very much, and they both laugh.

The fire on the hearth will burn all night. Nuta will keep it fed until she sleeps, and after that Lapa will rise once or twice to lay more herbs on it. It will never be large. Just a low, steady bed of coals, a little flame, and a thread of sweet smoke, rising through the house, and out through the small high window under the kitchen roof, into the night.

And outside that window, over the hills to the east, beyond Fiesole, something is changing. A little after half past nine by the sun, the moon is rising. It's a waning moon tonight, a little past full, nearly nine-tenths lit, and as it comes up over the ridge it lays a soft, pale, silver light across the olive terraces and the tiled roofs and the long empty road to the city. I worked that out, I should say, using a modern astronomical model, because nobody wrote down the moon over Fiesole on this particular night. But the sky keeps good records, and the sums are simple.

And in that moonlight, from where she stands at the door, Monna Tessa can see the white line of the road running down to Florence, and nothing on it, nothing at all.

## Part Five — Around Half Past Nine: A Letter by Moonlight

The small question for this part of the night is the one Niccolò has been carrying in his tunic since eight o'clock. What does the letter say?

Let's go up and sit with him while he reads it.

Imagine that you are climbing the outside stair to the loggia behind him. The stone steps are worn into shallow dips by a hundred years of feet. The moon has just cleared the ridge to the east, low and large and a little lopsided, the colour of old ivory, and it throws your shadows long across the floor of the loggia. Below, the whole valley is silvered. The olive leaves shimmer when the faint night breeze turns them. The cypresses stand black and still. Somewhere down the slope, a nightingale is singing, a long, liquid, tumbling song, stopping and starting again.

Niccolò sits on the stone bench under the middle arch. Nuta has brought out the oil lamp and set it beside him. He takes the folded paper from his tunic, breaks the little blob of wax that seals it, and unfolds it, and holds it toward the light.

You sit on the bench beside him, at a respectful distance, and wait.

It takes him a long time to read it, though it isn't long. He reads it twice. Then he lets out a breath that he seems to have been holding since April.

The letter is from his factor, a careful, elderly man named Ser Bindo, whom Niccolò has known all his life, and it says, in the dry, abbreviated language of a merchant's letter, the things a merchant needs to know. The workshop is closed and locked. The warehouse is shut. The wool that came in from the port before Easter is still in its bales, and Bindo has not opened them, and will not. Some of the spinners and weavers who worked for the house have died, and Bindo names them, and Niccolò's lips move as he reads the names. Some have fled to the countryside. Bindo himself is well, and his wife is well, and they are keeping to their house. The cathedral bells, he says, are quiet. And he believes, and he underlines the words, that in the last two weeks the mortality in the city has begun to ease.

That's the answer to our small question. The letter says that the house is shut, that some are lost, that Bindo is alive, and that there may be a little hope.

Niccolò folds it again, very carefully, and puts it back in his tunic, over his heart. He doesn't say anything. After a while, Lapa comes up the stairs and sits beside him, and he hands it to her without a word, and she reads it, and takes his hand.

Ser Bindo is invented, I should say, as is his letter. But letters very like it were written all over Europe this year, and some of them survive. And the most remarkable of them, perhaps, was written in Avignon, at the papal court, a little under three months before our night.

Its author was a man from Flanders named Louis Heyligen, a musician and a canon of the church of Saint Donatian in Bruges, who was living at Avignon as the representative of his fellow canons at the papal court. On the twenty-seventh of April in 1348, he wrote a letter home to Bruges. The original is lost, but a chronicler in Flanders copied out a version of it, and that copy survives. In it, Heyligen tells his friends what he has heard about where the sickness came from, far to the east. He describes how it came to the Mediterranean on ships, and he tells of galleys arriving from the east, carrying the sickness, and of how the plague reached Marseille. He describes the signs of the disease, and how quickly it killed. He writes that it was said that half the population of Avignon had died. And he describes the measures that the Pope and the town authorities had taken, and the fear in the streets. It is a frightened letter, and an honest one, written by a man who did not know whether he would live to see it delivered. And, as it happens, he did live. Heyligen survived the plague, and is thought to have lived until about 1361.

The papal court had been at Avignon since 1309, when a French Pope, Clement the Fifth, settled there rather than in a turbulent Rome, and it would stay until the 1370s. By 1348, Avignon was one of the busiest cities in Europe, full of cardinals and clerks, bankers and lawyers, envoys and petitioners, and a great many Italian merchants, Florentines among them. News from Avignon reached Florence quickly, by the standards of the age, because so many Florentine firms had agents there.

Think about the journey that letter made. From Avignon, up the valley of the Rhône, across France, into Flanders, carried by a courier, or by a merchant, or passed from hand to hand, over weeks. Every letter this year travelled like that, slowly, at the pace of a horse or a mule or a ship. When Niccolò's factor writes from Florence, a few miles away, the letter takes a day to reach him. When news comes from Avignon, or Venice, or Naples, it may take weeks, and it may be out of date before it arrives.

Let's set that against your world. If someone you loved were in a city where there was danger, you could reach them this minute. A message on your phone, read within seconds. A video call, with their face on your screen. News alerts arriving in your pocket faster than you could walk across the room. When we stayed apart in 2020, we were apart in body, but our voices travelled freely. Niccolò, sitting on his loggia in the moonlight, holding a single sheet of paper over his heart, has had no news from his city for nine days. And that news is already a day old.

The letter itself is a small marvel, if you look at it closely. It's written on paper, not parchment. Paper had come into Italy from the Islamic world, and by this century Italian mills, above all at the little town of Fabriano in the Marche, were making it in quantity from old linen rags, beaten to pulp in water by great hammers driven by mill wheels. Some Fabriano sheets carried a watermark, a faint design pressed into the paper while it was wet, which you can see if you hold the sheet up to the light. Merchants loved paper. It was far cheaper than parchment, and a merchant house like Niccolò's might write and receive hundreds of letters a year: to agents, to suppliers, to buyers, to partners, to banks. Ser Bindo is called Ser because that was the courtesy title of a notary, a man trained in the law and in the writing of contracts, and many merchants' factors had that training.

How many such letters were written in Tuscany in this century, we can only guess. But we do know of one merchant whose letters survived in astonishing numbers. His name was Francesco di Marco Datini, of the town of Prato, just a few miles from Florence. In the decades after this summer, he built up a great trading company, and when he died in 1410 he left his house, his papers and his fortune to the poor of his town. His papers were stored away, and forgotten, and rediscovered in the nineteenth century: well over a hundred thousand letters, along with account books, contracts and bills of exchange. It is one of the greatest archives of ordinary business life to survive from the Middle Ages. And here is the gentle connection to our night. In 1348, Francesco Datini was a boy of about thirteen. The plague of this year took his father and his mother, and he was left to make his way in the world with a younger brother. He grew up to become one of the richest men in Tuscany, and he never stopped writing letters. When I think of the paper in Niccolò's tunic, I think of the boy in Prato who would one day fill rooms with letters like it.

Now, there's another writer of this year who described where the plague came from, and his account is one of the most famous and most argued over in the whole history of the Black Death. His name was Gabriele de' Mussi, a notary of Piacenza, in northern Italy, and he wrote a treatise about the plague, probably around 1348 or 1349. In it, he tells a story about Kaffa, a trading city on the Crimean coast of the Black Sea that was held by the Genoese. He describes the Mongol army that was besieging the city, and how the sickness broke out among the besiegers, and he says that it then passed into the city, and that Genoese ships fleeing from Kaffa carried it into the Mediterranean. I'll leave aside the grimmest parts of his story. They are often retold, and they may or may not be true.

Because that is the question with de' Mussi. For a long time, people assumed that he had been at Kaffa himself, and saw these things with his own eyes. But historians who have looked closely at his life and his text think that he was almost certainly in Piacenza the whole time, and was writing down what he had heard from others, perhaps from sailors or merchants who had come back from the east. So his account is precious, and very early, but it's a report of reports. The siege of Kaffa was real. Whether the plague passed into Europe in the exact way he describes is something historians still argue about.

And that brings us to this part's disagreement, which is about the origin of the whole pandemic. Where did the Black Death begin?

For a long time, the most common answer was China, or somewhere in the steppes of Central Asia, and the evidence was thin. Then, in 2022, a team led by Maria Spyrou, Johannes Krause and the historian Philip Slavin published a remarkable study in the journal Nature. They had looked at two medieval cemeteries near Lake Issyk-Kul, in the Chüy Valley of what is now Kyrgyzstan. These cemeteries, excavated in the 1880s, contained an unusually large number of gravestones dated to the years 1338 and 1339, some of them recording that the person had died of a pestilence. The researchers took samples from teeth of people buried there, and found the DNA of the plague bacterium. And when they compared it with other plague genomes, they found that this strain sat very close to the common ancestor of the strains that went on to spread across the world, including the Black Death strain. They argued that the Black Death's ancestry could be traced to this region of Central Asia, about a decade before it reached the Black Sea.

It was a striking result. But not everyone thinks it settles the question. The historian Monica Green, who has written widely on the history of plague, has argued that the great branching of plague lineages that led to the Black Death happened earlier, in the thirteenth century, and that the movement of Mongol armies and their supplies across Eurasia may have carried the bacterium into new regions over many decades, long before 1346. In that view, the Kyrgyz graves are one important point on a much longer and more complicated journey. Most scholars agree that the pandemic came from somewhere in the interior of Asia, where plague lives among wild rodents to this day. Exactly where, and exactly when it began moving west, is still a lively and friendly argument.

Lapa hands the letter back to Niccolò. He tucks it away. She asks whether he'll go down to the city when it's over, to see to the workshop. He says not yet. Not until Bindo says it's safe, and not until the summer is over, and perhaps not until the first frost. He says he would rather lose a year's trade than lose any of them.

She leans against his shoulder. Below them, the nightingale has started again.

It is a strange thing to think about, sitting here, how far the threads of this night reach. The Genoese at Kaffa, on the Black Sea, were there because of trade: grain from the plains of the Ukraine, furs and wax from the north, silk and spices that came along the caravan routes from the east. The Genoese and the Venetians had trading posts strung along those coasts, and their galleys went back and forth every season between the Black Sea, Constantinople, and the ports of Italy. It was a connected world, more connected than we often imagine the Middle Ages to be. Wool from England came to Niccolò's workshop. Frankincense from Arabia came to Lapa's jar. And along the same roads and sea lanes, in the same holds and the same packs, without anyone knowing, came the sickness. Nobody in this house would put it that way. But Niccolò, a merchant, who knows how goods travel, has understood something of it in his bones. That is why the bales are still unopened.

From the stair, there is a small sound. Piero is standing there, in his long linen shirt, barefoot, holding the dog by the scruff of its neck. He says that he can't sleep, because it's too hot, and because the smoke makes his nose itch, and because Moro won't lie still. ## Part Six — Around Ten O'Clock: A Story on the Loggia

Here is the small question for this hour of the night. In a summer of fear, what did the doctors of Florence prescribe that you could not buy at any apothecary's shop?

Let's find out, out here on the loggia, in the moonlight.

Because the family has gathered. Piero is still here, between his parents, and the dog is at their feet. Caterina has come up from the kitchen, smelling of rosemary smoke, and curled herself onto the end of the bench. Meo is sitting on the top step of the stair, his back against the wall. Imagine that you are sitting on the warm stone floor of the loggia now, with your back against a pillar, and your knees drawn up. I won't tell you the whole of it. It's a long, winding, sly story, with a good deal of nonsense in it, and a donkey, and a bishop who comes off very badly. Piero laughs so much that he gets hiccups. I mean that quite literally. And other physicians of the time said much the same. In the medicine of this century, fear and grief were not just feelings. So Monna Tessa, telling her story about the donkey and the bishop, is, in the eyes of the best physicians of her city, practising medicine.

And here is the most beautiful connection of the whole night. That advice, to go into gardens, to keep good company, to sing, to tell tales, and to keep sadness at bay, is exactly what Boccaccio's ten young storytellers do in the Decameron. They leave the city. They go to a villa in these hills. They walk in its gardens, and eat simple food, and sing and dance in the evenings, and every afternoon, in the heat of the day, they sit in a shady place on the grass and tell stories, one each, ten a day. Boccaccio gives them names, and I'd like you to hear them, because they're lovely names, and most of them were chosen to mean something. Boccaccio says that he's hiding their real identities behind these names, and scholars have spent centuries enjoying the puzzle of what each name might mean: Fiammetta, the little flame, was the name Boccaccio used in several of his works for a woman he loved; Filostrato might mean one laid low by love; Panfilo, all love; Dioneo has an echo of Dione, the mother of Venus. Each of them has a part to play in the little world they make. He uses that freedom, as you might expect, for some of the most mischievous stories in the book. In the evenings, Boccaccio tells us, there is music. Dioneo plays the lute, and Fiammetta the viol, and the others dance, and someone sings a song. Every one of the ten days ends with a song, and the songs are part of the book, written by Boccaccio himself, so that the Decameron is not only a book of stories but a little songbook too.

It is an ordered, gracious, courteous kind of life. Somebody is always in charge of the household, of the meals and the servants and the hours. Nobody is allowed to bring sorrow into the garden. And it is easy to forget, reading it, that every page of it was written in the shadow of what Boccaccio had seen in the city.

Their stay lasts about two weeks. On ten of those days, they tell ten stories each, which is how the book comes to have a hundred stories, and how it gets its name, from the Greek for ten days. On some days, out of devotion, they rest and don't tell stories at all. And there's a detail I love: Boccaccio has them observe a kind of decency in their pleasures. They don't overeat. Let's set this beside your own life for a moment. You may be listening to this right now for something like the same reason. To be calmed. And here is this hour's disagreement, and it's about the Decameron, and it's a gentle one.

Readers have argued for centuries about what Boccaccio meant by the storytellers' flight. Others have read the frame very differently: as a picture of people rebuilding a small, orderly, decent world in the middle of chaos, with rules, and courtesy, and moderation, and kindness, as a kind of medicine for the soul, and a model of how to survive with your humanity intact. ## Part Seven — Around Half Past Ten: The Quiet Bells

The small question for this hour is a listening question. Out here on the hill, in the deep of a summer night, what can you hear? And what can't you hear, that you ought to?

Let's be very quiet for a moment, and listen.

The family has gone in. After the stories, Lapa sent the children down to bed, and they went without much protest, because it had grown late and the moon was well up and even Piero was yawning. Nuta carried Nanni. Monna Tessa went down the stairs on Meo's arm. And now the loggia is empty, except for you, and the lamp, which Niccolò left burning low on the bench, and the moonlight lying across the floor between the arches.

Imagine you are standing alone at the edge of the loggia, with your hands on the warm stone parapet, looking out. The whole valley lies open below you, silver and grey and black. The olive trees are a soft, shimmering grey. The cypresses are dark exclamation marks. The road is a pale thread. And beyond it, in the bottom of the valley, the city lies in its walls, dark, with only a handful of tiny lights still showing.

And you listen.

You hear the crickets, a great soft continuous chorus from every terrace and every patch of grass. You hear the nightingale, nearer now, in the laurel by the garden wall. You hear, from somewhere up the hill toward Fiesole, the hoot of a little owl, a single soft note, repeated, like someone blowing across the mouth of a bottle. You hear, from Cecco's side of the wall, a mule shifting its weight in its stall. A dog, far off. The faint whisper of the breeze through the olive leaves. And, if you listen very carefully, the tiny crackle of the fire in the kitchen below you, where Nuta has just laid on another handful of juniper.

That's a great deal to hear. But there is something you don't hear.

On an ordinary summer night, from a hill above Florence, you would hear bells. Florence is a city of bells. Every church has its bell, every monastery and convent and friary, and there are scores of them inside the walls. They ring for the services of the night and the morning. They ring for feasts. The great bell of the commune, in the tower of the palace of the government, rings to call the citizens together and to mark the closing of the gates. And above all, in the old way, the bells ring for the dead: a slow, deliberate tolling, from the dead person's parish church, so that the whole neighbourhood knows that a soul has passed, and can pray for it.

Tonight, as on most nights this summer, the bells of Florence are very nearly silent.

That's the answer to our small question. What you can hear tonight is the whole living world of a summer hillside. What you can't hear, that you ought to, are the bells.

Now, you might have noticed that I've been rather careful about time tonight. I've said "around ten" and "around half past ten" and "a little after half past nine by the sun." That's partly because this house has no clock. There is no clock anywhere on this hillside. In this century, mechanical clocks with striking bells were only just beginning to appear in the towers of a few great Italian cities. Milan had a famous striking clock in the tower of a church there by the 1330s, and others followed. But most people, most of the time, told the hours of the day by the sun, and the hours of the night by the stars and the moon, and above all by the bells of the churches, which rang for the canonical hours of prayer. When the bells fall silent, a whole city loses part of its sense of time. Dante, who knew those bells well, wrote about one of them in the Paradiso, the third part of his Divine Comedy, which he finished not long before his death in 1321. In the fifteenth canto, the poet meets the spirit of his own great-great-grandfather, a knight named Cacciaguida, who remembers the Florence of his own day, two centuries earlier: a small, sober, peaceful city, within its old circle of walls. And I'll paraphrase, because the lines are famous and I'd rather not set them down from memory: he says that the city, within that ancient circle, still takes its hours of terce and nones from there. Scholars generally understand him to mean the bell of the Badia, the old Benedictine abbey near the heart of the city, whose bell rang the hours of prayer, and by which the people of Florence, for generations, told the time of day and began and ended their work. Dante's Florence, in that canto, is a city measured out by the sound of a bell.

Tonight, the Badia's bell still rings for the hours, perhaps. The monks still keep their round of prayer. But from up here, on this hill, on this still night, you cannot hear it.

There's a reason the canonical hours mattered so much to time-telling. The day, in the church's reckoning, was divided by a series of services: matins and lauds in the night and before dawn, then prime at the first hour of the morning, terce at the third, sext at midday, nones in the afternoon, vespers at evening, and compline at bedtime. Those hours weren't fixed to the minute. They stretched in summer and shrank in winter, because the daylight was divided into twelve hours whatever its length. A summer hour in Florence was a long, generous thing. A winter hour was short. Nobody minded, because nobody had a clock to disagree with the sun.

Niccolò, standing here every evening this summer, has had to learn to read the time of night by the height of the moon over Fiesole, and by the slow wheeling of the stars.

Let's put that beside your world for a moment. You probably have a dozen clocks within reach right now, on your phone, on your oven, on the corner of a screen, all agreeing to the second. And you probably have something else in your pocket, too, that rings for news. A phone that buzzes and chimes and lights up whenever something happens, anywhere in the world. In 2020, many of us found that we had to learn to turn it off. To stop checking the numbers every hour. To stop watching the news before bed. Doctors and psychologists advised it: limit your news, protect your sleep, protect your peace of mind.

Keep that thought in mind for a moment. Because it is going to lead us straight to our second mystery.

Let's go back to the little city of Pistoia, twenty miles or so to the north-west, and to the health ordinances that its government passed in May of this year, the ones we met earlier tonight at the gate. They were drawn up in a time of fear by the city's council and its officials, men whom the translations call the Wise Men, and they deal, one by one, with the practical business of a town in a time of mortality. Who may travel where. What goods may come in. How the butchers must work. And a great deal about the dead, and the burying of the dead, and funerals.

It's worth saying a word about who those men were, because it tells us something about how a Tuscan city governed itself. Pistoia, like Florence, was a commune, a self-governing city republic, and at the top of its government in this period sat a small council of eight Elders, the Anziani, together with an official called the Standard-bearer of Justice. They were not kings or lords. They were citizens, merchants and notaries and guildsmen, and they were chosen for short terms, often by drawing names by lot from bags of eligible men, so that no one family could hold power for too long. That's who wrote the plague ordinances of Pistoia: ordinary, prosperous townsmen, serving their turn in office, in the worst season their city had ever known, sitting around a table and trying, item by item, to think of anything that might help.

They didn't get everything right. They couldn't have. Some of what they ordered rested on the idea of bad air, and did little good. But some of it, like keeping people from travelling to and from places where the plague already was, pointed toward the future, toward the idea that a sickness could be kept out by keeping people apart.

I won't take you through those rules about the dead. Some of them are hard to hear, and they are not what tonight is for. But I will tell you about the ones that concern the living. Because many of them are, in their way, rules about sound, and about fear.

The ordinances limited the gatherings of mourners. They forbade the old custom of crowds of people going to the house of the dead to wail and lament. They limited the number of people who could accompany a body to the church. They forbade criers from going through the streets announcing deaths and funerals. And then there is the rule about the bells.

So here, at last, is the second of our three mysteries. I asked you at the beginning of the night: why would a town that was losing so many of its people pass a law forbidding its church bells to ring for the dead?

The answer, in the words of the Pistoia ordinance itself, as given in one widely used English translation, is this: "So that the sounds of bells might not depress the infirm."

The bellringers of the cathedral tower in Pistoia were forbidden to ring any bell for a funeral, and no one else was allowed to ring those bells for such an occasion. A parish church or friary where the dead person was to be buried could ring its own bell, but only once, and moderately.

That's all. That's the whole reason. Not because the dead didn't matter. Not to hide anything. But because, in a town where people were dying every day, the bells were tolling every hour. And every time they tolled, every sick person lying in bed, and every frightened family behind its shutters, heard them, and counted them, and felt their courage drain away. The councillors of Pistoia understood, as Tommaso del Garbo understood, that fear itself was dangerous. That despair could weaken the sick. And so, in the middle of the worst summer anyone in Europe had ever known, they did something very human and very kind. They turned the bells down.

Nor was Pistoia alone. Florentine chroniclers of the next generations remembered something similar in their own city: that the ringing of bells for the dead, and the crying of deaths in the street, were stopped, so that the sick would not hear them. I'll paraphrase that, because I haven't been able to check the exact wording. But the instinct was the same. In a time of overwhelming loss, the authorities of more than one Tuscan city decided that the living needed quiet.

So when you stand on this loggia tonight, and hear nothing but crickets and the nightingale, you are hearing, in a sense, a law. A decision made by worried men in a council chamber, to spare the sick the sound of their neighbours' funerals. The silence over Florence is not only the silence of loss. It is also, in a strange way, a silence of mercy.

And it's that same instinct, isn't it, that made us turn off the news in 2020, and put our phones in another room at night. To protect the living from a sound they could no longer bear to keep hearing.

Now, here is this hour's disagreement, and it's about how many there were, in Florence, to lose.

How large was the city before the plague came? Giovanni Villani, in his great survey of about 1338, estimated, from the amount of bread the city consumed, that Florence had something like ninety thousand mouths to feed within its walls. Some modern historians think that the city might have been larger than that, perhaps as many as a hundred and twenty thousand; others think Villani's figure is about right. Boccaccio, as we heard, wrote that more than a hundred thousand died between March and July, which cannot quite be true if Villani was right. And then there's what came afterwards. In 1427, the Florentine government carried out an extraordinary tax survey, called the catasto, which recorded every household in the city and its territory, with its members, its property, and its debts. In the 1970s, the historians David Herlihy and Christiane Klapisch-Zuber analysed it in a famous study. They found that by 1427, after the first great plague and the repeated outbreaks that followed it over the next eighty years, Florence held only around forty thousand people, far fewer than in Villani's time. So we know the fall was enormous. But just how much of it happened in this single summer of 1348, and how much in the waves that came after, is still argued over. Many estimates for this year alone fall somewhere around half the city.

That is the last time tonight that I'll give you a number like that. You've heard enough of them. And the night, from here on, is going to grow gentler.

The lamp on the bench is nearly out. You pick it up, carefully, cupping your hand around the little flame, and carry it down the outside stair into the garden. The gravel is cool now under your feet. The herbs in their beds smell stronger in the dark than they did by day. The lilies are pale ghosts in the moonlight. And from the kitchen door, a thin ribbon of sweet smoke is drifting out across the garden, and you walk through it, and it smells of juniper, and rosemary, and the church.

## Part Eight — Around Eleven O'Clock: Rosewater and the Doctor's Courage

Here is the small question for this part of the night. In a house with no doctor, who does the doctoring?

Let's go upstairs and see who's awake.

The upper rooms of the house are reached by a narrow stone stair from the great room, and they're hotter than the rooms below, because the day's heat has soaked into the tiles of the roof and is slowly giving itself back. The shutters are open on the north side, where no sun ever falls, to let in what breeze there is. On the south side, they're closed, and latched. Lapa has her reasons, and we'll come back to them. In the first room, on a wide wooden bed with a straw mattress and a linen sheet, the three older children are lying in a row, as they do every night this summer: Meo by the wall, then Piero, then Caterina by the window. None of them has a cover. It's far too hot for that. In the next room, Nanni sleeps in a little bed beside his parents' great one, which is empty, because Niccolò and Lapa are still downstairs. And in a small room of her own at the top of the tower, which she climbed to on Meo's arm, Monna Tessa is not asleep at all.

Imagine that you are standing in the doorway of the children's room now, in the half dark. No moonlight comes in through the open north window, but the sky beyond it is pale with the moon's glow, and the room is not quite dark. The room smells of linen and straw and, faintly, of rosemary smoke, which has drifted up the stair. You can hear the three children breathing, at three different speeds. Piero has flung one arm across his brother's face, and Meo, without waking, has moved it. Caterina is lying on her side, facing the window, with her eyes open.

She can't sleep. She tells you so, in a whisper, when she sees you. It's too hot. Her skin prickles. And she's been thinking about the letter.

Then there are footsteps on the stair, slow and careful, and a small yellow light. It's Monna Tessa, with a tiny oil lamp in one hand and a little glass flask in the other. She heard Caterina turning over, through the floor. She hears everything.

She sits on the edge of the bed. She uncorks the flask, and a smell rises from it, sweet and cool and very faintly like the inside of a rose. She pours a little into her palm, and dabs it, gently, on Caterina's forehead, and her temples, and the inside of her wrists. Rosewater. Then she does the same for Piero, who doesn't wake, and Meo, who mumbles something about ledgers and rolls over.

Caterina lets out a breath. The rosewater is cool as it dries on her skin. Her grandmother strokes her hair, slowly, and starts, very softly, to hum.

That's the answer to our small question. In a house with no doctor, the doctoring is done mostly by the women of the house, and above all, by the oldest.

That was true almost everywhere in medieval Europe. Learned physicians, the university men with their Latin books, were expensive, and most of them lived in towns. For the ordinary illnesses of ordinary families, fevers and coughs and burns and cuts and stomach-aches, the care came from mothers and grandmothers, from neighbours, from the local barber, who also pulled teeth and let blood, and from the apothecary's shop. And women carried a great store of practical knowledge, from one generation to the next: which herbs to boil for a cough, how to cool a fever with damp cloths, how to make a poultice, how to dress a wound, which foods to give a sick child. Monna Tessa's little flask of rosewater, made by steeping and distilling the petals of roses, was used to cool and comfort and to sweeten the air of a sickroom, and the plague writers of this year recommended it, along with vinegar, for sprinkling in houses and on the face and hands.

Let's set that beside your home for a moment. Somewhere in your house, there's probably a cupboard, or a drawer, with a jumble of plasters and paracetamol and cough syrup and a thermometer. If someone in your family is ill in the night, you might look up their symptoms online, or call a helpline, or drive to a hospital that's open all night, with doctors who've trained for years and machines that can see inside the body. Monna Tessa has a flask of rosewater, a jar of honey, a bundle of dried herbs, and sixty-four years of experience. In ordinary times, against ordinary illness, that was worth a great deal. Against the plague, honestly, none of it was worth very much at all. Which is precisely why this family's real defence is the gate.

The rosewater itself has travelled a long way to reach that little flask, at least in the knowledge of how to make it. The art of distilling, of heating a liquid in a closed vessel so that its vapour rises, cools, and drips down again as a purer, stronger liquid, was greatly developed by physicians and scholars in the Islamic world, and the word for the vessel they used, the alembic, comes into Italian and English from Arabic. Rosewater was made in great quantities in Persia and Syria, and was traded across the Mediterranean, and by this century Italian apothecaries were distilling their own. Monna Tessa's flask came from the same little shop near the Old Market as Lapa's frankincense. Its glass, very likely, was blown in Venice, or in a glasshouse in the Tuscan countryside.

She uses it sparingly, as she uses everything. She was a girl in a household that was not rich, and she has never forgotten it. When she was first married, she kept the household accounts in her head, because she could not write, and her husband, who could, used to check them against his ledgers and find that she was always right. She learned to read, a little, later in life, from her own children's lessons. She still prefers to count on her fingers.

And there's one more thing she does, every night, before she goes back up to her room, and she does it now. She goes to the window, and looks out at the hill, and makes the sign of the cross, slowly, toward the city in the valley, where her husband is buried, in the family's tomb in the church of their parish. She doesn't say anything. She just does it, and then she turns away.

Caterina is asleep. Monna Tessa sits a while longer, humming, and then she corks her flask, and picks up her lamp, and goes slowly back up the stair to her room.

And I want to tell you, now, about the learned physicians, because some of them showed great courage this year, and it seems right to remember it.

We met Guy de Chauliac earlier tonight, the Pope's physician at Avignon. In his great book of surgery, finished in 1363, Guy left one of the most precise medical descriptions of the plague that we have. He describes the pestilence at Avignon in 1348, and says that it came in two forms. In the first form, which came first and lasted about two months, the sickness seized the chest and the lungs, with a continual fever, and was swift. In the second form, which lasted for the rest of the epidemic, the fever came with swellings, in the armpits and the groin. That description, of a form of the disease in the lungs and a form with swellings, matches very well what modern doctors call pneumonic and bubonic plague. And Guy wrote about himself, too, with a frankness that is very rare for a medieval author. I'll paraphrase him, through a modern historian's retelling, because I haven't been able to check his exact Latin. He said that he did not dare to stay away from his patients, for fear of being disgraced, and that he was in continual fear. He said that toward the end of the epidemic he fell ill himself, and was in danger for some weeks, and recovered.

I find that very moving. A great physician, honest enough to say that he went to his patients not because he was fearless, but because he was ashamed to run away, and that he was afraid the whole time. That's the kind of courage most of us would recognise.

And not every physician was so fortunate. Some seventy miles south-east of here, in the university city of Perugia, there was a famous professor of medicine named Gentile da Foligno, one of the most respected medical writers in Italy. In the spring of this year, as the plague approached, he wrote several consilia, pieces of formal advice on how to avoid the plague and how to treat it, for the cities and the people who asked him. He kept working through the outbreak. And he died, in June of 1348, a few weeks before our night. His advice was copied and read for generations afterwards.

It's worth knowing, too, that Italian cities in this century had something that might surprise you: doctors paid out of public funds. Many towns, large and small, hired a physician or a surgeon on a salary, under a contract that historians call a condotta, to live in the town and treat its citizens, and sometimes to treat the poor for nothing. It was a kind of public medical service, centuries before anything of the sort existed in most of Europe. In a summer like this one, some of those salaried doctors stayed at their posts, and some fled, and in the years that followed, many towns had to work hard to find and keep a doctor at all. Being a doctor in 1348 was not a safe or comfortable profession. It took a certain kind of person to keep going to the sickroom door.

Now, here's this hour's disagreement, and it comes straight out of Guy de Chauliac's two forms.

Plague, as we now understand it, can take several forms. In its bubonic form, it's usually carried by the bite of an infected flea, and it causes the swellings Guy described. It can't easily pass straight from one person to another. In its pneumonic form, when the infection reaches the lungs, it can pass from person to person, through the air, in coughs and breath. That second form is much more dangerous, and much faster, but it's also self-limiting in a way, because it kills too quickly to spread very far without help. So which form drove the Black Death across Europe? Some historians and scientists have argued that the pneumonic form played a large part, especially in winter, and in the very fast spread of some outbreaks. Others, including Ole Benedictow, have argued strongly that pneumonic plague was always a minor part of the story, and that the great engine of the pandemic was bubonic plague, spread by fleas. Guy's account, with its two months of a lung disease followed by months of swellings, has been read in support of both views. Most scholars now think that both forms were present, in different proportions in different places and seasons, and that the bubonic form did most of the work. But just how much each one mattered is still discussed.

And here, at last, I can tell you why Lapa keeps the south windows shut. Some of the physicians of this century taught that the winds from the south were warm and damp, and so most likely to carry corrupted air, and advised keeping the windows on that side of a house closed in a time of pestilence, and opening those to the north, where the cool, dry winds came from. Lapa heard that advice in April, from the apothecary, and she has kept to it all summer. In this heat, it costs her a good deal of comfort. She keeps to it anyway.

Caterina, in the next room, has turned over again. And Piero, in his sleep, says something quite clear and distinct about a lizard, and then is quiet. Children's sleep in summer is a restless, tumbling thing, full of small voices. Lapa says that Piero has talked in his sleep since he could talk at all, and that it's the only time he ever tells the truth. Monna Tessa says he gets it from his grandfather, who once, in his sleep, confessed to having eaten an entire cake meant for a wedding, and was never allowed to forget it. These are the stories families tell about themselves, small and silly and precious, and they are told in every language and every century. Nobody ever writes them down. That's why, when you find one in an old letter or a diary, it feels like finding a coin in the grass.

Let's step out onto the little landing at the top of the stair, where there's a narrow window looking north, up toward Fiesole. The stones of the hill town glow faintly in the moonlight. Above it, the sky is thick with stars, though the moon has washed out the faintest of them. The Great Bear, which Italians call the Great Chariot, is low in the north-west now, its handle curving down toward the hills. And high overhead, cutting across the sky, you can just make out a long, faint band of pale light, the Milky Way, dimmed tonight by the moon, but still there, the way it has been over these hills for every summer night there has ever been.

You stand there for a while, at the narrow window. It's cooler here. The breeze, what there is of it, comes from the north. And from the room below, and the room beside you, and the little room at the top of the tower, there is the soft, steady sound of people, one after another, beginning to fall asleep.

Downstairs, Lapa and Niccolò are still awake. You can hear their voices, very low, from the great room. And from the kitchen, the faint crackle of the fire.

## Part Nine — Around Midnight: The Wool and the North

The small question for this hour comes from Niccolò's trade. The finest wool in his warehouse, the wool he hasn't opened, came from a long way away. Where? And what was happening there?

Let's go down to the great room, where he and Lapa are still awake.

Imagine that you are coming softly down the stone stair, into the largest room of the house. It has a high ceiling of dark wooden beams, a floor of red brick tiles, cool under your bare feet, and whitewashed walls. Against one wall stands a great carved chest, the kind called a cassone, which came with Lapa when she married, painted on the front with a faded scene of knights and ladies in a garden. There's a long table, a bench, two chairs, a shelf with a few pieces of glazed pottery, blue and white and green. A tall wooden cupboard holds the family's linen. And in the corner, on a small stand, a little painted panel of the Virgin and Child, with a gilded background that catches the light of the oil lamp and glows faintly in the dark.

That chest deserves a moment. In Florence, a bride's family gave her a dowry, in money and in goods, and the goods often travelled to her new home in a pair of painted wooden chests, carried through the streets in a little procession for everyone to see. Inside were her linen, her clothes, her sheets and towels and table-cloths, her sewing things, perhaps a few small treasures. In the next century, these chests would become great works of art, painted by famous artists with scenes from ancient history and poetry. Lapa's is a plainer, older thing, but she loves it. When they left the city in April, it was one of the few pieces of furniture that came with them, strapped to a mule, full of the household's linen. Clean linen, every Florentine housewife believed, was one of the foundations of a healthy house. Hers is washed and aired and folded, every week, by Nuta, in the big stone trough beside the garden well, and dried in the strong sun on the box hedges.

Niccolò and Lapa are sitting at the end of the table, with the lamp between them. They're talking about money, as married people do, at midnight, in difficult years. How long the stores will last. Whether Cecco can bring in the grape harvest in September, with only his own sons to help. What Ser Bindo should do with the wool.

And Niccolò says something that answers our small question. He says that the wool in the warehouse is English. The best of it, the finest and longest and softest, came from the monasteries of England, from great flocks on the hills, shipped across the sea and down through France or round by Gibraltar to the ports of Italy. Florentine merchants and bankers had been buying English wool for generations. Some of them had lent vast sums to the English king, Edward the Third, to pay for his wars in France, and had been ruined in the 1340s when he didn't pay them back. Niccolò's own father lost money in those years, and Niccolò has never trusted kings since.

We know a surprising amount about that trade, from a remarkable book written by a Florentine just a few years before our night. His name was Francesco Balducci Pegolotti, and he worked for the great Florentine banking and trading company of the Bardi, which sent him to Antwerp, to London, to Cyprus. Sometime around 1340, he put together a handbook for merchants, which later came to be called the Pratica della mercatura, the practice of trade. It is full of exactly the things a merchant needed to know: the weights and measures of dozens of cities, the coins in use, the customs dues at each port, the routes to the east, even advice on the journey to China. And it includes a long list of the religious houses of England and Scotland that sold wool to Italian merchants, close to two hundred of them, abbeys and priories, with notes on the quality of each one's wool and what it fetched. Many of them were Cistercian monasteries, whose great flocks grazed the hills of Yorkshire, the Welsh borders and the Cotswolds. When Niccolò speaks of the best English wool, he means wool very like that: from a named abbey, in a named valley, graded and priced and listed in a Florentine merchant's book.

That's the answer to our small question. His finest wool came from England. And England, though nobody in this house yet knows it, is about to meet the same sickness that has come to Florence.

The plague reached the south coast of England in the summer of 1348, probably in June, a few weeks before our night. It came, chroniclers said, through a port in Dorset, which most later historians have taken to be Melcombe, now part of Weymouth. Through the autumn and winter of this year, it moved slowly inland and along the coasts, to Bristol, to the towns of the south-west, to London. The worst of it, for most of England, would come in the spring and summer of 1349. So while Niccolò sits here in the lamplight, wondering about his bales, the shepherds on the hills where that wool was grown are living their ordinary lives, for a few months more.

And from that English year, 1349, some remarkable records survive. Let me tell you about two of them.

The first is a letter, written by a bishop to his clergy. His name was Ralph of Shrewsbury, and he was Bishop of Bath and Wells, in Somerset, in the south-west of England, from 1329 until his death in 1363. On the seventeenth of January in 1349, with the plague spreading through his diocese, he wrote to the priests of his parishes. The letter survives, and it's been translated by Rosemary Horrox in her collection. I'll paraphrase it, because I haven't been able to check her exact words. The bishop wrote that many priests were dead, and that in many parishes there was no priest left to hear the confessions of the dying. And so he told his people something remarkable. If they were near death and could find no priest, they should make their confession to one another, to any lay person, as the apostles had taught, and if no man could be found, then even to a woman. He also said that where no priest could be found, a deacon might give the sacrament to the dying.

That might not sound like much to us. But in the medieval church, confession was a sacrament, something that only a priest could properly hear. For a bishop to tell his people to confess to their neighbours, even to their wives and mothers and sisters, was an extraordinary thing. It was a practical man's answer to an emergency. Whatever else happened, he was saying, nobody in his diocese should feel that they had died alone and unheard.

The second record is a law. In June of 1349, when the plague had swept through much of England, the king and his council issued what's called the Ordinance of Labourers, dated the eighteenth of June. It begins, in a widely used English translation, by explaining why it was needed: "a great part of the people, and especially of workmen and servants, late died of the pestilence." And because so many workers had died, it goes on, those who were left had begun to refuse to work unless they were paid excessive wages. So the Ordinance tried to turn back the clock. It commanded that every able-bodied man and woman without land of their own must work for anyone who needed them, and must take only the wages that had been customary in the twentieth year of the king's reign, that is, in 1346 and 1347, or in the five or six ordinary years before.

It didn't work. Two years later, in 1351, Parliament passed a stronger law, the Statute of Labourers, which complained that the servants and labourers were still ignoring the Ordinance, and still demanding more. Within a generation, wages in England had risen whatever the law said.

And there's a chronicler who saw it happen. His name was Henry Knighton, a canon of the abbey of Saint Mary of the Meadows, in Leicester, in the English Midlands, and he wrote a chronicle of his times, probably in the 1380s and 1390s. He describes the plague year in England, and among other things he describes its strange effect on the countryside. I'll paraphrase him. He says that sheep and cattle wandered through the fields and among the crops, because there was no one to look after them, and that many of them died in ditches and hedges for lack of herdsmen. He says that so few labourers were left that the harvest could hardly be gathered, and that the workers who remained demanded much higher wages, and got them. Knighton was interested in money and prices, and he recorded them carefully, so that his chronicle is one of our best sources for what happened to ordinary working people in the years after the plague.

Let's set that beside our own world. In 2021 and 2022, after the worst of the pandemic, many countries saw something that economists called a labour shortage. Workers left their jobs, or changed them, or demanded better pay and conditions, and in many places, for a while, they got them. Newspapers wrote about the Great Resignation. Employers complained. Governments worried. It is a pale and gentle echo of 1349. But it rhymes. When a great many people are suddenly gone, or unwilling to work as before, the ones who remain find that their labour is worth more.

And here's the disagreement for this hour. Was the Black Death, in the end, good for the people who survived it?

For a long time, many historians told the story of the late fourteenth and fifteenth centuries in England as a kind of golden age of the labourer. With so many dead, the survivors could command higher wages, cheaper land, better food, and more freedom. The old system of serfdom, in which peasants were bound to their lord's land and owed him labour, began to crumble. And yet the story isn't simple. The Ordinance and the Statute show us how hard the lords and the government fought to hold wages down, and in 1381 that conflict boiled over into the great uprising called the Peasants' Revolt. The historian Samuel Cohn, comparing labour laws across Europe after the plague, has argued that governments everywhere reacted with fear and repression, trying to control the workers who survived. Other historians, including Mark Bailey, in his book After the Black Death, published in 2021, have argued that in England the change was faster and more far-reaching than many had thought, that the old manorial system was deeply transformed within a generation or so, and that ordinary people gained real and lasting benefits. Most historians would agree that, on average, the survivors and their children lived better than their grandparents had. How quickly, how widely, and at what cost, is still being argued.

Niccolò, of course, knows none of this. He's not thinking about England, or labourers, or laws. He's thinking about Cecco, and the grape harvest. And Lapa, beside him, says something that has been on her mind. She says that when the summer is over, Cecco will want a better share. That the farm will need more hands than his sons can give it, and that there will be fewer hands to hire. That they'll have to be generous.

Niccolò thinks about it, and says that Cecco can have whatever he asks. He says it lightly. But he means it.

In Tuscany, as it happens, just that kind of bargaining took place in the years after 1348. With so many people gone, landowners found that good farming families were scarce, and the sharecroppers who survived could often negotiate better terms, or move to a better farm. The great mortality reshaped the countryside of Tuscany, slowly, just as it reshaped the fields of England.

Outside, the moon is still climbing, slowly, over the hills to the south-east of the city, and the shadows of the cypresses lie long across the terraces. It's past midnight. Up and down the valley, in villas and farmhouses and little hill villages, people are sleeping, or lying awake, or watching over someone, the way people do in every hard summer. And in a great many of those houses, I think, someone has left a little fire burning, with a handful of herbs on it, for the same reason Lapa has.

Lapa stands, and stretches, and says that she's going to look at the fire one more time before she sleeps. Niccolò says he'll come up soon. He stays at the table a moment longer, alone in the lamplight, with his hands folded. Then he turns down the wick of the lamp until the flame is a tiny blue bead, and follows her.

Before he goes, he looks for a moment at the little painting in the corner. It was made in Florence, in a workshop near the cathedral, by one of the many painters who supplied small devotional panels like it to merchant households: tempera on wood, with a background of real gold leaf, beaten so thin that a breath would lift it. The finest painter of such panels in the city, in the years before this summer, was a man named Bernardo Daddi, whose gentle, glowing Madonnas hung in churches and houses all over Florence. Daddi is thought to have died this very year, in 1348. In Siena, the brothers Pietro and Ambrogio Lorenzetti, two of the greatest painters of the age, disappear from the records at about the same time, and most historians believe that the plague took them both. Ambrogio had painted, a decade earlier, the great frescoes of Good and Bad Government in Siena's town hall, with their famous picture of a peaceful city and its happy countryside. They are still there today, on the walls, and you can stand in front of them. The painters are gone. The pictures of a good, calm, ordinary world are not.

And the great room is dark, except for the faint gold of the little painted Virgin in the corner, catching the very last of the light.

## Part Ten — Around One in the Morning: Parchment, Fleas and Forty Days

Here is the small question for this hour of the night, and it's the question underneath the whole of this story. Why, in the end, did this family survive, when so many others did not?

Let's go down to the kitchen, where the fire is, to think about it.

Imagine that you are sitting on the low stool by the hearth now, where Nuta sat earlier. Nuta has gone to bed, on her pallet in the little room behind the kitchen, and you can hear her breathing through the open doorway, slow and deep. The house is quiet. The moon has climbed round toward the south, though in midsummer it never rises very high, and its light comes in through the small window under the roof and lies in a pale slanting shape on the far wall. The fire on the hearth has burned down to a low bed of red coals, with a fringe of grey ash around it, and a last few twigs of juniper glowing at the edges like tiny orange threads. The sweet smoke is thinner now, a faint blue haze rather than a white plume.

And then Lapa comes in, quietly, barefoot, in her linen shift, with her hair down, carrying no light. She knows the way in the dark. She kneels beside you on the warm brick, takes a handful of rosemary from the jar, and a few more twigs of juniper, and lays them on the coals. She blows gently. The twigs catch with a soft crackle, and a new thread of fragrant smoke curls up toward the beams.

She sits back on her heels and watches it. She does this twice every night. She's done it every night since April.

So, why did they survive? Let's take it slowly, because the answer has several parts, and they're all true at once.

The first part is timing. They left early. In the first week of April, when the sickness was only beginning to spread through the city, Lapa and Niccolò made up their minds, and within a few days, they were gone. Many people who fled later, in May or June, carried the sickness with them without knowing it. Leaving early, before the worst, was perhaps the single most important choice they made.

The second part is distance and separation. They didn't just leave the city. They shut the gate, and kept it shut, and let almost nothing and no one through. The basket on the ledge. The coins in vinegar. The letter left in the open air. Cecco's family on their side of the wall, talking across it. It was a strict, lonely, disciplined way to live, for months on end, and it worked, in part, because they were rich enough to afford it, and because they had a farm around them that could feed them.

The third part is the bales. Niccolò's wool, and every bale of cloth and bundle of goods in his warehouse in the city, stayed exactly where it was. Nothing from the outside world came into this house in quantity. No old clothes. No secondhand bedding. No bales from the port.

The fourth part is cleanliness, of a kind. The washed hands at table. The linen, washed every week in the trough and dried in the hot sun. The swept floors. The household kept itself, by the standards of its time, very clean.

And the fifth part is luck. Simple, undeserved luck. That no flea came in on the dog. That no sick traveller came to the gate. That the sickness, which did come into the countryside around Florence this year, as Boccaccio tells us, didn't come up this particular hillside, to this particular farm. Many families who did everything this family did were not so fortunate. And some who did very little were spared. Nobody in 1348 could have told you why.

That's the answer to our small question. They survived through early flight, strict separation, unopened bales, clean linen, and a great deal of luck.

And now I want to tell you what science has learned, in our own time, that helps explain why some of those choices may have mattered more than the people who made them could know.

As we heard earlier, the bacterium of the Black Death was Yersinia pestis, the plague. For most of the twentieth century, the textbook story of how it spread was about rats. Black rats, living in houses and ships and granaries, carried fleas, and when the rats died of plague, their hungry fleas jumped to humans and bit them. That's how plague spread in the outbreaks studied by scientists in India and elsewhere around 1900, and it was assumed that the Black Death worked the same way.

But some researchers began to wonder whether that could explain the speed of the medieval pandemic, and its pattern within households. And in 2018, a team led by Katharine Dean, together with Boris Schmid, Nils Christian Stenseth and other colleagues at the University of Oslo, published a study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, using records of deaths from nine outbreaks of plague in Europe during the centuries of what's called the Second Pandemic, which began with the Black Death. They built mathematical models of three ways plague might spread: by rats and their fleas, by pneumonic plague passing directly between people, and by human parasites, fleas and body lice living on people themselves and in their clothes and bedding, passing between people who lived close together. And they found that the model with human parasites fitted the patterns of deaths in most of those outbreaks better than the other two.

If they were right, then it's no surprise that plague moved so readily within households and between neighbours, and along the roads with travellers and their packs, and in old clothes and bedding. And then some of this family's choices look wiser than they knew. The gate, keeping strangers and their clothing away. The bales, unopened. The linen, washed every week and dried in the sun. The washed hands. Lapa believed she was guarding against bad air. She may in fact have been guarding, without knowing it, against lice and fleas.

And here is this hour's disagreement, because not everyone agrees with that study, by any means. Ole Benedictow, whom we met at the start of the night, has long and vigorously defended the role of rats and their fleas in the Black Death, and has argued that the human-parasite theory doesn't fit the evidence of how plague behaves. Other scientists have pointed out that the models depend on the quality of a few sets of historical death records, and that different outbreaks may have spread in different ways. Many researchers now think that more than one route was at work, rats and their fleas in some places, human fleas and lice in others, and pneumonic spread in some seasons, and that the balance between them is still being worked out. It's a real and lively scientific argument, and it's far from over.

Now, there's one more voice from 1349 that I want you to hear, before this part of the night is done, because it's the voice of someone who did not know whether anyone would survive, and wrote for them anyway.

In the town of Kilkenny, in Ireland, there was a Franciscan friar named John Clynn, who kept a chronicle, the Annals of Ireland. When the plague reached Kilkenny, he wrote about it, and at the end of his account, he did something extraordinary. I'll paraphrase him, from an older English translation, because I haven't been able to confirm the wording of the modern one. He wrote that he was leaving parchment, so that the work could be continued, in case any man should survive, and anyone of the race of Adam should escape this pestilence and continue the work he had begun. A little after his last entries, in the summer of 1349, a different hand added a note that it seemed the author had died. Whether he died of the plague, we don't know for certain.

But think of it. A man, in a small stone room in Ireland, writing in the deepest fear, and leaving blank pages behind him. For whoever might come after. For us.

And we did come after. That's the thing to hold on to. We are the ones he left the parchment for.

Clynn's town of Kilkenny was a long way from Florence, at the very western edge of the Christian world as anyone in this house would have imagined it. And yet the same sickness reached it within about a year of reaching Tuscany, carried, like everything else in this story, by the ships and roads of trade. Clynn's chronicle survives in copies, and it was printed in the nineteenth century, and a modern edition and translation was published by the historian Bernadette Williams in 2007. So the parchment he left was, in a sense, picked up. His words were continued, copied, edited, translated, and read, by people he could never have imagined, in a world he could not have pictured.

Lapa, kneeling here by her little fire, knows nothing about Ireland. She has heard of it, perhaps, as a cold green island somewhere beyond England, at the edge of the map. But she would understand John Clynn very well, I think. Every night, when she lays the herbs on the coals, she is doing something like what he did with his blank pages. Not for herself, exactly. For her children. For the morning. For whoever comes after.

There's one more true thing that the science of our own time has added to this story, and it's a strange and rather wonderful one. The plague bacteria that geneticists have recovered from Black Death burials, in London and elsewhere, have taught them a great deal about how plague has changed over the centuries. The strain of 1348 was, in a sense, the ancestor of many of the plague strains that still exist in the world. And plague has not disappeared. It still lives among wild rodents in parts of Asia, Africa and the Americas, and still occasionally infects people. But today, caught early, it can be cured with ordinary antibiotics, in a matter of days. The sickness that emptied half of Florence, in Lapa's summer, is now something a doctor in a modern hospital can treat with a course of tablets.

Let's set all this beside your own world, as we've done all night. In 2020, many of us were asked to isolate for a set number of days if we'd been exposed to the sickness, or arrived from abroad. Ten days, or fourteen. Some countries built special quarantine hotels at airports, where travellers stayed in a room for two weeks before being allowed out into the world. We grumbled. We counted the days on calendars. We had food delivered to the door. And we called it, all of us, in every language, by an old Italian name.

And that brings us, at last, to our third mystery.

So here, at last, is the answer to the third of our three mysteries. I asked you at the very beginning of the night: why does the English word for keeping travellers apart, to keep a sickness out, come from the Italian word for the number forty?

The answer comes from a little city on the far side of the Adriatic Sea, and it comes from a generation after our night, so I want to be clear about the date. The city was Ragusa, which we now call Dubrovnik, on the coast of Croatia, a small, rich, careful republic of merchants and sailors, which traded with Venice and with the whole of the eastern Mediterranean. The plague came back again and again in the decades after 1348. And on the twenty-seventh of July, in the year 1377, twenty-nine years after this night, the Great Council of Ragusa passed a new law. People coming from places where there was plague were not to enter the city or its territory until they had first spent a month in isolation, on the little island of Mrkan, just off the coast, or in the small town of Cavtat, a few miles to the south. A month. Thirty days. In the language of the time, a trentino, a period of thirty.

Then, in 1397, the period was lengthened, and the number became forty days. In Italian, forty is quaranta, and a period of forty days, quarantena, or in the dialect of Venice, quarantina. And that word, carried by sailors and merchants and officials from port to port across Europe, came into English as quarantine.

Why forty, rather than thirty? Nobody is entirely sure. Forty was an old and important number. Christ fasted forty days in the wilderness. Lent lasts forty days. And some physicians of the time, following ancient medical teaching, believed that an acute illness would declare itself within forty days. Historians still discuss which of those reasons mattered most. The records also show that officials could make the stay shorter or longer as they saw fit. But forty days is the number that stuck, and the word that came from it is the one we all used, in 2020, in a hundred languages.

So that's the third mystery. The word for keeping travellers apart comes from the Italian for forty because a small republic on the Adriatic, after long and hard experience of the plague, learned to make its visitors wait, first thirty days and then forty, before letting them in. Our family, behind their gate, are doing something like it by instinct, a generation early.

Lapa lays one last sprig of rosemary on the coals, and watches it curl and smoke. Then she stands, and brushes the ash from her knees, and looks for a moment at the little fire, glowing steadily in the dark kitchen. And she goes back up the stairs to bed.

## Part Eleven — Around Half Past Two: The Garden Asleep

Here is a small, gentle question for this hour of the night. Somewhere on these hills, Boccaccio placed the villa of his ten storytellers. Which one was it?

We'll come to that. But first, let's go out into the garden, very quietly, because the whole house is falling asleep.

Imagine that you are stepping out of the kitchen door, onto the gravel, into the deep of the night. The air has cooled at last. Not much. But enough that it feels soft on your face, like the inside of a sleeve, and you can breathe it deeply. The moon has swung round into the south, and hangs above the dark line of the hills beyond the city, round and pale, a little lopsided, and its light lies on everything like a thin layer of frost that isn't cold. The herb beds are silver. The box hedges are black velvet. The lilies are pale and closed. And the scent of the garden is stronger now than at any time of the day: rosemary, sage, lavender, the sweetness of a jasmine growing over the wall by the gate, and, threaded through all of it, very faint, the juniper smoke from the kitchen fire.

The crickets are quieter now. The nightingale has stopped. Even the owl up the hill has gone silent. And in the stillness you can hear, very clearly, the small sounds of the house behind you, as one by one, the people in it let go of the day.

Nuta is the first. She's been asleep for a while, on her pallet behind the kitchen, her arm under her head, breathing slow and even. She's nineteen. She works from before dawn until after dark, and she sleeps the moment she lies down, the way young people who work hard always have. She's dreaming, perhaps, of her mother's house, in a village in the Mugello, the green valley north of here, where her family is, and where, the last she heard, all of them were well.

Then Meo. Up in the children's room, he has been lying awake, as boys of fourteen do, thinking about his little account book, and the sea voyage story he means to tell tomorrow, and the workshop by the river, and whether his father will let him go there when they go home. And then, between one thought and the next, he isn't thinking at all. His hand, which was resting on his chest, slides down onto the sheet. He is asleep.

Let's stand by the well a moment longer, in the moonlight, and come to our gentle question.

In the introduction to the third day of the Decameron, Boccaccio moves his storytellers from the first villa they stayed in to a second, a little way off, and describes its garden. I'll paraphrase his description, because it's long and I'd like to keep it soft. He describes a garden enclosed by a wall, with broad straight walks shaded by pergolas of vines, just then flowering and filling the air with their scent, and beds of roses, red and white, and jasmine, and a great lawn of grass so green it seemed almost dark, starred with flowers, and in the middle of the lawn, a fountain of white marble, carved with wonderful skill, with a figure at its top throwing a jet of water high into the air, falling back with a lovely sound into the basin, and then running away through little channels to water the whole garden. There are hares and rabbits and young deer in it, he says, wandering quite tamely. And the storytellers are so delighted by it that they say that if paradise were to be made on earth, they could not imagine any other form for it than this garden.

Our garden tonight is a much humbler thing. No marble fountain, no deer. Just herbs and lilies and a fig tree and a well. But it isn't hard, standing here in the moonlight, to understand why Boccaccio set his paradise on these slopes.

So, which villa was it?

And here is this hour's disagreement, and it's the gentlest one of the whole night.

People have been trying to answer that question for centuries. Boccaccio says that the first villa stood on a little hill, a short distance from the city, a couple of miles or so, away from the main roads, with a beautiful courtyard and loggias and rooms, and gardens and meadows and wells of cool water around it. The second is a short walk away. But he never names either one. And his descriptions, lovely as they are, are general enough to fit a great many of the villas on these hills.

So local tradition, and generations of scholars and guidebook writers, have offered their own answers. For a long time, it was commonly said that the first villa was the one now called Poggio Gherardo, on a hill on the road toward Settignano, and that the garden of the third day belonged to the Villa Palmieri, on the slopes below Fiesole. Other writers reversed the order, or put forward other houses. The beautiful Villa Schifanoia, also on this side of the city, has been linked to the storytellers too, and so have several others. And most careful modern scholars would say, quite simply, that Boccaccio's descriptions are too general to identify any one villa securely, and that he may well have been imagining an ideal villa rather than describing a real one. The traditions are lovely. The proof is lacking. And so the question has been left, very happily, open.

Boccaccio knew these slopes well, which is one reason people have always wanted to find his villa here. His family is thought to have owned a small property near Settignano, a village on the next hill along from Fiesole, and he spent part of his youth around here. A few years before this summer, perhaps around 1344 to 1346, he wrote a long, sweet poem in Italian called the Ninfale fiesolano, the Nymph Tale of Fiesole. It tells the story of a young shepherd named Africo, who falls in love with a nymph named Mensola, one of the huntress-nymphs of the goddess Diana who were said to live in the woods on these hills, long ago. It's a sad and tender story, and at the end of it, the two young lovers are turned into the two little streams that run down the slopes below Fiesole, which are still called the Africo and the Mensola today. So the very water that runs past these villas, in Boccaccio's imagination, is made of a love story. If you listen very carefully tonight, below the garden wall, you might just hear one of them, running thin and quiet in its summer bed, over the stones, down toward the Arno.

I rather like that. It means that every old villa on these hills, on a moonlit night, can be the storytellers' villa, a little. Including this one.

Let's put that beside your world for a moment. Today, if you travel to Tuscany, you can stay in an old farmhouse very like this one. Many of them have been restored and turned into holiday houses, with swimming pools where the threshing floors used to be, and wifi in the old pigeon towers, and a view down over the olive groves to Florence that hasn't changed very much in seven hundred years. People go there, as Boccaccio's storytellers did, to rest, to eat simple food, to walk in gardens, to sit on a loggia in the evening and talk. They are on holiday, not in flight. But the instinct, to leave the crowded city for a while and go up into the hills for peace, is exactly the same one.

Look up, while you're out here. Even with the moon so bright, a few of the brightest stars are shining, high in the western half of the sky. The highest of them is Deneb, at the tail of the Swan, which flies down the line of the Milky Way. A little lower, toward the west, is a brilliant blue-white star that we call Vega. And lower still, toward the south-west, is Altair, in the Eagle. Together, those three make a great triangle that stands overhead in the middle of summer nights, and that is now, in the small hours, tilting slowly down toward the west, as it has over these hills every summer since long before Fiesole was built. I checked their positions for this hour with the same astronomical model I used for the moon. Some of their names came into Europe from Arabic astronomy, through translations of Arabic books, during the centuries before this one. Vega, for example, comes from an Arabic phrase for a swooping eagle. They're the same stars you can see from your own garden, on a clear summer night, if you look up.

Back in the house, Piero is asleep. He's been asleep, really, since he came down from the loggia, but he's been tossing and turning and talking about lizards. Now he has gone very still. He is lying on his back, with his arms flung out above his head, the way small children sleep when they are utterly gone, and his mouth is a little open. The dog, Moro, who was not supposed to come upstairs, is curled up at the foot of the bed, his nose under his tail.

And Caterina, beside him, who couldn't sleep, and who had rosewater on her wrists, has turned onto her side toward the window, toward the faint pale glow of the moonlit sky, and her eyes, which have been open a long time, have closed. She was thinking about the fire, and how she'll help to keep it burning tomorrow. And now she's asleep.

There's one more true thing I'd like you to know, a small, comfortable one, while the house goes quiet. The great Florentine villas that grew from farms like this one became, in the next century, some of the most admired houses in Europe. Wealthy Florentines wrote about the pleasures of country life, about the healthy air of the hills, about walking among their vines and olives, about the joy of a garden. They built loggias and planted gardens and laid out walks, and some of the ideas they shaped on these hillsides, about how a house and a garden and a landscape could be made together into a single calm and beautiful place, spread all over the world. Every English country house with a view, every garden with a terrace and a fountain, every porch where someone sits on a summer evening looking out over their land, owes a little to the villas of these hills.

Nanni, in the little bed beside his parents', has never been awake at all, except for a moment when Nuta carried him down from the loggia. He's three. He sleeps the deep, damp, total sleep of the very young, with his cheeks flushed and one hand curled against his chin.

Up at the top of the tower, Monna Tessa has finished her prayers. She has said them every night of her life, and tonight she said them more slowly than usual: for her husband, for her son and his wife, for each of her grandchildren by name, for Nuta, for Cecco and Bice and their sons across the wall, for Ser Bindo, for the city. She tucks her wooden beads under her pillow, where they always go. She lies on her side, facing the little window, from which, by day, she can see the cathedral. And she closes her eyes. Monna Tessa is asleep.

Then Niccolò. He came up the stairs a while ago, and lay down beside his wife, and lay awake, with his hands folded on his chest, the letter under his pillow, thinking about bales and harvests and Ser Bindo's underlined words. The mortality, Bindo wrote, has begun to ease. He says it over to himself, the way you might hold a smooth stone in your hand. Begun to ease. And somewhere in the middle of saying it, he falls asleep.

That leaves only Lapa.

She's lying on her back, listening, as she has listened every night this summer. She can hear them all. Nanni's quick breath beside the bed. Niccolò's slow breath beside her. Through the wall, the children, and the dog. Above, the faint creak of the tower floor as Monna Tessa turns over. Below, Nuta, behind the kitchen. And under all of it, so faint that it's more a feeling than a sound, the tiny crackle of the fire on the kitchen hearth.

She counts them, one by one. All here. All well.

And Lapa is asleep.

Out in the garden, you're the only one awake now. The moon is beginning its long slow fall toward the west. The stars are wheeling, very slowly, above the tower. A breath of cooler air comes down the hill from Fiesole and stirs the leaves of the fig tree, and then is still. The whole hillside sleeps, under the moon. And in the kitchen, the fire sleeps too, low and red and faintly fragrant, waiting for morning.

## Part Twelve — Around a Quarter Past Four in the Morning: The Fire at Dawn

The last small question of our night is the simplest of all. On a summer morning in this house, what is the very first thing anyone does?

Let's wait and see.

The night is ending. The moon has crossed the sky and is sinking now toward the hills in the west, pale and thin-looking, like a coin worn smooth. In the east, beyond Fiesole, the sky has begun to change. First it went from black to a deep, dark blue. Then a band of grey-green light appeared low along the ridge. And now that band is turning, very slowly, to a soft, clear apricot gold. The stars are going out one by one, the faint ones first, then the brighter ones, until only a few are left, and then only one, low in the east, very bright, shining alone above the hill.

In the olive trees, the first bird wakes. Just a few notes, tentative, as if it's testing the air. Then another answers it. Then a blackbird, from the laurel by the wall, begins to sing properly, a rich, slow, unhurried song. And within a few minutes, the whole hillside is singing.

Inside the house, everyone is still asleep. Meo is sprawled across most of the bed. Piero has somehow ended up with his feet on the pillow. Caterina is curled up small. The dog has moved from the foot of the bed to the middle of it. Nanni is a warm little bundle, one fist against his mouth. Monna Tessa is exactly where she was, on her side, facing her window, her beads under her pillow. Niccolò is breathing slowly, the letter under his head.

And Lapa opens her eyes.

She doesn't need the birds to wake her. She has woken at this grey hour every morning this summer. She lies still for a moment, listening, as she did last night. Everyone breathing. Everyone here. Then she slips out of bed, pulls a light gown over her linen shift, and goes, barefoot and quiet, down the stone stairs, through the great room, where the little painted Virgin is beginning to glow again in the first faint light, and down the two worn steps into the kitchen.

That's the answer to our last small question. The very first thing anyone does, on a summer morning in this house, is see to the fire.

Imagine you are kneeling beside her on the warm brick, in the grey half-light. The kitchen is cool now, cooler than at any time since yesterday. The small high window under the roof has turned from black to pale grey. And there on the hearth, in front of you, is the fire. All night long, through the stories and the letter and the moonlight and the sleeping, it has been here. It's very low now. A small heap of coals, glowing red at the heart, grey with ash at the edges, and a faint, last thread of smoke rising from it, carrying the very last breath of juniper and rosemary.

Lapa takes the little iron poker and draws the coals together into a neat heap in the middle of the hearth. She blows on them, gently. They brighten, orange, then gold. Then she lays on a handful of small dry sticks, olive prunings, which burn hot and fast, and they catch at once with a soft crackle. And then, from the jar on the shelf, she takes a sprig of rosemary, and lays it on top.

The needles curl. A thin white plume of fragrant smoke rises, and curls up toward the beams, and drifts out through the door into the garden, where the birds are singing.

The fire is awake. And, one by one, so is the house.

Nuta first, of course, from her pallet behind the kitchen, yawning, pinning up her hair, reaching for the water jar. Then Monna Tessa, upstairs, who has heard the crackle of the sticks through two floors and a tower, and is already calling down to ask whether anyone has thought to fetch the water. Then the dog, who comes clattering down the stairs as if he's been let out of prison. Then Piero, who sits up and announces that he dreamed about a lizard as big as a mule. Then Caterina, who tells him that's silly. Then Meo, groaning. Then Nanni, who wakes up crying because everybody else is awake and he wasn't consulted. And then Niccolò, who comes down last, in his shirt, rubbing his face, and stands in the kitchen doorway, and looks at the little fire on the hearth, and at his wife kneeling beside it, and says nothing, and smiles.

The kitchen fills with the ordinary sounds of a morning. Nuta goes out to the well with the jar and comes back with it brimming and cold, and pours some into the copper pot to heat, and some into the basin for washing. The washing comes first, as it does every morning: faces, hands, the back of the neck, in cool water from the well, with a rough linen cloth. Piero protests, as he does every morning, and is washed anyway. There's bread from the basket, and the last of the cheese, and a few figs that Caterina has run out to pick from the tree on the south wall, the first really ripe ones of the year, purple and soft and split a little at the top, and warm already from the early sun. There's watered wine for the grown-ups, and milk for Nanni, which Cecco's family leaves on the ledge at the gate each morning, from their goat.

Monna Tessa comes down last of the women, fully dressed, her black veil pinned, her beads at her belt. She sits on the bench by the hearth and holds out her hands to the little fire, though the morning is already warm, because she has done it every morning of her life and sees no reason to stop now. She asks Lapa whether the fire kept well. Lapa says it did. Monna Tessa nods, as if she had personally arranged it.

Meo has his account book out again at the end of the table, and is adding up something with great concentration. Niccolò looks over his shoulder and sees that his son has been keeping a list, all summer, of every basket that has come through the hatch, and what was in it, and what it cost. It's very neat. Niccolò is more pleased by it than he can say.

Let's set this beside your own home, one last time. Perhaps your mornings begin with an alarm, or a phone, or a kettle that boils by itself while you stand and wait, or a coffee machine that started on a timer before you woke. Lapa's morning has none of that. But it has something very old and very simple at its heart. A fire, kept alive through the night, and woken with a breath. Every morning, the same.

And I want to tell you about one last disagreement, a very small and gentle one, about the name that we've used for this whole long night, the Black Death.

Nobody in this house would know what you meant by it. In 1348, people called this sickness by many names: the pestilence, the great mortality, the plague, the sickness. In Latin, writers often called it the great mortality, magna mortalitas. In Italy, it was la mortalità, or la moria, or simply la pestilenza. The name Black Death, as we use it, seems to have come much later. Scholars who have traced it find it in Scandinavian writings of the sixteenth century, and in a history of Denmark printed in 1631, and it was taken up in English in the nineteenth century, especially after a popular history book of 1823 used it. And people disagree about what the black meant. Some have thought it described the sickness itself. Others, perhaps more convincingly, have argued that it comes from an old Latin phrase, in which the word for black also meant dreadful, or terrible, or grim, and that later writers took it literally. Either way, the name is a later one. The people who lived through it, like Lapa and Niccolò and Monna Tessa, knew it simply as the great mortality, the year of the pestilence, the summer when they went up to the hill.

There's a last passage from Boccaccio I'd like you to have, because it belongs with this morning. At the end of the Decameron, after their hundred stories, the ten young storytellers decide that it's time to go home. They've been away about two weeks. They've rested, and eaten simply, and sung and danced and told their tales. And so, one morning, they set out together from the villa in the hills, and walk back down to Florence, and return to the church of Santa Maria Novella, where they first met, and there they take their leave of one another, kindly, and the young men go off about their business, and the women go home to their houses. I'm paraphrasing, but that's how it ends. Not with a great event. Just with ten people, rested and restored, walking home together down a hill in the morning.

Our family will not go home for a while yet. Not until Ser Bindo writes again, and then again, and says that it's truly safe. Not, perhaps, until the grape harvest is in, and the first cool mornings of autumn have come. But they will go home. Down the long white road, past the cypresses and the olives and the vines, and in through the city gate, to the house near the river, and to the workshop, and to whatever, and whoever, is waiting for them there.

Down in the yard, there's a clatter of a pail, and Cecco's voice calls out over the wall, cheerful and loud, good morning to the house, good morning, Monna Lapa, the sun's nearly up, and the figs on the south wall are turning. And Niccolò goes out into the garden, into the first gold light, and calls good morning back across the wall.

And the sun rises. Over the ridge beyond Fiesole, a little after half past four by the sun, the first sliver of it appears, very bright, so bright you can't look at it, and pours a flood of warm gold light down across the hillside. It lights the top of the tower first, and then the tiled roof, and then the loggia, and then the garden, the herb beds and the lilies and the fig tree and the well, and then, slowly, the whole long slope below, terrace after terrace, olive tree after olive tree. And far below, down in the valley, it lights the city. The red roofs, and the towers, and the great unfinished cathedral, and Giotto's bell tower in its scaffolding, and the long thin bridge over the river, all of it glowing, rose and gold, in the morning.

Lapa comes to the kitchen door and stands in the sunlight for a moment, shading her eyes, looking down at the city with her husband. Then she goes back in, to the hearth.

And inside, the fire burns on. Low and small and steady on the warm brick, just as it did at dusk last night, just as it will tonight, and every night, for as long as this summer lasts. The fire that was kept, and fed, and watched over, through the dark. Caterina comes and kneels beside her mother, and takes a sprig of rosemary from the jar, and lays it, very carefully, on the coals. And a thin white thread of sweet smoke rises from it, and curls up toward the old dark beams, and drifts out through the door, into the bright morning.

They have survived the night. They knew how. And now, so do you.

Thank you for spending this long summer night with me, and with Niccolò and Lapa, and Monna Tessa, and Meo and Caterina and Piero and little Nanni, and Nuta, and Cecco and Bice across the wall, in their quiet house on the hill below Fiesole. If this story helped you rest, or carried you off to sleep somewhere along the way, I'm truly glad. And if you'd like to come back for more nights like this one, you might consider liking this video and subscribing to the channel, gently, whenever you're ready. There's no hurry at all.

Next time, we'll travel to another city, and another century, and another long night. We'll go to London, in September of 1666, as the Great Fire burns, and we'll spend the night with an ordinary London family, and find out, slowly and calmly, how they could have survived it: what they carried, where they went, and how the diarist Samuel Pepys buried his wine and his Parmesan cheese in his garden for safekeeping. I hope you'll join me there.

Until then, sleep well. Keep cool. And may the small fire you keep, whatever it is, still be glowing in the morning.

## How the sources were checked

- **The family is fictional.** Niccolò, Lapa, Monna Tessa, Meo (Bartolomeo), Caterina, Piero, Nanni (Giovanni), the servant Nuta, the sharecroppers Cecco and Bice, the factor Ser Bindo, the dog Moro, and Ser Bindo's letter are all invented. The script says so in Part One, and says in Part Five that Bindo and his letter are invented. No invented person is presented as appearing in any record. Lapa's vinegar-washed coins and the gate ledge belong to the invented house, and Part Two says so.
- **The setting is real.** The hillside below Fiesole, above Florence, on the night of Monday 14 to Tuesday 15 July 1348 (Julian calendar), while the plague was in Florence (Boccaccio gives March to July 1348). The villa itself is invented. Part Eleven explains that Boccaccio's storytellers' villas cannot be securely identified.
- **How quotes were checked.** Direct page fetches were blocked in this session, so each quotation and record was checked through web-search results that show or summarise the text. The session's shared web-search limit was reached after about two dozen searches. Every item that was not confirmed is either paraphrased and flagged as a paraphrase in the narration, or listed below as "not re-verified this session" so it can be checked before publication. There are only five verbatim quotations: Boccaccio's opening sentence, Boccaccio's flowers-and-herbs passage, the Pistoia bell clause, Agnolo di Tura's "end of the world" sentence, and the preamble of the Ordinance of Labourers. Each one matched at least one published English translation. Translations differ, so check each against the edition named before publishing.
- **Left out or paraphrased because they could not be verified:** the Horrox wording of Bishop Ralph's letter (paraphrased); the Horrox wording of John Clynn (paraphrased from an older translation); Guy de Chauliac's "fear of infamy" passage (paraphrased via a modern retelling, Tuchman); Tommaso del Garbo's words (paraphrased); the Paris consilium's regimen chapters (no wording is quoted; only the causes section is summarised); the claim that Louis Heyligen's letter mentions the consecration of the Rhône (left out); the story that Giovanni Villani left a blank in his last chapter (left out); Agnolo di Tura's line about burying his children (left out on purpose for tone); Dante's Paradiso 15 lines on the Badia (paraphrased, not quoted); Boccaccio's descriptions of the villa and the Day 3 garden, and the Decameron's ending (paraphrased).
- **Computed, not quoted.** These were calculated with PyEphem for Fiesole (43.806° N, 11.293° E), using the Julian calendar, and converted from UTC to local mean solar time (UTC + 0:45). Sunset on 14 July 1348 was about 7:33 pm. Moonrise was about 9:35 pm, and the moon was waning gibbous, about 87% lit. The moon crossed the meridian at about 3:05 am at only about 35° altitude, which is why the script never calls it "high". Sunrise on 15 July was about 4:37 am. At about 2:30 am Deneb, Vega and Altair stood in the western half of the sky. The weekday (14 July 1348 Julian was a Monday) and the 1345 grouping of Saturn, Jupiter and Mars in Aquarius were checked the same way. The narration says the moon and the stars were worked out with a modern model.
