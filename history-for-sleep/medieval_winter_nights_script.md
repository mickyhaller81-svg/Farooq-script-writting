# How Medieval Peasants Survived the Coldest Winter Nights | History for Sleep

## Part One — Around Half Past Four in the Afternoon: The Last Light

The light is going now, and it is going early.

Over the long chalk hills of the Yorkshire Wolds, the January sky has turned the colour of pewter, and a low red line sits along the western edge of the world, as if someone had laid a single ember on the horizon and left it there to cool. The grass on the slopes is stiff with frost that never fully melted today. In the bottom of a narrow valley, where a small stream runs past a mill pond skinned with ice, there is a cluster of low roofs. They are thatched with straw, grey-gold and heavy, and they do not have chimneys. Instead, from each one, a thin blue thread of smoke seeps up through the thatch itself, as though the houses were quietly breathing.

Above them, on a little rise, stands a small stone church. A cow lows, low and patient, from inside one of the houses. And a woman in a hooded woollen cloak hurries back from the stream with a wooden bucket, her breath coming out in white clouds, her feet crunching on the frozen ruts of the lane.

This is the village of Wharram Percy. The year is 1340. And the night that is beginning right now, the twentieth of January, is one of the coldest nights of the year.

When most of us imagine the Middle Ages in winter, we imagine a great hall with tapestries, a roaring fireplace big enough to stand in, a lord in furs, knights coming in out of the snow. We imagine the castle.

But that is not where we are going tonight. Not the castle of the picture books. The great majority of people in medieval England lived in places like this, in single-storey houses of timber and earth and straw, with one fire in the middle of the floor and no glass in the windows. And it is one of those houses, and one of those families, that we are going to spend the whole night with, from this last smear of sunset until the grey light of morning.

I should tell you something honestly, before we go in. If you were taken from your own warm bed tonight and set down in this cottage in your nightclothes, you would very probably not know how to live through it. Not because the people here were stronger than us, but because they knew a hundred small things that we have forgotten. Without those things, on a night like this one, you would not have survived until morning.

So tonight, we are going to learn those things. Slowly. One at a time.

And while we do, I'd like you to carry three small mysteries with you, the way you might carry three smooth stones in a coat pocket.

The first is this. On the coldest night of the year, why would a careful mother, who loves her children, deliberately bury the family's only fire before she lies down to sleep?

The second. Why would a family who could not afford to lose anything at all choose to sleep only a few steps away from a large animal, under the same roof, breathing the same air, all night long?

And the third. Why might every person in this house wake up in the very middle of the night, in the deepest dark, and think nothing strange about it at all?

I'll answer each of them in its own time. The first a little before the evening is out. The second in the deep of the night. And the third when the house is very nearly asleep. There's no hurry. We have the whole night.

Now, before we lift the latch, I want to be clear with you about something, because it matters on this channel. The family you are about to meet is invented. Their names, their ages, their little habits and quarrels and kindnesses, I have made up. But almost everything around them is real. The village is real. Its church still stands. The kind of house they live in has been dug out of the ground and measured. And the laws they lived under, the records written about people like them, the poems and the account books and the court rolls, those are real, and I'll tell you the names and the dates as we go, so that you can look them up yourself, if you ever want to, on some wakeful afternoon.

So. Let's meet them.

The man coming up the lane behind the woman with the bucket is Robert. He's about thirty-six years old, which in this village makes him a solid, settled, middle-aged man. He holds what is called a bovate of land, sometimes called an oxgang, which in this part of England is the usual way of measuring a farmer's share of the great open fields. A bovate varied from place to place, but it was usually somewhere between ten and twenty acres, scattered in long strips across the fields that surround the village. It was enough, in a good year, to feed a family. In a bad year, it was not.

The woman with the bucket is his wife, Agnes. She's thirty-three. She spins wool almost every waking hour her hands are free, she brews ale when the household has barley to spare, and she has buried two babies in the churchyard on the rise, which is a thing that is not unusual here, and which she does not talk about.

Inside the house, sitting nearest to the fire, as she always does, is Robert's mother, Maud. She's sixty-one, a widow, with a face like a winter apple and hands that never stop working. She has lived in this house since she came here as a bride more than forty years ago.

Then there are the children. William is thirteen, tall and gangly, and old enough now to be counted among the men of the village for certain legal purposes, which he is very proud of. Alice is ten, quick and watchful, her mother's shadow. Thomas is six, and he is the one who barks back at the dog. And in a wooden cradle near the hearth, wrapped up tightly in linen and wool, is Joan, who is nine months old and has only just learned to pull herself up on the edge of the cradle and look out over the rim at the world.

Seven people. One room, more or less. One fire.

Here is a small question to begin with, one that we'll answer before this part is done. What, exactly, is this house made of? Because to understand how anyone survived a night like this, you first have to understand the walls that stood between them and the cold.

Imagine, now, that you are standing in the lane beside Agnes as she reaches the door. You're cold already. The air smells of frost and woodsmoke and, faintly, of animals. The door in front of you is made of rough planks, hung on wooden or iron hinges, and it's held shut with a simple latch, a wooden bar on the inside that lifts when you pull a leather thong threaded through a small hole. Agnes pulls the thong. The bar lifts. The door swings inward with a sigh.

And you step inside.

The first thing you notice is not the dark, though it is dark. It's the smoke. It hangs in a soft blue layer under the roof, a few feet above your head, gently drifting, filling the space between the rafters like a low cloud. It stings your eyes for a moment, and then, strangely, you stop noticing it. The second thing you notice is the warmth. It isn't a great warmth, not like stepping into a modern kitchen. But after the knife of the wind outside, it feels like being wrapped in a blanket. And the third thing you notice is the fire itself, right there in the middle of the floor, on a flat hearth of stones, a low heap of glowing wood and orange flame with nothing around it, no grate, no fireplace, no chimney. Just fire, sitting on the floor of the house like a living thing.

Your eyes adjust. You see that the walls are not straight and smooth. They're made of a timber frame, the great curved timbers called crucks rising from near the ground up to the peak of the roof like the ribs of an upturned boat, and between the timbers, panels of woven hazel or willow, called wattle, smeared thickly with a mixture of clay, earth, straw and dung, called daub. At Wharram Percy, the walls often sat on footings of chalk rubble, because chalk is what the Wolds are made of, and it was lying there in the fields for anyone to gather. The floor under your feet is beaten earth, hard as stone from years of feet, with rushes and straw strewn over it here and there. The roof above the smoke is thatch, and it is thick, a foot or more in places, and wonderfully good at keeping heat in.

That's the answer to our first small question. Chalk, timber, hazel, clay, dung, straw. Almost everything in this house came from within a walk of the door.

Now, a few words about how we know this.

For forty years, from 1950 until 1990, a historian named Maurice Beresford and an archaeologist named John Hurst came back to this very valley summer after summer with teams of volunteers, and slowly, carefully, dug up the deserted village of Wharram Percy. It became one of the most famous excavations in Europe. They found the outlines of roughly forty peasant houses and their outbuildings, along with two manor houses and the church of Saint Martin, which still has much of its twelfth-century stonework. The results filled thirteen published volumes. Today, if you visit, you'll see the grassy humps of house foundations in the turf, and the roofless church, and the pond, and almost nothing else. The village was almost entirely deserted by the early sixteenth century. But in 1340, it is alive, and it is full of smoke and voices.

And here is our first place where historians disagree.

When Beresford and Hurst first dug these houses, they found foundation after foundation laid on top of one another, walls shifting a little this way or that over the generations. For a while, that was read as a sign that peasant houses were flimsy things, thrown together by the families themselves, falling down and rebuilt every generation or so. It fitted an older picture of the medieval peasant as poor, ignorant, and hardly better housed than his animals. But later scholars, including Stuart Wrathmell, who edited several of the Wharram volumes, and the economic historian Christopher Dyer, came to argue something quite different. They argued that many of these houses were sturdy, carefully made timber buildings, often put up by skilled carpenters, built to last for decades. The overlapping foundations, in that reading, are not evidence of collapse, but of families rearranging and improving their homes over long lifetimes. The debate has shifted a long way since the first spade went into the ground, and it continues. But tonight, I'll lean towards the later view. Robert's house is old, it is solid, and it was here before he was born.

Let's look at it the way a modern visitor would. In your own home, the walls are probably built around insulation, fibreglass or foam, and your windows are double-glazed, and the warm air from your heating is kept in by a sealed envelope that engineers have spent a century perfecting. A modern thermostat is often set to around sixty-eight degrees Fahrenheit, or twenty degrees Celsius. Robert's house has no glass at all. Its windows are small openings with wooden shutters, closed now against the dark. The thatch and the thick daub do a remarkably good job of holding heat, but there are gaps everywhere, at the eaves, around the door, at the smoke-hole. And yet the house has one great advantage that yours does not. It has people in it, pressed close together. A resting human body gives off something like a hundred watts of heat, roughly the warmth of an old-fashioned light bulb. Seven people, in a small space, under a thick roof, are a little furnace all by themselves.

There's a poem I want to share with you before this first part ends. It was written about fifty years after our night, in the 1390s, by Geoffrey Chaucer, and it's called the Nun's Priest's Tale, one of the Canterbury Tales. It begins not in a castle at all, but in a cottage just like this one. Chaucer describes a poor widow, a little advanced in age, living in a small cottage beside a grove in a valley. She keeps three large sows, three cows, and a single sheep, which Chaucer tells us was called Malle. And then he gives us a line that I think about every time I picture a house like Robert's. In a modern-spelling version of the text, it reads: "Full sooty was her bower, and eke her hall, in which she ate full many a slender meal."

Her bower, meaning her private chamber, and her hall, meaning the main room, were both black with soot. Because that is what happens when you have a fire on the floor and no chimney. Every beam, every rafter, every underside of the thatch in Robert's house is coated in a soft, velvety black. It is not dirt, exactly. It is the record of every fire that has ever burned here, every winter evening, layer upon layer, going back before anyone in the room was born.

Agnes sets the bucket down by the door. Maud looks up from the fire and says something to her about the cold. Thomas is trying to get the dog to sit. Little Joan, in her cradle, has discovered her own foot.

Outside, the red line on the horizon has gone. The night has begun.

## Part Two — Around a Quarter Past Five: Rushes, Fat and Firelight

Here is the small question for this part of the night. When a candle costs more than you can easily spare, how do you light a room through sixteen hours of winter darkness?

Because that's what this family faces. At this time of year, in the north of England, the sun is gone by half past four in the afternoon and will not be properly back until around eight the next morning. That's more than fifteen hours of night. In your own home, you would barely notice. You'd reach for a switch on the wall, and the room would fill with light at once, and you would not think about it again until bedtime. Most of the people who have ever lived on this earth would find that single act, the flick of a switch, more astonishing than almost anything else about our world.

In Robert's house, the first and most important light is the fire itself. For most of the evening, the whole family will live inside the circle of its glow. That's where Maud sits, on a low bench, so close to the hearth that the hem of her skirt is in danger. That's where Agnes will spin, and where Alice will help her, and where Robert will mend things with his knife. Beyond the edges of that warm orange circle, the house fades into shadow, and then into true dark.

But firelight flickers, and it doesn't reach into corners, and sometimes you need to see something properly. For that, there is the rushlight.

Imagine you are sitting on the bench beside Alice now, close enough to feel the heat of the fire on one side of your face and the cold of the room on the other. In her lap is a bundle of rushes, gathered last summer from the wet ground down by the stream and dried in the rafters ever since. They are the common soft rush, long, green-grey, and as thick as a knitting needle. Alice takes one, and with her thumbnail, very carefully, she peels away the outer skin, leaving only a thin rib of skin running down one side to hold it together. What's left inside is the pith, white and spongy and soft, like a long thread of pale moss.

Her grandmother is watching. Maud did this as a girl, and her mother before her. Peel too much, and the pith breaks. Peel too little, and it won't drink the fat.

Then comes the fat. On the edge of the hearth sits a shallow pan, and in it, melting slowly, is a lump of grease saved from the cooking, bacon fat, or mutton fat if they have it, kept in a pot through the autumn. Alice draws the peeled rush through the warm grease, lets it cool in the air for a moment, and draws it through again. Now it's a long, stiff, glistening taper, almost the colour of honey.

When it's lit, it burns with a small, steady, yellow flame. Not bright. Not like a candle, even. But enough to see your hands by.

Now, we know the most about rushlights from a later writer, and I want to be honest about that. In 1789, an English country parson and naturalist named Gilbert White published a book called The Natural History and Antiquities of Selborne, about his village in Hampshire. In one of its letters he described, in loving detail, how the poor families of his parish made rushlights, much as Alice is making them now. He even timed one. A rushlight about twenty-eight and a half inches long, he reported, burned for fifty-seven minutes. And he wrote, simply, that "these rushes give a good clear light." That's four and a half centuries after our night, so I'm not pretending he saw Alice's rushes. But the method was ancient, and cheap, and the rush itself has not changed. The soft rush still grows in wet fields all over Britain today.

So, just under an hour of small yellow light, from a single rush that cost nothing but time and a little fat. That's the answer to our small question. You light the long winter dark with the fire, mostly. And when you need more, you make your own light, out of the summer, and the stream, and the bacon.

What about real candles? Candles existed, of course. A tallow candle, made from animal fat with a wick of twisted cotton or linen, gave a better light, but it smoked and smelled and guttered, and it cost money, or at least the fat of animals you might rather eat. The finest candles were made of beeswax. They burned clean and bright and sweet-smelling, and they were very expensive, and so most of them went to the church. The little church of Saint Martin up on the rise will have wax candles at its altar on feast days. Robert's house will not.

Let's pause there and think about your own home again. A typical modern LED bulb gives off roughly eight hundred lumens of light. A single rushlight, or a single candle, might give somewhere around a dozen. If you sat in Robert's house tonight with one rushlight burning, you would be sitting in something like one-sixtieth of the light of a single bulb in your kitchen. And yet you would be able to see. The human eye is a remarkable thing. Give it time in the dark and it opens itself up like a flower, and a single flame can feel like a lamp.

Scientists who study vision say that the eye needs around twenty to thirty minutes in darkness to adapt fully, as the light-sensitive rod cells in the retina slowly recover their strength. Nobody in this house knows that, of course. But every one of them has lived it, every winter evening of their lives. Step outside to the byre, and step back in, and for a little while the room seems black. Then, gradually, the faces come back, the cradle, the beams, the dog. The people of Robert's world lived far more of their lives in that half-light than we do, and their eyes, used to it, would have found a modern room at night almost painfully bright.

The rushlight, once lit, needs somewhere to sit. It can't simply be laid down. In later centuries, people used little iron stands with a pair of jaws, like pliers, to grip the rush at an angle. Robert's household has something simpler, a split stick pushed into a crack in a beam, and a small clay dish underneath to catch the drips. Fat that falls on the rushes on the floor is fat that might catch fire, and that is a danger we'll come back to before long.

Now, while Alice dips her rushes, Maud begins to talk, and what she talks about tonight is the weather.

She's sixty-one, as I said. Which means that when she was a woman in her thirties, with small children of her own, she lived through a time that everybody in this village over the age of thirty remembers, and nobody speaks about lightly. From about 1315 to 1317, it rained. Across northern Europe, summer after summer, the rain came down until the fields turned to bog and the grain rotted in the ear. Harvests failed, one after another. Historians call it the Great Famine, and it was one of the worst disasters in European memory. Maud remembers it as the years when the bread was grey and thin, and then there wasn't any.

She doesn't tell it as a frightening story. She tells it the way old people sometimes tell of hard times, as a measure of how good things are now. Look, she says. We have bread. We have ale. We have a cow in the byre and pottage on the fire. Be thankful.

And here we come to the next point where historians, and climate scientists, disagree. You may have heard of something called the Little Ice Age, a long cool period in the climate of the northern hemisphere, when winters in Europe could be bitterly cold and rivers froze over. Almost everyone agrees that it ended around the middle of the nineteenth century. But when did it begin? Here, the answers stretch across about four hundred years. Some researchers point to the years around 1300, when the warm, dependable summers that northern Europe had enjoyed for generations began to fail. A study led by the geologist Gifford Miller at the University of Colorado, using plants that had been frozen under Arctic ice caps, suggested a sudden onset between about 1275 and 1300. Others prefer a date around 1550, when the great valley glaciers of the Alps and Norway began to grow, and still others push it later. It depends partly on what you choose to measure. So whether Robert and Agnes are living at the very beginning of the Little Ice Age, or in a cold stretch that came before it, depends on which scientist you ask. What matters to them, tonight, is simpler. It's cold. Their parents said the winters were milder once. And the rushes need dipping.

Agnes has sat down now, beside the fire. She has her distaff tucked under one arm, a stick wound with a cloud of combed wool, and her spindle in the other hand, a slender stick with a round weight of stone or bone on it called a whorl. She sets the spindle turning against her thigh, and as it drops and spins, she draws out the wool between her fingers, and a thread appears, as if out of nothing. She can do this without looking. She can do it in the dark. She has done it while walking to the well, while minding the baby, while talking, while half asleep.

There's a passage in a great poem of this century that I want you to hear now, because it could have been written about Agnes. The poem is called Piers Plowman, by a writer we know as William Langland. He revised it several times, and in the latest version, which scholars call the C-text, probably from the 1380s, there's a passage about the people Langland thought deserved charity most of all. They are not the beggars at the church door, he says. They are the poor folk in cottages, charged with children, who pay the lord's rent, and turn whatever they can spin into house-rent, milk and meal. In Langland's Middle English, these are the "pore folk in cotes," "charged with childrene." And Langland describes their suffering in winter, the "wo in winter-tymes," and the waking at night, "wakynge on nyhtes," to rise and "rokke the cradle" when the child cries.

I'm giving you those phrases in fragments, because the spellings vary between manuscripts and editions, and I'd rather be exact about the pieces than careless about the whole. If you'd like the complete passage, it's in Passus Nine of the C-text, around lines seventy to ninety-seven, in Derek Pearsall's edition. But even in fragments, you can hear it, can't you? A woman by a fire, a cradle beside her, a spindle in her hand, getting up again and again in the cold dark to rock the baby back to sleep. Six hundred and fifty years ago, a poet looked at a house like this one and saw exactly what we are seeing now.

Little Joan, as if she'd heard, begins to fuss in her cradle. Without pausing her spindle, without even looking down, Agnes puts out one foot and rocks the cradle with her toe. Back and forth. Back and forth. The fussing stops.

Thomas is lying on his stomach on the floor near the hearth, chin in his hands, watching the flame of the first rushlight. He asks his grandmother why the rush burns and the bucket doesn't. Maud tells him that's a very good question and that he should ask the priest. William, who is thirteen and knows everything, tells him it's because of the fat. Thomas asks why the fat burns. William doesn't know. Maud laughs, a creaky, comfortable laugh, and pokes the fire with a stick, and a small fountain of sparks rises towards the soot-black thatch and winks out before it gets there.

That, too, is something Maud watches. Every spark. Every time. She has watched sparks rise towards that roof for forty years, and she has never once stopped watching.

It's about half past five now. Outside, the last of the dusk has drained away. The stars are out, sharp and many, because there is no other light anywhere in this valley, not a single lit window, not a lamp, nothing but the faint red glow leaking from the eaves of a dozen houses and the cold white light of the stars. The frost is thickening on the thatch.

One more small, strange, true thing about tonight's date, while we're looking at those stars. When Robert and Agnes call this the twentieth of January, they're using the old Julian calendar, the one introduced in the days of Julius Caesar. Over the centuries, that calendar had slowly drifted out of step with the sun, by about a day every hundred and twenty-eight years. By 1340 it was running roughly eight days behind the seasons. Most of Catholic Europe would correct it in 1582, under Pope Gregory the Thirteenth, but Britain and its colonies did not switch until 1752, when eleven days were simply dropped from the month of September. So by our reckoning, this frozen night would actually fall at the very end of January. It makes no difference to the cold. But I like knowing it. Even the calendar on Robert's wall, if he had one, would be telling a slightly different story from the stars.

And somewhere outside, in the yard, Robert is still working, because there are animals to see to, and the beasts must come home before the night truly closes in.

## Part Three — Around Six O'Clock: The Beasts Come Home

The small question for this hour is a farmer's question. If you had to choose one animal to pull your plough, and keep it alive through a winter like this one, would you choose a strong, slow ox, or a quick, clever horse? And why would the answer matter so much to a family like Robert's?

Hold that thought. We're going outside.

Imagine that you have pulled your borrowed cloak tight and stepped out of the door behind Robert, into the yard. The cold takes your breath. It's a dry, still cold, the kind that makes the inside of your nose prickle and your eyes water. The ground is iron hard. Above you, the stars are so thick that the sky looks dusted with frost, and the long pale band of the Milky Way runs right across it from one hill to the other, something most of us living under city lights have never properly seen. There is no moon yet. The only sounds are your own footsteps, the creak of Robert's boots, and from the dark shape of a lean-to against the side of the house, the slow, warm, grassy breathing of animals.

Every house in Wharram Percy sits in its own plot of land, called a toft, with a fenced yard and, behind it, a longer strip of garden ground called a croft, where Agnes grows leeks and cabbages and beans and onions in summer. Tonight the croft is bare, frozen ridges under starlight. In the yard there's a pile of firewood under a low thatched cover, a heap of dung waiting to go out on the fields in spring, and a few hurdles, woven panels of hazel, leaning against the fence.

Robert lifts a hurdle aside, and there she is. Her name is Brownie, or at least that's what Thomas calls her, and she is the most valuable thing this family owns. A brown cow, not large by modern standards, with a broad, soft nose and a winter coat grown thick and shaggy. In summer she gives milk, and from the milk Agnes makes butter and cheese, which are some of the most precious foods the family has. She'll calve again in the spring, if all goes well. Beside her, half asleep, is last year's calf, a young heifer that might one day be sold, or kept.

Robert speaks to her softly, the way people who work with animals always do. He runs his hand along her neck. He checks that she has hay, and water that hasn't frozen solid in the trough. Then he leads her, slowly and patiently, through a separate doorway into the far end of the house itself.

We'll come back to that in the deep of the night, to why she sleeps under the same roof as the family. That's one of our three mysteries, and its time is not yet. For now, let's just notice it. Notice that the cow does not stay out in the cold.

Brownie is a cow, not an ox. Robert doesn't own a plough team of his own. Very few men with a single bovate did. A full plough team in the Middle Ages, on heavy soil, might be as many as eight oxen, yoked in pairs, and no ordinary peasant could afford eight oxen. Instead, Robert and his neighbours share. One man brings an ox, another brings two, another brings the plough itself, and together they plough each other's strips in turn. It's one of the quiet, essential cooperations of village life, and it means that the whole community is bound together, not only by the church and the lord, but by the yoke.

And that brings us to our small question, the ox and the horse.

There's a book on exactly this subject, written in about 1280, roughly sixty years before our night. It's called the Husbandry, and its author was a man named Walter of Henley. He wrote it in French, the language of the English upper classes at the time, as practical advice for running a manor estate. It was copied again and again, and an English translation by Elizabeth Lamond was published in 1890, with the original French on the facing pages.

Walter had firm opinions. In a famous passage, he compares the cost of ploughing with horses against ploughing with oxen, and he comes down on the side of the ox. I'll paraphrase him rather than quote, because the exact wording in the surviving manuscripts is not secure, and I'd rather not put words in his mouth. His argument goes roughly like this. A horse costs more to keep than an ox. It must be fed oats, not just hay and straw. It must be shod with iron, and the shoes must be renewed. And when the horse grows old and worn out, there is little left of it but its hide. But when the ox grows old, you can fatten it up on grass and eat it. So the ox, in Walter's reckoning, earns its keep twice: once in the field, and once on the table.

Historians have noticed that Walter exaggerated a little, particularly about the cost of shoeing, to make his point. But his argument made sense to the stewards of great estates, and for a long time, oxen did most of the heavy ploughing on lords' land.

Here, though, is the point where the historians disagree, or at least where they discovered something surprising. In 1986, a historian named John Langdon published a study called Horses, Oxen and Technological Innovation, drawing on large numbers of medieval records to count how many of each animal were actually being used. And what he found was that ordinary peasants did not follow Walter's advice at all. On the lords' estates, oxen still outnumbered horses by about two to one as late as the end of the fourteenth century. But among the peasants, by the late thirteenth century, something like half of their draught animals were already horses. The smallholders, in particular, were leading the change.

Why? Because a horse is versatile. It's faster. It can pull a plough, yes, but it can also pull a cart to market, or be ridden, or carry a load of wood along a frozen track. For a family with only one or two animals, an animal that could do many things was worth more than an animal that could do one thing well and then be eaten. The great lords had the luxury of specialisation. The peasant had to be practical. So the answer to our small question is not as simple as Walter thought. A rich man's steward might choose the ox. A poor man with a single animal might well choose the horse. And Robert, who has only his cow and a share in his neighbours' oxen, simply chooses to keep what he has alive until spring.

Let's think about that in modern terms for a moment. For many families today, the second most valuable thing they own, after their home, is their car. Imagine if your car needed feeding twice a day, could fall ill and die, could be stolen in the night, and could not be replaced for years if you lost it. Imagine if, without it, you could not grow your food. That's what a beast of burden was to a medieval peasant household. That is what Brownie is to Robert, even though she pulls no plough. She is the milk, the butter, the cheese, the next calf, and in the very worst year, a price at market that might buy enough grain to see them through.

There's a book that shows us this world better than almost any other, and I want to tell you about it while Robert finishes in the byre. It's called the Luttrell Psalter. It was made in England sometime around 1320 to 1340, which is to say, within living memory of our night, for a knight named Sir Geoffrey Luttrell, who was lord of the manor of Irnham, in Lincolnshire, not so very far south of here. A psalter is a book of the Psalms for private prayer, and most of its pages are filled with beautiful Latin text. But in the margins, the artists did something wonderful. They painted the ordinary people of the English countryside at work. There's a famous sequence of farming scenes, on the pages that scholars number as folios 169 verso to 173 verso, showing a year in the fields: men ploughing with a team of oxen, sowing seed, harrowing, reaping, carrying sheaves. Elsewhere in the book there are women spinning, and cooking, and people feeding animals. It's held today in the British Library in London, which acquired it, through the British Museum, in 1929, and you can look at its pages online.

When I picture Robert walking behind a plough team in the spring, I picture him as one of those small, sturdy, hooded figures in the margins of the Luttrell Psalter, leaning into the plough-handles, breath steaming, while a boy walks ahead with a goad to keep the oxen moving. That boy could be William, in a year or two.

Robert comes out of the byre end now and pulls the door to. He doesn't latch it from outside, because it's latched from within. Then he does one last round of the yard. He checks the woodpile. He looks up at the sky, the way farmers always have, reading the weather in the stars. Clear tonight. Hard frost. No snow. He carries in an armful of wood, split logs of ash and thorn and some broken branches of oak, and kicks the door shut behind him with his heel.

Inside, the warmth meets him like a hand on the shoulder.

There are other animals in this house, too, though they're small ones. A handful of hens has already found its way up to roost on a beam in the dark at the byre end, where they'll sit all night with their heads tucked under their wings, warm and fluffy and silent. The dog, who has no name in any record anywhere but whom Thomas calls Bran, flops down by the hearth with a groan of contentment. And hanging high up in the rafters, where the smoke is thickest, there are two long flitches of bacon, the salted sides of the pig that was killed in November.

That, too, is a true and ancient rhythm. In the medieval calendar, November was the month for killing the pig. You can see it in the carved and painted Labours of the Months on church walls and in manuscripts all over Europe: a man with an axe, a fat pig, a bowl for the blood. It made sense. By November, the pig had eaten its fill of acorns and beechmast in the autumn woods, and there would be no more free food for it until spring. Better to kill it, salt it, and hang it in the smoke, where the smoke would slowly cure it and keep the flies away. In February, when the stores ran low, those flitches would still be there. Some of the most important larders in medieval England were the rafters of smoky cottages.

William has taken his father's armful of wood and stacked it neatly beside the hearth, though not too close. He's learned that. Maud nods at him in approval. Thomas has gone to look at the hens, and Alice has told him to leave them alone. William is very conscious, these days, of being nearly a man. And in one specific, legal sense, he already is one. In much of medieval England, every male over the age of twelve was expected to be enrolled in a group called a tithing, originally a group of about ten men and boys who were held collectively responsible for one another's good behaviour. If one of them broke the law and ran, the others could be fined for failing to bring him in. This system was called frankpledge, and once or twice a year, at a special session of the manor court known as the view of frankpledge, the lord's steward checked that every man and boy who should be in a tithing was in one. It's in records of exactly this kind that historians find the names of thousands of ordinary medieval boys at the very moment they crossed from childhood into a kind of adulthood. William was sworn in last year. He stood with the men. He has not stopped mentioning it since.

Agnes is still spinning. Her thread grows longer on the spindle, round and round, in the firelight.

And Robert sits down at last, on the end of the bench, and stretches his frozen feet towards the hearth, and lets out the long, deep sigh of a man whose work, for today, is done.

It's a little after six. The beasts are home. The door is shut. And now, at last, it's nearly time for supper.

## Part Four — Around Seven O'Clock: Pottage, Ale and the Covering of the Fire

Here is this hour's small question, and it's a simple one. What is in the pot?

Because there has been a pot on the fire all this time, hanging from an iron hook on a chain from a beam, or perhaps resting on three short legs in the edge of the embers. A big, round-bellied pot of bronze or earthenware, blackened with use. And from it, for the last hour or more, a smell has been rising that has made Thomas ask, four times, whether it's ready yet.

Imagine you are sitting at the board now. That's what they call the table, a board, a set of planks laid across a pair of trestles, which can be taken down and leaned against the wall when it's not needed. That's where we get the old phrase "room and board". You're on a bench, shoulder to shoulder with Alice on one side and William on the other. In front of you is a wooden bowl, turned on a lathe from a single piece of ash or maple, and a wooden spoon. There's no fork. Forks will not come into ordinary English use for another three hundred years. You have your spoon, your fingers, and, if you're a grown man, your own knife, which you carry at your belt all day.

Agnes ladles out the pottage.

It's thick, more like porridge than soup. There are dried peas in it, and beans, saved from last summer, and oats to thicken it, and leeks from the croft, and an onion, and some cabbage leaves gone soft and sweet in the cooking. There are herbs from the garden, dried in bunches in the rafters. And tonight, because it is very cold and Robert has been working since before dawn, there's a little bacon in it too, cut from the flitch in the smoke above your head, so that small, salty, smoky pieces float in every spoonful.

That is pottage. That's the answer to our small question. For most English peasants, for most of the Middle Ages, it was the centre of almost every meal: a slow-cooked mix of grain and pulses and whatever vegetables the season allowed, kept simmering over the hearth, sometimes for days, with more added as it was eaten.

With it, there's bread. Not the soft, white wheaten bread of a lord's table, but a dense, dark, chewy loaf made of mixed grains. In many places, it was a mix of wheat and rye that people called maslin. Here on the Wolds, it might well have been mostly barley. And there's ale. Not the bitter, hoppy beer of a modern pub. Hops would not be widely used for brewing in England until the fifteenth century. This ale is thick, sweetish, cloudy, a little like liquid bread, brewed by Agnes herself a few days ago from malted barley. It doesn't keep for long, so it's drunk fresh.

The economic historian Christopher Dyer, in a well-known study published in 1988, looked at the records of what harvest workers on great estates were given to eat, over about two centuries. He found that in the thirteenth century, these workers' meals were overwhelmingly bread, with some cheese, and rather small amounts of ale, fish and meat. Two centuries later, bread mattered much less, and meat and ale made up a far bigger share of their diet. Barley and rye bread gave way to wheat. Bacon gave way to beef. Dyer was careful to say that harvest workers ate better than most peasants did on an ordinary day. But it gives us a window. In 1340, Robert's family is eating in the older pattern. Mostly grain, in bread and pottage. A little cheese and milk. Meat as a treat.

Ale and bread were so important that the law watched over both. Since the reign of Henry the Third, in the 1260s, a set of rules known as the Assize of Bread and Ale had fixed what bakers and brewers could charge, linked to the price of grain. In villages all over England, men were appointed as ale-tasters to check that ale was sold at a fair price and of good quality, and the brewers who broke the rules, many of whom were women like Agnes, turn up in their thousands in manor court records, paying small fines. So often, in fact, that historians suspect those fines worked more like a licence to brew than a punishment.

Supper doesn't take long. Nobody here eats slowly. Thomas scrapes his bowl with his spoon until Agnes takes it from him. The dog gets the last crust. The bowls are wiped out with a handful of straw and set aside.

And now, while the family settles back towards the hearth, I'd like to answer the first of our three mysteries. Because Maud is doing something that will lead us right to it.

She has reached behind her bench and brought out an old, cracked, earthenware lid, and set it down by the hearth, ready. And she has laid a short iron rake beside it, and a little pile of dry wood ash in a pot. Thomas, who has seen her do this every night of his life, asks her, as children do, why she has to put the fire to bed. Isn't it cold? Won't they freeze?

So here, at last, is the first question I asked you at the beginning of the night. On the coldest night of the year, why would a careful mother, who loves her children, deliberately bury the family's only fire before she lies down to sleep?

The answer has two halves. The first half is fear. And the second half is hope.

The fear first. This is a house built of timber, wattle, straw and dried rushes, with a roof of thatch a foot thick, and an open fire on the floor in the middle of it with nothing to contain it at all. While someone is awake, the fire is a friend. They're watching. They're watching the sparks, as Maud does, every single one. But once everyone is asleep, an untended open fire is the most dangerous thing in the whole village. A log rolls. A spark floats up into the dry thatch. An ember tumbles onto the rushes on the floor. A tallow drip catches. And a house that has stood for fifty years can be gone in minutes, with the family still inside it, and the flames can leap from one thatched roof to the next until a whole lane is burning.

We know that this happened, and we know it from records of a very particular kind. In medieval England, whenever someone died suddenly or by accident, a royal official called the coroner was supposed to hold an inquest, with a jury of local men, and to record what had happened. The office of coroner goes back to 1194. Their records, the coroners' rolls, survive for a number of counties, and they are among the most intimate documents we have about ordinary medieval life, because they describe, in plain words, exactly what people were doing at the moment something went wrong. For Bedfordshire, the rolls from about 1265 to 1317, and from 1378 to 1380, were translated and published in 1961 by the historian R. F. Hunnisett. And the American historian Barbara Hanawalt drew on coroners' rolls like these in her 1986 book The Ties That Bound, to reconstruct the everyday life of peasant families: where the men were working, where the women were, what the children were doing, and the accidents, including fires, that sometimes broke into those ordinary days.

That's the fear. And it's exactly why, at bedtime, the fire must be covered.

But here's the hope. You don't put the fire out. You put it to sleep. Maud will take the rake and draw the glowing embers together into a tight heap in the centre of the hearth stones. Then she'll bank them over with ash, burying them deep, and perhaps set the old earthenware lid over the top. Under that blanket of ash, starved of air, the embers will not die. They'll smoulder, very slowly, all night long, hardly burning at all, giving off a gentle, steady warmth through the hearth stones and the ash. And in the morning, the first person awake will rake the ash aside, and there they'll be, still glowing red, waiting. A handful of dry straw, a few twigs, a breath or two, and the fire will leap back to life.

That matters more than you might think. Lighting a fire from nothing in the Middle Ages was a slow, fiddly job, with a flint, a piece of steel, and a scrap of tinder, a charred cloth or dry fungus, that had to be kept bone dry. On a frozen morning, with cold fingers, it could take a long time. The alternative was to go next door, carrying a pot, and beg a few live coals from a neighbour. Better by far never to let the fire go out at all.

So this is why the careful mother buries the fire. Not to kill it, but to keep it safe, and to keep it alive. Banked, the fire can't escape into the thatch. Banked, it uses only a tiny fraction of the fuel. And banked, it will still be there, faithfully glowing, in the morning. It is, if you like, the family's promise to itself that there will be a tomorrow.

And the word for this, the act of covering the fire at night, gives us one of the most ordinary words in the English language. In Norman French, the phrase was couvre-feu, meaning "cover fire". In English, it became curfew. Originally, a curfew was simply the time, usually marked by the ringing of a bell, at which households were expected to cover their fires for the night. In some places, the domed covers used for this were themselves called curfews.

And here, too, there's a disagreement among historians.

There is a famous and very old story that the curfew bell in England was introduced by William the Conqueror, soon after 1066, as a way of controlling the conquered English, forcing them to put out their fires and lights at eight o'clock in the evening so that they couldn't gather in the dark and plot against their new Norman rulers. The Elizabethan historian John Stow, in his Survey of London, published in 1598, recorded the tradition of a decree that a bell should be rung nightly at eight o'clock in every town and village. And you'll still find the story told confidently in books and online today. But many historians have long been sceptical. As far back as 1839, the historian Thomas Keightley pointed out that the attribution to William appears only in later chroniclers, not in anything written at the time. Others have argued that the custom of ringing a bell to cover fires is likely to be much older than the Conquest, and that it was simply about preventing fires, not about suppressing rebellion. The truth is that we don't have the contemporary evidence to be sure. It's one of those stories that is too good not to be told, and too thin to be trusted.

What we can say is that in the villages of 1340, with or without a bell, every household knew that the last task of the evening was to cover the fire. In some places, church bells did ring at nightfall. Whether the little bell of Saint Martin's will ring tonight, nobody in this house will particularly notice. They know when it's time, the way they know when the cow needs milking.

Now, let's set that against your own home for a moment. Your house has a smoke alarm, probably more than one, on the ceiling, with a little light that blinks green to tell you it's awake. Your heating system has a thermostat that turns the furnace on and off by itself, all night, without anyone watching it. Your stove has a knob that turns the gas off, or a switch for the electric ring. All of that is, in a sense, a machine for doing what Maud does with her rake and her ash. Keeping fire useful, and keeping it from killing you, while you sleep.

Maud isn't covering the fire yet. It's far too early for that. There's still work to be done tonight, and talk to be had, and the fire has more to give. But the lid is there by the hearth, and the rake, and the ash. Everybody in this house knows that before they lie down, the fire will be put to bed. The first mystery is solved.

And the fire, uncovered for now, burns on, low and orange and steady, in the middle of the floor.

Robert leans forward and lays another split log of ash across the fire, carefully, crosswise, so that it won't roll. Ash is the wood everyone wants. It burns well even when it's not fully seasoned, with a clear, steady flame and very little smoke, and countryfolk all over England have praised it for centuries. Thorn burns hot and fierce. Oak burns long and slow, if it's dry, but it must be dry. Each family in the village knows its woods the way a modern family knows the settings on a stove. The new log catches. A small new flame runs along its underside, blue at the root, then yellow, then orange. Thomas, lying on his stomach again, watches it all the way along.

## Part Five — Around a Quarter to Eight: Wool, Thread and Women's Work

The small question for this part of the evening is about the clothes on everyone's back. On a night like this, how many layers is each person in this house wearing, and who made every one of them?

Let's start with the second half of the question, because the answer is sitting right in front of us, spinning.

Agnes has not stopped. All through supper, her spindle sat in her lap, and the moment the bowls were cleared, it was turning again. Beside her now, Maud has a pair of wool cards on her knees, flat wooden paddles set with rows of tiny wire teeth, or in older households, the dried seed heads of the teasel plant. She lays a tuft of raw, washed wool on one, draws the other across it, again and again, until the tangled fibres lie straight and fluffy and soft, and then she rolls them off into a loose little sausage of wool called a rolag. She hands it to Alice, who adds it to the cloud on her mother's distaff. Round and round goes the spindle. The thread grows.

Imagine that Agnes turns to you now, in the firelight, and holds out the spindle with a little smile, inviting you to try. You take it. It's lighter than you expect, a smooth stick of wood about the length of your hand and forearm together, with its round stone whorl near the bottom. She shows you how to give it a flick against your leg to set it turning, how to let it drop and spin, how to draw out the fibres from the distaff with your other hand, pinching and releasing, so that the twist runs up into them and turns them into thread. You try. The thread is lumpy. Then it breaks, and the spindle clatters onto the floor, and Thomas laughs, and Agnes laughs too, and picks it up, and shows you again. In her hands, it looks like nothing. In yours, it's impossible. It took her years to learn.

And that's the first part of the answer. Almost every thread of wool and linen in this house was spun by the women of this family. Spinning was so universally women's work in medieval England that it shaped the language itself. An unmarried woman was a spinster, because that is what unmarried women spent much of their lives doing. The female line of a family was called the distaff side. Agnes spins in every spare moment, and so did her mother, and so will Alice. The weaving of the thread into cloth was more often done by a specialist, sometimes a village weaver, at a loom, and the cloth might then be sent away to be fulled, beaten in water to thicken and felt it. But the spinning came from here. From this bench. From these hands.

Now the first half of the question: the layers.

Next to the skin, everyone wears linen. For the women, a long, loose linen shift or smock. For the men, a linen shirt, and short linen underdrawers called braies. Linen was made from flax, grown in small plots and laboriously processed. The flax plants were pulled up whole, roots and all, and left to soak in water or lie out in the dew for weeks, until the woody stems began to rot away from the fibres inside. This was called retting, and it smelled terrible. Then the stems were dried and beaten, and scraped with a wooden blade, called scutching, and drawn through combs of iron spikes, called heckling, until all that was left were long, fine, silvery fibres, ready to spin. Every linen shift in this house went through all of that before Agnes ever touched it with her spindle. No wonder linen was precious, and patched, and passed down, and worn until it fell apart. Over that, a woollen tunic: for Robert and William, a knee-length tunic belted at the waist; for Agnes, Maud and Alice, a longer gown called a kirtle. On their legs, woollen hose, a kind of long cloth stocking, tied up to a belt or to the braies. On their feet, simple leather shoes, or, around the house, nothing at all but the hose. And on their heads, almost always, a hood, often with a short cape that covers the shoulders, or for the married women, a linen headcloth or wimple wrapped around the head and neck. Over everything, when they go outside, a heavy cloak. That's four or five layers, all of natural fibres, all loose enough to trap warm air between them.

Compare that to your own wardrobe. You probably own many times more clothing than this entire family put together. Much of it, if you look at the labels, is made of synthetic fibres that would astonish Agnes: polyester fleece, thermal base layers, down jackets filled with feathers held in by woven nylon. And you wash it all in a machine that does in an hour what would have taken Agnes a full day at the stream, in water so cold it numbed her hands. But in one sense, Agnes understood exactly what the makers of modern thermal clothing understand: that warmth comes from layers, and from air trapped between them.

What colour are their clothes? Mostly the colours of the sheep, and of the cheap dyes that could be made from local plants. Greys, browns, undyed creams, a dull russet red. In fact, a generation after our night, in 1363, the English Parliament passed a law known as the Statute Concerning Diet and Apparel, one of the most detailed attempts ever made in England to control who could wear what. Among many other rules, it said that carters, ploughmen, oxherds, cowherds, swineherds, dairymaids, and other people working on the land who owned less than forty shillings' worth of goods, were to wear only cheap cloth, called blanket and russet. They were not to dress above their station. The law was so unworkable that it was abandoned within about a year. But it tells us exactly what an Englishman of the governing classes thought a man like Robert ought to wear.

Wool mattered to more than just this family. In the fourteenth century, raw English wool was the most valuable export the kingdom had, shipped in vast quantities to the cloth-making towns of Flanders and Italy. The chalk hills around Wharram Percy were sheep country, then as now. And here is a remarkable coincidence of dates. In this very year, 1340, King Edward the Third was deep in the early years of what we now call the Hundred Years' War with France, and he needed money badly. Later that year, Parliament granted him an unusual tax: a ninth of every sheaf of grain, every fleece, and every lamb produced in the country's rural parishes. To collect it, juries in parish after parish were questioned on oath about what their parish produced. The surviving returns, known as the Nonarum Inquisitiones, the inquisitions of the ninth, from 1340 and 1341, were printed in 1807, and historians still use them to understand the farming of England, parish by parish, in exactly the year of our story. Somewhere in the countryside, in the months after our night, men of Robert's kind will be asked, on oath, how much wool their parish shears.

Agnes, of course, isn't thinking about the king. She's thinking about William's hose, which have holes in both heels, and Thomas's tunic, which he's outgrowing, and the length of thread on her spindle, and whether there'll be enough wool to last until the shearing.

There's a book I'd like to bring into the room now, because it lets us see a very different winter household, in the same century, and it makes a fascinating contrast. It's known as Le Ménagier de Paris, usually translated as The Goodman of Paris. It was written in about 1393 by an elderly and wealthy Parisian, for his very young wife, to teach her how to run a household. It's full of recipes, gardening tips, advice on choosing servants, how to get rid of fleas, and how to behave as a good wife. In one passage, he describes the comforts a husband hopes to find when he comes home. In the translation by Gina Greco and Christine Rose, published in 2009, he writes: "Make certain that in winter he has a good fire without smoke." He goes on to speak of letting the husband rest warmly wrapped and cosy. In the same part of the book, he warns of the miseries of holes in the roof and smoking fires.

A good fire without smoke. In a wealthy Paris townhouse of the 1390s, with chimneys and fireplaces built into the walls, that was something one could demand. In Robert's house, it is a dream. The smoke is part of the house. It's in the rafters, the thatch, the clothes, their hair. They do not notice it any more than we notice the hum of the refrigerator.

And now, because we're talking about women's work and women's lives, here is a place where historians have disagreed quite sharply.

In 1989, the historian Caroline Barron published an influential essay suggesting that the decades after the Black Death, which is still in this family's future, may have been something of a golden age for women in London. With so many people dead and labour scarce, she argued, women had new opportunities to work, to earn, to run businesses, and to marry or not marry as they chose. Other historians, above all Judith Bennett, pushed back. Bennett, who had studied the brewing women of the English countryside in great detail, in books such as Women in the Medieval English Countryside and Ale, Beer, and Brewsters in England, argued that while particular women might have done well, the basic position of women changed very little across the medieval centuries. She later called this pattern a "patriarchal equilibrium": women's work remained lower-paid, lower-status, and more easily pushed aside by men, whatever the economic weather. Other scholars have taken positions in between. It's one of the liveliest debates in medieval social history, and it hasn't been settled.

What both sides would agree on is what we can see in this room. Agnes works constantly, at many tasks at once. She cooks, she brews, she spins, she tends the baby, she keeps the hens, she makes the butter and cheese, she works in the croft, and at harvest time she'll be out in the fields with the sickle beside Robert. Her work is never finished, and it's never written down in any account roll as wages. Most of what we know about women like her comes from the edges of records: a fine for brewing here, a mention in a court case there, a figure in the margin of the Luttrell Psalter carrying a basket, or feeding the hens.

Maud has finished carding. She sets the cards aside and stretches her fingers towards the fire, slowly opening and closing them. Her knuckles are swollen. She doesn't complain. She just holds them there, in the warmth, the way you might hold your hands around a mug of tea.

Little Joan is asleep now. She fell asleep while nobody was watching, the way babies do, one fist up by her cheek, her mouth slightly open. Agnes glances down at her and doesn't stop spinning, but something in her face goes soft.

Thomas has been yawning for some time, and denying it. Alice has taken the last rolag from her grandmother and is winding it onto the distaff for her mother, carefully, the way she's been shown.

Outside, the frost has deepened. There is not a breath of wind. And in the silence, if you listen very carefully, you can hear the small creaks and ticks of the house itself, the timbers contracting in the cold, the thatch settling, a mouse in the straw, the slow, contented chewing of Brownie at the far end of the house, beyond the partition.

Maud begins, quietly, to sing. Not loudly, and not to anyone in particular. It's a tune with no beginning and no end, the kind of song that has been passed from mother to daughter for so long that nobody remembers where it came from. Her voice is thin and cracked and perfectly in tune. Agnes's spindle turns in time with it, without her seeming to notice. Thomas's eyes close, and open, and close again. Robert, at the end of the bench, has taken out his knife and a strip of leather and is mending the strap of a shoe, slowly, in the firelight, his head nodding a little to the rhythm. This is what an evening sounded like, in most of the houses of medieval Europe. Not silence. Not music, exactly. But this: a low voice, a turning spindle, a crackling fire, and the breathing of people and animals in the dark.

Alice, meanwhile, has picked up the little bundle of wool that her grandmother carded, and is teasing out a stray knot with her fingers, the tip of her tongue between her teeth. She's ten. By the time she's fourteen or fifteen, she'll spin as fast as her mother, and she'll know how to brew, and make cheese, and keep hens, and nurse a fever, and stretch a pot of pottage to feed one more mouth than it was made for. Nobody will ever write any of it down. It will simply pass, from Maud's hands to Agnes's to hers, the way it always has.

It's nearly eight o'clock. There are only a few tasks left before bed. And one of them, perhaps the most important of all, is to make sure that there will be enough fuel for the fire, tonight, and tomorrow, and for the rest of this long winter.

## Part Six — Around Half Past Eight: Wood, Bracken and the Putting to Bed of the Fire

Here is this hour's small question. The fire has burned all evening and will smoulder all night. So where does the fuel come from? And, a question that would matter a great deal to Robert: whose is it?

Look at the wood stacked by the hearth. Some ash, some thorn, a few twisted branches of oak, and beside them, a basket of other things: dried bracken, a bundle of gorse, which people here call furze or whin, and a few dark, flat, crumbly cakes that turn out, when you look closely, to be dried cow dung mixed with straw. In some parts of England, families burned peat or turf cut from the moors. On the open chalk Wolds, where trees were relatively scarce, people burned whatever they could find, and that included the dung of their own animals, dried in summer and stacked away like treasure.

Imagine you are walking with William, on a short winter afternoon, along the edge of the fields, gathering. You're looking for dead branches, fallen twigs, broken hedge-wood, anything that will burn. Your arms are full. Your fingers ache. You've been out for hours, and you have one armful. And then you pass the edge of a wood, a stand of trees that is clearly managed, cut and fenced, and full of good, dry, fallen timber. William stops you. He shakes his head. That wood is not for you. That wood belongs to the lord.

And that's the heart of the answer to our small question. In medieval England, almost all land, including woods, belonged to someone, and for a village like this one, that someone was the lord of the manor. Peasants had rights to some of it, carefully defined by custom. There was a right called housebote, which allowed a tenant to take wood to repair his house. There was firebote, sometimes called estovers, which allowed a certain amount of wood to be taken for the hearth. There might be rights to gather dead wood, or to cut furze and bracken on the common. But all of these were limited, and guarded, and anyone who went beyond them could find himself in front of the manor court.

The manor court is one of the great sources for this world, and I want to explain it properly, because we'll keep meeting it. Every few weeks, or every few months, depending on the manor, the lord's steward would hold a court, usually in the lord's hall or the church or even under a tree. The tenants were obliged to attend. The court dealt with land changing hands, with disputes between neighbours, with fines for brewing and baking, with marriages and deaths, with roads and ditches and straying animals, and with every small offence that troubled the life of a village. And a clerk wrote it all down, in abbreviated Latin, on long strips of parchment that were sewn together and rolled up. These are the court rolls. Tens of thousands of them survive in English archives, and they are where the names of ordinary medieval people most often appear.

Here's a real example, from the manor of Ludshott in Hampshire, from a roll of the fifteenth century, transcribed and translated by local historians in the village of Headley. In it, a man named John Langford, of Grayshott, is recorded as having cut and carried away one cart-load of wood, and one cart-load of bracken, from the common pasture of the lord's tenants, without permission. He was fined three pence. The same rolls record other men cutting boughs in the lord's woodland, and felling oaks, and carrying the timber away for their own use. It's a reminder of how valuable wood was. Men were willing to risk the court, and the fine, for a cart-load of fuel.

And there's another record, older and sadder, that I'll tell you very gently, because it shows the same truth from a different side. In the Bedfordshire coroners' rolls, the ones we met earlier, there's an entry about a man named Simon Daffe, of the village of Great Barford. On the thirtieth of January in 1269, in the depth of winter, Simon went into the River Ouse, intending to retrieve a piece of wood and take it to a meadow called Mulnemade on the east side of the town. And he drowned, the jurors said, by misadventure. That's all. A man, a river in January, and a piece of wood. Seven hundred and fifty years later, we know his name only because he wanted that piece of wood enough to wade into cold water for it. It reminds us that on the edge of every winter in medieval England, fuel was not a small thing. It was the difference between a warm house and a cold one.

You may have heard a pleasant old explanation for the English phrase "by hook or by crook", which is genuinely old, turning up in English writing by the late fourteenth century. The story goes that some tenants were allowed to take from the lord's woods whatever dead branches they could pull down using a billhook or a shepherd's crook, and no more. It's a lovely picture, and it may even be true. But I should tell you honestly that word historians regard it as one possible origin among several, and none of them has been proven. I mention it only because it captures something real: the rules about wood were exact, and people knew them to the inch.

There's one more quiet detail about tonight's wood. The ash logs by Robert's hearth were almost certainly not cut from a whole tree. Across medieval England, woods were managed by a method called coppicing, in which trees such as hazel, ash and willow were cut down to a stump every few years, and allowed to send up a crop of new straight poles. Some of the hazel in the wattle of Robert's walls came from exactly such a coppice, and the same stools, cut and regrown again and again, could go on producing for centuries. A few coppice stools in English woods today are thought to be many hundreds of years old. The medieval woodland was not wild. It was a crop, grown slowly, and harvested with care.

So that's our answer. The fuel comes from the hedges, the commons, the edges of the fields, the dung of the animals, and the lord's woods, as far as custom allows. And whose is it? Mostly, it belongs to someone else. Every log by Robert's hearth was gathered by permission, by right, or at a risk.

Let's set that beside your own home. Somewhere in your kitchen drawer, or in your inbox, there's probably a heating bill, or a gas bill, or an electricity bill. It might feel like a burden. But the fuel arrives at your house through pipes and wires, invisibly, at any hour, in any weather, and you never have to wade into a river or cut a single branch. If Robert could see a gas boiler, I think he would sit down in front of it and stare at it for a very long time.

Now, here's a disagreement among historians that touches this question directly.

In the middle of the twentieth century, the great economic historian Michael Postan argued that by around 1300, England had simply run out of room. The population, he believed, had grown so large over the previous two centuries that there was no longer enough good land to go round. Families were farming poorer and poorer soils, the woods and pastures were being cleared and ploughed, and the peasantry were being squeezed towards the edge of starvation, which is why the Great Famine of 1315 hit so hard. In this view, a family like Robert's, scraping for fuel and guarding every cart-load of bracken, is living in a land stretched to its limit.

But other historians have argued that this picture is too simple. John Hatcher and Mark Bailey, in a book called Modelling the Middle Ages, published in 2001, examined Postan's model alongside other ways of explaining the medieval economy, and many scholars, including Bruce Campbell and Christopher Dyer, have emphasised other forces: the growth of markets and towns, the role of lords' demands, trade, money, technology, and the climate itself. England around 1300, in this view, was not only overpopulated, but also more commercial and more inventive than Postan allowed. The debate has gone on for decades, and most historians now think the truth involves all of these things at once. What no one disputes is that around 1340, in villages like this, the woods were precious, the commons were crowded, and every armful of fuel had a story behind it.

Robert, by the hearth, has finished mending his shoe strap. He sets it aside, and stands, and stretches, and his head nearly touches the smoke layer. He looks at the woodpile, then at the fire, and then at his mother. She nods. It's time.

But first, the beds.

There's no separate bedroom in this house, not really. The family sleeps in the same main space where they eat and work, or in a small inner room partitioned off at one end, which Chaucer would have called the bower. In Robert's house, he and Agnes sleep on a low wooden bedframe against the partition wall, with a mattress, a big linen sack stuffed with straw, or with chaff, the papery husks winnowed from the grain, which is softer and quieter. Over the mattress, a coarse linen sheet, if they're lucky, and then coverlets of wool, thick and heavy, the kind of cheap blanket cloth that the statute of 1363 would later name. They have a long bolster at the head, stuffed with straw or chaff. A feather pillow is a luxury for wealthier people.

The children sleep together, close to the warmth of the hearth, on a pallet, a straw mattress laid right on the floor. William on one side, Alice on the other, and Thomas in the middle, where it's warmest. Maud has her own pallet, nearest of all to the fire, because she is old and feels the cold most, and because, I think, she likes to keep an eye on it. And Joan's cradle is pulled close to her parents' bed, where Agnes can reach it in the dark without getting up.

Everyone sleeps close. Nobody sleeps alone. In a cold house, that's not only affection. It's survival. Every body is a small warm stove, giving off its hundred watts or so, and under a pile of woollen coverlets, three children pressed together make a warm little nest of their own.

Do they undress? Partly. They'll take off their outer tunics and gowns and lay them over the coverlets for extra warmth, and keep on their linen, and probably their hoods or caps, because a great deal of warmth escapes from an uncovered head. Their hose they may well keep on, too.

Thomas has fallen asleep on the bench. He's slumped sideways against his grandmother, his mouth open, utterly gone. Robert scoops him up, all long limbs and dangling feet, and carries him to the pallet, and lays him down, and pulls the coverlets over him. Thomas doesn't wake. He sighs and rolls over and burrows down. He's the first of the family to fall asleep tonight, apart from the baby, and in a moment he'll be warm right through.

And now, Maud reaches for the rake.

Imagine you are kneeling beside her on the hearth stones. The heat on your face is strong. She draws the burning logs apart, and with the iron rake, she pulls the glowing embers into the centre of the hearth into a tight, red, breathing heap, about the size of a loaf of bread. The flames flicker down. She lays the unburned ends of the logs to one side, where they'll be ready in the morning. Then, from the pot beside her, she scoops dry, grey wood-ash, and she pours it, gently, over the embers, covering them completely. They disappear. For a moment, a thin red glow shows through the ash in one place, and she covers that too. She pats the mound gently with the flat of the rake. Then she lifts the old earthenware lid and sets it over the top.

That's it. The fire has been put to bed.

The room changes at once. The orange light is gone. What's left is a faint, warm, rosy glow from around the edges of the lid, and from a few cracks in the ash, and the last little yellow flame of a rushlight in its split stick on the beam. The smoke begins to thin, very slowly, and drift away through the thatch. The warmth of the hearth stones themselves, which have been soaking up heat all evening, will go on radiating into the room for hours.

It is, I think, one of the most ancient gestures there is. Somewhere in almost every human culture that has ever lived in a cold place, someone, at the end of the day, has knelt by a fire and covered it, and trusted it to be alive in the morning.

Before she lies down, Agnes does one last thing, the same thing she does every night. She goes to the door, and checks the latch. The wooden bar is down in its socket. The leather thong that lifts it from outside has been pulled in through its hole, so that nobody in the lane can open the door without breaking it. Tonight the house is closed.

Agnes snuffs out the rushlight with her fingers. And the room is dark.

## Part Seven — Around Half Past Nine: The Other End of the House

The small question for this hour is a quiet one. Now that the fire is covered and the rushlight is out, what does a medieval house sound like, in the dark, when everyone has gone to bed?

Let's listen.

Imagine that you are lying on the end of the children's pallet now, under the edge of a heavy woollen coverlet that smells of sheep and smoke. The straw in the mattress crackles faintly whenever anyone moves. It's very dark. Not completely, because there's still that soft rosy glow leaking from around the lid on the hearth, but dark enough that you can see only the faintest outlines: the hump of the coverlet, the curve of a crucked timber rising into the black, the pale shape of Maud's headcloth on the pallet near the fire.

And you hear things. You hear Thomas breathing beside you, slow and deep, already far away. You hear Alice turning over and whispering something to William, and William telling her to hush. You hear the small, regular creak of the cradle as Agnes, in the bed by the partition, reaches out a hand and rocks it once in her sleep. You hear Maud murmuring her prayers, very softly, her lips barely moving, the old Latin words worn smooth by sixty years of nights. You hear a mouse, somewhere in the thatch, and the scrape of the dog's claws as Bran resettles himself by the hearth. Far off, outside, an owl.

And from beyond the partition, at the other end of the house, you hear something large shift its weight in the straw, and let out a long, warm breath, and then begin, slowly and rhythmically, to chew.

That's the answer to our small question. A medieval house at night is not silent. It is full of breathing.

Now, this end of the house where Brownie sleeps is the part we've been circling around all night. So let's go and look at it.

At Wharram Percy, the excavators found the remains of a number of peasant houses that were long, narrow buildings, laid out along the line of the slope. These are the kind that archaeologists and historians have often called longhouses. In the classic form, one end of the building was for people, with the hearth, and the other end was for animals, with a drain running down the middle of the floor to carry away the wet, and often a cross-passage between the two, running from a door on one side of the house to a door on the other. Among the buildings excavated at Wharram was one known as Building 6, which English Heritage describes as exceptionally large by the standards of the houses preserved as earthworks on the site, and of relatively high status. The excavated farmsteads were published in detail in a volume called Wharram Six: Domestic Settlement Two, Medieval Peasant Farmsteads, in 1989. Elsewhere in England, especially on the uplands of Dartmoor, excavators have found stone longhouses with exactly this arrangement, the byre end set a little downhill so that everything drained away from the living space.

In Robert's house, the arrangement is simple. Through a low partition of wattle at the far end is the byre, with its own outside door, the one Robert led Brownie through at six o'clock. The floor slopes down a little, and there's a shallow gulley. On the far side, Brownie and her heifer stand or lie in deep straw. The hens roost on the beam above them. And through the partition, which doesn't reach all the way to the roof, the air of the whole house moves freely, from the hearth end to the byre end and back again.

It smells, of course. It smells of cow, and of hay, and of dung, warm and sweet and earthy. But I'll tell you something that people who grew up on small farms often say: you stop noticing it very quickly. And for the people in this house, it would be the smell of safety.

Here's another true thing about animals in medieval villages, and it tells us how precious they were. In many manors, when a tenant died, the lord was entitled to a payment called a heriot. Very often, the heriot was the dead man's best beast: his best ox, his best horse, or his best cow. The manor court would record it, and the animal would be led away to the lord's own herd. Imagine that. A family in mourning, and on the same day, the cow taken from the byre. You find heriots recorded in court rolls all over England, sometimes noted simply as a cow or an ox, sometimes with its colour. If Robert were to die this winter, Brownie could well be the beast that goes.

Brownie herself would look small to a modern eye. Archaeologists who study animal bones from medieval sites have found that medieval cattle were generally much smaller than the big dairy breeds of today, often not much taller at the shoulder than a large pony. They gave far less milk, too. And in winter, with nothing to eat but hay and straw, a cow like Brownie would usually give little or no milk at all. She is, as farmers say, dry. The milk, the butter and the cheese will come back in the spring, when the new grass comes and the calf is born. Until then, she is kept for the future, the way you might keep a seed.

That's why the stores matter so much. Hanging in the rafters, sitting in pots and crocks by the walls, there are the remains of last summer and autumn: the bacon, a little hard cheese, a crock of salted butter, dried peas and beans, a sack or two of grain, the bunches of herbs, a string of onions. From November until the first green things of spring, this family is living on what it saved. And there's a time that comes at the end of every winter, in late spring and early summer, which later generations would call the hungry gap, when last year's stores are almost gone, and this year's crops are not yet ripe. Every careful household in this village is already counting towards it.

The hens on the beam are part of the same careful sum. They lay very few eggs in the dark of winter, because hens lay according to the length of the day. But they matter in another way. On many manors, tenants owed small customary payments in kind to their lord, alongside their money rent: a hen or two at Christmas, and a number of eggs at Easter. Historians find these Christmas hens and Easter eggs in rentals and custumals, the written lists of what every tenant owed. Robert has already handed over his Christmas hen this year, a month ago. He grumbled. Everybody grumbles.

And Bran, the dog by the hearth, has his own job. He's no lapdog. He's a working dog, the kind of rough, medium-sized, shaggy animal that guarded yards and helped drive cattle. If anything comes near the byre door in the night, a fox for the hens, or a stranger, Bran will know before anyone else, and everyone will know a second later. He is, in his way, the house's alarm system, and he lies right in front of the covered fire, between the door and the sleeping children.

Think again of Chaucer's poor widow, from earlier in the night, in her sooty cottage with her three sows, her three cows and her sheep called Malle. Chaucer was writing a comic tale about a rooster and a fox, but he didn't need to explain any of that to his readers. Every one of them would have known exactly what a cottage with animals in it looked like, smelled like, and sounded like at night. It was simply how most of England lived.

And here we come to one of the places where historians genuinely disagree, and it's about this very building.

How common, really, were longhouses, with people and animals under one roof? For a long time, it was widely assumed that this was simply how medieval peasants lived, all over England. But the definition itself has been argued over. The Welsh scholar Iorwerth Peate, writing in the mid-twentieth century, used a broad definition: a longhouse, for him, was a house where the family and the cattle shared a building, and where you could get from one to the other inside. Later, the architectural historian Peter Smith argued for a stricter definition, and pointed out that many buildings that had been called longhouses actually had separate entrances for the animals and the people, and so, he argued, shouldn't count. Some historians today prefer the plain, descriptive phrase "house-and-byre homestead" to avoid the argument altogether. And there are popular writers who've gone further, pointing out that in the very few medieval pictures we have of animals inside houses, the animals seem to have their own doors and their own part of the building. Meanwhile, archaeologists have found that in some regions, the longhouse was a common type for a while and then went out of use, with byres moved into separate buildings across the yard. Over the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, in many parts of lowland England, that's exactly what happened.

So the honest answer is that it varied. From region to region, from village to village, from family to family, and over time. Robert's house, with its byre at one end, a partition, and its own byre door, is one real kind of medieval house. It was not the only kind.

Let me give you the modern comparison now. Many modern homes, especially in North America, have an attached garage, a big unheated space on the other side of a wall, where the most valuable movable thing the family owns spends the night. And some modern homes have heated floors, or a heat pump drawing warmth from the air or the ground. Brownie is, in a sense, both of those things. She's the family car, parked in the garage. And she's the family's heater, quietly running all night.

Because now, at last, we come to the second of our three mysteries. I asked you at the start of the evening: why would a family who could not afford to lose anything at all choose to sleep only a few steps away from a large animal, under the same roof, breathing the same air, all night long?

There are three answers, and they're all true at once.

The first answer is warmth. A cow is a very large, very warm animal, and digesting a stomach full of hay is a slow, steady process that produces a great deal of heat. By modern farm estimates, a full-grown cow gives off something like a kilowatt of heat, roughly as much as a small electric heater, and she does it all night, without any fuel but the hay she's already eaten. Compare that to each of the seven people in this house, at around a hundred watts each. Brownie, alone, may be giving off more warmth than the whole family put together. On a night when the fire is covered and the frost is creeping across the thatch, that warmth, rising and drifting through the partition, matters.

The second answer is safety. Brownie is the most valuable thing Robert owns. In a byre across the yard, she'd be out of sight and out of hearing. Here, if she's unwell in the night, if she's restless, if she begins to calve early, if someone tries the byre door, Robert will hear it, because he's lying a few yards away. She cannot easily be stolen. She cannot fall ill in the night without someone knowing. She cannot freeze.

And the third answer is care. A cow in winter needs feeding, watering, and watching. With the byre under the same roof, all of that can be done without going out into the snow and the dark. Robert can check her by the light of a rushlight, in his shirt and hose, and be back under the coverlets in a minute.

So that's our second mystery solved. The family shares the house with the cow because she keeps them warm, because they keep her safe, and because, in a world where losing her might mean hunger, it would be foolish to do anything else.

As if he'd been listening, Robert gets up.

You feel the pallet shift as he pads past in the dark, barefoot on the cold earth, towards the partition. He doesn't need a light. He has walked this path in the dark ten thousand times. You hear the soft rustle of straw, the creak of a hurdle, and his voice, low and calm, saying something to the cow. You hear her answer with a breath. You hear him run his hand along her side, and then the small sounds of a man checking that all is well: the trough, the straw, the door. Then he pads back again, past the hearth, and pauses there for a moment, looking down at the glow around the edges of the lid. He doesn't touch it. He just looks. Then he goes back to bed.

Alice is asleep now. She fell asleep with her face pressed into Thomas's back, one arm thrown over him. William is still awake for a little longer, staring up into the dark, thinking whatever thirteen-year-old boys think about in the dark, and then he too is gone, his breathing slowing, his face slack.

Maud's prayers have stopped. Her lips have stopped moving. She is lying on her side, facing the fire, as she always does, and her eyes are closed.

The house breathes. The cow chews. The lid on the hearth glows softly at its edges. And outside, the frost tightens its grip on the valley, and the stars wheel very slowly across the sky above the roof.

## Part Eight — Around Half Past Ten: Bells, Stars and the Keeping of Time

Here is the small question for this hour of the night. There is no clock in this house. There is no clock anywhere in this village. So how does anyone know what time it is?

Agnes is awake. She often is, at this hour, lying on her back in the bed by the partition, listening to the house. Robert is asleep beside her, a warm, heavy shape under the coverlets, breathing slowly through his nose. The children are a single soft mound on their pallet. Maud is still, facing the hearth. The cradle is still. And Agnes, for once, has nothing in her hands.

Imagine you are sitting quietly on the edge of the bed beside her, in the dark. You can just make out her face in the faint red glow from the hearth, her eyes open, looking up at the roof. She isn't worried. She's simply awake, the way mothers of small children often are, in that drowsy, watchful in-between place where you're resting but you haven't quite let go. You could ask her the time. And she would tell you, without hesitation, that it's not long after the full dark of the evening, and a good while yet before midnight. She would know.

How?

Partly, she knows by the fire. Maud covered it about two hours ago, and the glow through the ash has a particular colour and strength at this point in the night, a little dimmer than when it was first banked, a little brighter than it will be by dawn. Partly she knows by her own body: how long she's been lying here, how tired she is, whether the baby is due to wake. Partly she knows by the cold, which deepens through the night in a way you can feel on your face. And if she were to get up and open the shutter, she'd know by the stars.

Every farming family in medieval Europe could read the night sky as a clock. In the north, the stars of the Great Bear, which the English called the Plough, or sometimes Charles's Wain, wheel slowly around the Pole Star through the night, like the hand of a vast clock face turning backwards. Shepherds and ploughmen knew roughly where the Plough would stand at dusk at each time of year, and where it would stand by midnight, and where by dawn. They knew which stars rose late in winter, and which set early. You didn't need a dial. You had the whole sky.

And then there were the birds. People in the Middle Ages commonly spoke of the night in terms of cockcrow. The first cockcrow came in the very small hours, well before dawn, and the last came as the sky began to pale. Robert's neighbours keep cockerels, and on a still night like this, their crowing carries right across the valley.

And there were the bells. The great monasteries of medieval Europe kept a strict round of prayer through the day and night, known as the canonical hours, and some of the services were held in the deep of the night, when the monks rose from their beds and went to the church in the dark to sing. In towns and near great churches, bells rang for these services, and for the hours of the day: prime in the early morning, terce, sext at midday, none in the afternoon, vespers at evening, compline at bedtime. The little parish church of Saint Martin up on the rise doesn't keep the full round of monastic hours, but its bell rings for Mass and for the great feasts, and in many places a bell rang at the evening's end.

That's our answer. Agnes knows the time by the fire, the sky, the cold, the cockerels and the bells, and by a lifetime of nights just like this one.

There was one other old way of measuring the hours of the night, and it comes to us from one of the oldest biographies in English history. In about the year 893, a Welsh monk named Asser wrote a Life of King Alfred the Great, the king of Wessex, whom he knew personally. Asser tells a story, which I'll paraphrase, about how Alfred, wanting to divide his days and nights fairly between prayer, work and rest, had candles made of a fixed weight of wax, each one marked so that it would burn for a set number of hours. Six of them, burned one after another, lasted a whole day and night. And because the draughts that blew through the doors and windows and the gaps in the walls of his halls made the candles burn unevenly, Alfred had lanterns made for them out of wood and thin, translucent plates of horn, to shield the flames. Even a king, four and a half centuries before our night, lived in buildings so draughty that his candles guttered in the wind. And even a king had to invent his own way to tell the time in the dark. Robert, of course, could never afford to burn beeswax to measure the night. He lets the night measure itself.

It's worth knowing, though, that real mechanical clocks were just beginning to appear in this very century. In the 1320s and 1330s, an abbot of Saint Albans named Richard of Wallingford designed an extraordinary astronomical clock for his abbey, with dials showing the movements of the sun, the moon and the tides. Around the same time, a handful of cathedrals and great churches began installing striking clocks, driven by weights, that rang a bell on the hour. Within a few generations, clocks with bells would be ringing in town after town across England. But none of that reaches Wharram Percy. Not in 1340. Here, time is still the old, soft, round kind of time, measured in dusk and cockcrow and dawn.

Let's set this beside your own home. Right now, wherever you are, you're probably within reach of several clocks. One on your phone, one on the oven, one on the microwave, one on your bedside table, one on the corner of a screen, all agreeing with each other to the second, because they're set by signals from atomic clocks that are accurate to a fraction of a second over millions of years. And if you woke in the night and wondered what time it was, you'd reach out and look. Agnes would have to feel her way to it. And I think she'd be surprised at how often we feel the need to know.

Now, here's another true thing about the night in this century. You might expect that a village like this would have someone keeping watch all night, walking the lanes, guarding against thieves. In some times and places, it did. In 1285, King Edward the First issued a law called the Statute of Winchester, which among other things ordered that in walled towns, the gates should be shut from sunset until sunrise, and that watches should be kept in cities, boroughs and towns, by a set number of men, all night "from the sun setting unto the sun rising." Any stranger passing in the night could be arrested by the watch and held until morning. But here's the detail I love. The statute only required these watches from Ascension Day, in late spring, until Michaelmas, at the end of September. The warm half of the year. In the depth of winter, on a frozen night like this, the law didn't ask anyone to stand in the lane until dawn. Perhaps because so few people would be out. Perhaps because the cold itself was a kind of watchman.

So tonight, in the lanes of Wharram Percy, there is no one. The frost lies white and undisturbed on the ruts. A fox crosses the end of the lane and slips under a hedge. That's all.

Lying awake, Agnes says a prayer. It's one of the three that every Christian in medieval England was expected to know by heart: the Pater Noster, which we call the Lord's Prayer, the Ave Maria, the Hail Mary, and the Creed. She says them in Latin, the way she learned them as a girl, from her mother and from the priest, without knowing exactly what every Latin word means, but knowing very well what the prayers are for. She prays for Joan, who has a cough that comes and goes. She prays for the two babies in the churchyard. She prays for a good spring.

And here we reach a point where historians have disagreed very strongly, and for a long time.

For many years, the dominant picture of religion in late medieval England, shaped partly by historians writing from a Protestant perspective, was of a church in decline. The clergy, in this view, were often ignorant and greedy, ordinary people's faith was mostly superstition and habit, and the old church was a tired, hollow thing waiting to be swept away by the Reformation of the sixteenth century. One influential version of this view was set out by the historian A. G. Dickens, in his book The English Reformation, first published in 1964. But in 1992, the historian Eamon Duffy published a landmark book called The Stripping of the Altars, which argued almost exactly the opposite. Drawing on wills, church records, prayer books and the surviving art of parish churches, Duffy argued that late medieval English religion was rich, vigorous and deeply loved by ordinary people, woven through every part of their lives, and that the Reformation destroyed something many people treasured. Duffy's book focuses mainly on the fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries, a little after our night, and historians since have argued about the balance between his view and the older one. But for a woman like Agnes, praying in the dark for her baby, I suspect Duffy would recognise what he was writing about.

There's a kind of record I want to tell you about now, because it shows how people like Agnes remembered time, not by the clock, or by the calendar, but by the events of their own lives.

When a landholder who held land directly from the king died, and his heir was a child, the heir would have to prove, when they came of age, that they were old enough to inherit. This was done through a process called a proof of age, and these proofs survive in large numbers among the records known as inquisitions post mortem, from the thirteenth to the fifteenth century. A jury of local men would be asked, on oath, how they knew the heir's age. And each man had to give a reason. Not a date written down somewhere, but a memory. A juror might say that he remembered the year because his own son was born that same week. Or because that was the year of his own marriage, or the year his father died. Or because he had been at the church on the day of the baptism. Scholars who've studied these proofs point out that men often drew on the memories of their wives, kin and neighbours to recall such events, sometimes fourteen or twenty-one years after the fact. Historians argue about how far these stories can be trusted, because some of them look formulaic, repeated from one proof to another. But even so, they tell us something true. In a world without clocks or diaries, a year was remembered as the year the barn burned, or the year of the great snow, or the year the baby came.

Agnes, if you asked her, would say that Joan was born in the spring after the wet harvest, in the week the cuckoo came. That's how she counts.

Agnes counts her own life in feasts, too, the way everyone does here. The year turns not on numbered months but on the great days of the church. Michaelmas, at the end of September, when rents are due. Martinmas, in November, when the pig is killed. Christmas, with its twelve days of rest from the heaviest work of the fields. The first Monday after Twelfth Night, ten days ago, which later generations would celebrate as Plough Monday, when the ploughmen went back to work after the Christmas rest. Candlemas, coming soon, at the beginning of February, when the church is full of candles. Lady Day in March. Easter. Whitsun. Lammas, at the start of August, the loaf-mass, when bread from the first harvest is blessed. Every one of them is a mark on the year, the way the cockcrows are marks on the night.

Robert stirs, and mutters something about the cow, and settles again. The cradle gives a small creak as Joan turns in her sleep. And Agnes, at last, closes her eyes.

Her breathing slows. Her hand, which had been resting on the edge of the cradle, slips down onto the coverlet. And the last person awake in the house is asleep.

It's a little before eleven. The whole family is sleeping now, all seven of them, under the soot-black thatch, around the softly glowing hearth. The cow chews. The dog sighs. The frost thickens.

Outside, something is changing, very slowly, although nobody in the house will see it. Over the low eastern ridge of the valley, a little before half past eleven, the moon is rising. It's a waning moon tonight, a little more than two-thirds full, past its brightest and growing thinner night by night, and as it climbs, it lays a pale, cold, silver light across the frozen thatch and the white fields and the still surface of the mill pond. I worked that out, by the way, using a modern astronomical model, because nobody wrote down the moon over Wharram Percy on this particular night in 1340. But the sky keeps better records than any clerk, and the sums are simple enough. In a little while, a thin blade of that moonlight will find its way through a crack in one of the shutters and lie across the floor of the house, a few inches from the hearth.

This, in the language of this world, is what people called the first sleep. And we'll hear a great deal more about that phrase before this night is done.

## Part Nine — Around Midnight: The Baby, the Moon and the Lullaby

The small question for this part of the night is about the smallest person in the house. Why is little Joan wrapped up as tightly as a parcel? And what does a medieval mother do when her baby wakes at midnight in a freezing house?

It's midnight, or near enough. The moon is up now, over the eastern ridge, and just as I promised, a thin blade of silver light has found its way through a gap between the shutter and the wall, and lies across the earthen floor a little way from the hearth, cold and bright and perfectly still. The glow around the lid on the hearth is fainter than it was. The cold in the room has deepened. If you put your hand out from under the coverlet now, you'd feel the air on your skin like cold water, and you'd pull it back again quickly.

And from the cradle comes a small sound. Not a cry, yet. A sort of creaking, snuffling, complaining sound, the sound of a baby who is waking up and is not at all sure she approves.

Agnes is awake at once. Mothers are. She doesn't need to think about it. Her hand is on the edge of the cradle before her eyes are properly open, and she rocks it, once, twice, three times, the way she did at suppertime, with that same unhurried rhythm. Back and forth. Back and forth.

For a moment it works. Then Joan's complaint rises into a thin, real cry. She's hungry.

So Agnes sits up, pulling the coverlet around her shoulders, and lifts Joan out of the cradle.

Imagine you are sitting beside her now, on the edge of the bed, in the moonlight and the faint red glow. The baby she lifts out is tightly wrapped. Her arms are bound down by her sides, her legs are wrapped straight together, and over the top of it all are long bands of linen wound round and round her little body, so that she looks like a small, warm, wriggling loaf. Only her face shows, red and crumpled and furious, inside a linen cap.

This is swaddling, and it's the answer to the first half of our small question. Across medieval Europe, and for centuries afterwards, babies were commonly swaddled in bands of cloth for much of their first months. Medieval people believed it helped a baby's soft limbs to grow straight and strong, and many writers on childcare said so. But it did other things too. It kept the baby very warm, wrapped in layer after layer of linen and wool, on nights exactly like this one. It kept her from kicking off her coverings in the cradle. And it seemed to calm her, as tight wrapping still calms many babies today. By nine months, Joan is getting near the age when the swaddling bands would usually come off, at least in the daytime. She has been pulling herself up on the edge of the cradle, after all. But at night, in January, Agnes keeps her bound up snug.

Joan has a name, of course, and in this world, her name came to her in a very particular way. She was carried to the church of Saint Martin on the day she was born, or the day after, because babies were baptised as quickly as possible, in case they should not live. At the font, she had godparents. In England, the custom was for a child to have three: for a girl, two godmothers and one godfather. And very often, a child was given the name of one of its godparents. So Joan is named for her godmother, a neighbour's wife who lives three tofts down the lane, who held her at the font, and who has given her, as a christening gift, a small coral bead on a thread, which Agnes keeps tied to the cradle. Coral, for some reason nobody here could explain, was believed to protect children. It hangs there now, catching a tiny glint of moonlight.

Agnes loosens the top bands a little, and opens the front of her own linen shift, and puts the baby to her breast, and holds her close under the coverlet. The crying stops almost at once, replaced by a small, contented, rhythmic sound. And Agnes leans back against the wall, and closes her eyes again, and sits there in the moonlight, feeding her daughter in the cold.

That's the second half of our answer. What does a medieval mother do when her baby wakes at midnight? Exactly what mothers have always done. She feeds her, holds her, warms her, and sings to her.

Most medieval babies were breastfed, usually by their own mothers, and often for a long time, well into their second year, which also helped space the births of brothers and sisters. Wealthy families sometimes hired a wet nurse to feed their babies for them, and the books of advice written for great households spend a great deal of time on how to choose a good one. For a family like Robert's, that question never arises. Agnes is Joan's food, and her warmth, and her cradle-rocker, all at once.

And she does sing. Very, very softly, so as not to wake the others. Not words, at first. Just a tune, a low humming, the sort of sound that seems to come from somewhere very deep in the chest.

There's a poem I'd like you to hear now, because it's one of the most precious survivals of this whole century. In a small manuscript now in the British Library, known as Harley 913, put together around 1330, which is to say within a decade of our night, there is a lullaby. It's in English. It's six verses long. And scholars believe it is the earliest lullaby in the English language to survive. Its first line, in the Middle English of the manuscript, goes like this:

"Lollai, lollai, litil child, whi wepistou so sore?"

Lullay, lullay, little child, why do you weep so sore?

You can hear it, can't you? "Lollai, lollai." It's the same sound, the same soft, rocking, repeated syllable that mothers in English have used for their babies ever since. Lullay. Lullaby. And yet the poem is not quite what we might expect. As it goes on, the mother tells her child, gently, that perhaps there is good reason for weeping, because life in this world is hard, and full of sorrow, and the child has come into it as a stranger. Scholars have long debated whether it's a true lullaby, sung to real babies, or a religious poem written in the form of one, perhaps by a friar, because Harley 913 seems to have been a kind of portable commonplace book belonging to a Franciscan. There's also a Latin version of the first two verses on another page of the same manuscript. But whatever it was meant to be, the opening line has the sound of a real mother's voice. A tired woman, in the night, holding a crying baby, and asking her, tenderly, why she's crying so.

Think back, too, to the lines from Piers Plowman that we heard in the early evening, from the 1380s: the poor women in their cottages, charged with children, with their woe in winter-time, and their waking at nights to rise and rock the cradle. Langland wasn't writing a lullaby. He was writing a plea for charity, an argument that the truly deserving poor were not the loud beggars at the church door but the silent, exhausted mothers in their cottages at midnight. And yet, put the two pieces side by side, the poet's plea and the friar's lullaby, and you have a picture of this exact moment, drawn twice, by two different hands, in the same century.

I like to imagine Agnes humming something like it now. Lollai, lollai. And Joan, warm and fed, slowly stops sucking, and her eyes close, and her fists uncurl.

And here we reach a point where historians have disagreed for more than half a century, and it's about this very moment.

In 1960, the French historian Philippe Ariès published a book that became enormously famous, translated into English two years later as Centuries of Childhood. One of his most striking claims was that the medieval world did not really have a concept of childhood as a special stage of life, as we do. In medieval art and writing, he argued, children appeared as little adults, and parents, faced with such high rates of infant death, may have kept a certain emotional distance from their babies, not investing too much love in a child who might not live. It was a powerful argument, and for a while it was widely accepted. But in the decades since, many historians have challenged it, firmly. Shulamith Shahar, in Childhood in the Middle Ages, published in English in 1990, and Nicholas Orme, in his book Medieval Children, published in 2001, gathered a great wealth of evidence, from toys and games, from children's songs, from medical advice, from the coroners' rolls, from the grief recorded in letters and poems, to argue that medieval parents loved their children deeply, recognised childhood as a distinct stage of life, and mourned bitterly when their children died. Most historians today side with Shahar and Orme, though Ariès is still respected for asking the question in the first place.

I think if you sat beside Agnes, right now, watching her face in the moonlight as she looks down at Joan, you would not need a historian to tell you which side you were on.

Let's set this moment beside a modern home, as we've done all night. If you have a baby today, you might have a monitor in the nursery, with a camera and a microphone and perhaps a thermometer, sending a picture to your phone. You might have a special sleeping bag for the baby, rated for the temperature of the room. You might have a bottle warmer, and a night light that glows softly, and a machine that plays the sound of rain. Agnes has none of that. But she has the cradle within arm's reach, the swaddling bands, her own warm body, and her voice. And when the baby cries, she is there in a second, because she's never more than a few feet away.

She keeps Joan with her for a little while after the feed, holding her upright against her shoulder, patting her back gently, the way mothers still do. Then she wraps the bands snug again, and lays her back in the cradle, and tucks the little woollen coverlet in around her, and rocks the cradle with her hand. Back and forth. Back and forth.

Joan is asleep again. She was hardly awake in the first place.

But the night is not quite as still as it was. The crying, small as it was, has reached into the sleep of the others. You can hear it in their breathing, which has changed, become shallower, lighter. Maud has moved. Thomas has turned over and flung out an arm. Robert, beside Agnes, has stopped snoring and is lying very still, the way people do when they are just at the surface of sleep, not quite awake, not quite under. It's past midnight now. The first sleep of the night, the deep sleep that came on in the early evening, is thinning, like mist when the sun comes up.

And over the next hour, as I'll tell you in the next part of our night, every one of them is going to wake.

But not yet. For now, Agnes lies down again, and pulls the coverlet over her shoulder, and puts her hand back on the edge of the cradle. The blade of moonlight has moved a little across the floor, the way the shadow on a sundial moves, and it's touching the edge of the hearth stones now. On the hearth, the lid sits in its mound of ash, faintly rimmed with red. Underneath it, the embers are still alive, as they have been all night, breathing very slowly, in the dark.

It's a strange and lovely thing to think about. If you could lift that lid right now, and brush away the ash, you'd find, at the heart of the mound, a few coals still glowing orange, as hot as the fire was at suppertime, just much, much smaller. All the warmth of the evening, folded up small and kept safe.

Joan coughs once in her sleep, a small dry cough, and Agnes's hand goes still on the cradle, listening. Nothing more comes. Joan breathes on, slow and even. Agnes's hand starts to rock again.

And so the house rests, for a little while longer, in the deep middle of the night. The moonlight lies on the floor. The cow is quiet. The dog's ears twitch at something only he can hear, and then lie flat again.

If you were here, in your nightclothes, you'd be very cold now. The air at the edges of the room may well be close to freezing, and the cold has a way of finding the gaps between the coverlets. But under the coverlets, in the nest of bodies and wool and straw, it's warm. Not warm the way your bed is warm, with its electric blanket or its duvet of goose-down. But warm enough. Warm enough to sleep. Warm enough to wake in the morning.

That's what all of these small things have been for, this whole night. The thick thatch, the chalk and daub, the banked fire, the cow beyond the partition, the swaddling bands, the layers of wool, the children sleeping three to a pallet. Not one of them, on its own, would keep you alive on a night like this. But together, they make a kind of shelter that no single part could make alone, and the family inside it sleeps on through the deepest cold of the year.

## Part Ten — Around One in the Morning: The Waking Hour

Here is the small question for this hour. If you woke up in the middle of a winter night, with no lamp to switch on, no phone to look at, no book to read, and many hours until morning, what would you do?

Because that's exactly what's about to happen in this house.

It begins with Robert. Somewhere around one o'clock, he simply opens his eyes. He isn't startled. Nothing woke him. He just surfaces, the way a swimmer comes up for air, and lies there for a while in the dark, completely awake, listening. He can hear Agnes breathing beside him, and the cow, and the children, and somewhere outside, very far off, a cockerel crowing on the other side of the valley, the first cockcrow of the night, thin and clear in the frozen air.

Imagine that you're awake too, lying on the end of the children's pallet. It's very dark now. The moonlight has moved off the floor and up onto the wall, a pale smudge on the daub. The glow around the lid on the hearth is fainter than ever, just a dull, dark red line. And you feel it, too, that same strange, calm, clear wakefulness. Not tired. Not restless. Just awake, in the middle of the night, as if it were the most natural thing in the world.

Robert gets up. He pulls his tunic over his shirt and steps barefoot across the cold floor. First, he goes to the hearth, and crouches beside it, and holds his hand over the lid, feeling for the warmth. It's there. Faint, but there. He nods to himself. He doesn't lift the lid. He doesn't need to. The fire is alive. That's all he wanted to know.

Then he goes through to the byre, as he did earlier, and you hear the soft murmur of his voice again, and the rustle of straw, and Brownie's long, calm breath. Then he comes back, and in the dark corner by the door, he uses the chamber pot, a plain earthenware jug kept there for the purpose, because nobody goes out into the yard on a January night if they don't have to. Then he climbs back into bed, and lies down, but he doesn't go back to sleep. Not yet.

And now Agnes is awake too. She was awake, of course, at midnight, for the baby, and drifted off again. Now she's awake for no reason at all. She turns her head on the bolster and finds Robert looking at her in the dark, and they begin to talk.

They talk very quietly, in the low murmur of people who've shared a bed for fifteen years and don't need to explain anything to each other. They talk about the spring. About the strip in the north field that floods every year, and whether to sow it with oats or beans. About the heifer, and whether she should be sold at the fair or kept. About William, who's growing so fast that he'll need new hose before Easter. About Joan's cough, which seemed better tonight. About nothing much. Robert says something that makes Agnes laugh, a small, muffled laugh into the coverlet. Then they're quiet for a while, just lying close, his arm around her, in the warmth.

On the far side of the hearth, Maud is awake too. She has been, for a little while. She is sitting up on her pallet, her coverlet around her shoulders like a cloak, and she has in her hands a little string of wooden beads, worn smooth. People in this century called a string of prayer beads like this a paternoster, after the first words of the Lord's Prayer, and they used them to count their prayers in the dark. Maud's lips are moving. Click. Click. Click. Each bead slides through her fingers with a tiny wooden sound. She is praying for her dead husband, and for her sons, and for her grandchildren, one by one, by name.

And on the children's pallet, William and Alice are whispering. They're not supposed to be. They know that. But they're awake, and it's dark, and there's nothing else to do, so they whisper about the things children have always whispered about at night. About a boy in the next toft who fell through the ice on the mill pond last winter and came out shrieking. About the dog. About what Alice wants to do when she's grown, and William telling her that girls can't, and Alice telling him that he's wrong, and to go to sleep. Thomas, between them, wakes up enough to ask for a drink, and William gets up, grumbling, to fetch him a cup of ale from the jug by the wall, and Thomas drinks it, half asleep, and lies down again.

So there's our answer to the small question. What do you do, in a medieval house, when you wake up at one in the morning? You check the fire. You see to the animals. You use the chamber pot. You pray. You talk quietly to the person beside you. You whisper with your brother and sister. You think. You lie close and warm in the dark. And you don't, it seems, worry very much about being awake at all.

Let's put this beside your own life for a moment. Many people today, if they wake at one or two or three in the morning and can't get back to sleep, feel something close to panic. They look at the clock and calculate how many hours are left before the alarm. They worry that something is wrong with them. They pick up their phone, and the bright light of the screen wakes them further. Whole industries have grown up around this experience. In this house, nobody is looking at a clock, because there's no clock to look at. And nobody seems to think there's anything wrong.

There's a line in Chaucer that I've been waiting all night to share with you, because it's about exactly this. It's in the Squire's Tale, another of the Canterbury Tales, written in the late fourteenth century. The heroine of the tale is a princess named Canacee. One evening, Chaucer tells us, she goes to bed early, soon after the evening falls, and so she wakes in the early hours of the morning, after what Chaucer calls her "first sleep." Her companions, who stayed up late, sleep on until full daylight. I'll paraphrase the rest, because the exact Middle English line varies between editions. But that phrase, "first sleep," is the key. Chaucer doesn't explain it. He doesn't need to. He expects his readers to know exactly what he means.

And historians have found that phrase, and its partner, "second sleep," all over the place.

The historian who first noticed this was A. Roger Ekirch, of Virginia Tech, in the United States. He was researching the history of night, and he kept coming across references, in diaries, letters, court depositions, medical books, prayer manuals and poems, to people's "first sleep" and "second sleep," as if everyone knew that a night's sleep came in two parts. In 2001, he published an article in the American Historical Review called "Sleep We Have Lost: Pre-industrial Slumber in the British Isles," and in 2005, a book called At Day's Close: Night in Times Past. In his book, he reported more than five hundred references to this two-part pattern, and he has said since that he's found more than two thousand, in a dozen languages. His argument was that before the age of artificial light, people in Europe commonly went to bed not long after dark, slept for several hours, woke for an hour or so in the middle of the night, and then fell asleep again until morning. That waking hour, he argued, was used for exactly the kinds of things we've seen in this house tonight: prayer, quiet talk, tending fires, checking on animals, thinking, and love.

He found support from an unexpected direction. In the early 1990s, a psychiatrist at the United States National Institutes of Health named Thomas Wehr ran an experiment in which volunteers spent many nights in rooms that were dark for fourteen hours at a time, like a winter night. After a few weeks, many of them began to sleep in two blocks, with a quiet waking period of an hour or two in the middle. Ekirch saw this as a glimpse of what the long nights of the past might have done to human sleep.

Now, there's something about tonight that Agnes remembers, lying here in the dark, and it makes her smile. Tomorrow, the twenty-first of January, is the feast of Saint Agnes, the young Roman martyr whose name she carries. Which makes tonight Saint Agnes' Eve. In later centuries, English country girls would keep this night with little customs and fasts, in the hope of dreaming of the man they would marry, customs that were written down only long after our night. And in 1819, nearly five hundred years after Agnes lies here in her bed, the poet John Keats would write a long and beautiful poem called The Eve of St. Agnes, which begins with a line that could have been written about this valley tonight: "St. Agnes' Eve—Ah, bitter chill it was!" And then: "The owl, for all his feathers, was a-cold." Agnes, of course, knows nothing of Keats. But she knows the cold. And she knows her saint's day is coming, and that in the morning, if there's time, she'll go up to Saint Martin's and say a prayer to the girl whose name she was given at the font.

She whispers this to Robert, and he says, sleepily, that every day is Saint Agnes' day in this house, and she tells him not to be foolish, and they're quiet again.

Out in the valley, the cockerel on the far side crows again, and another answers it, nearer. In the great monasteries of England tonight, at around this hour, the monks are rising from their beds in the dark and filing into their cold churches by the light of a single lantern, to sing the night office, the longest of all their services. Up in the dales to the west and north, in the great abbeys of Yorkshire, at places like Rievaulx and Fountains and Byland, the chant is rising now into high stone vaults, in the dark, while the world outside sleeps. For the monks, the middle of the night was not a time for sleeping. It was one of the holiest times of the day. And many devotional writers encouraged ordinary men and women, too, to use the quiet of the night for prayer, when the mind was clear and the house was still. Maud, with her beads, is doing exactly what the books advised, though she has never read a book in her life.

Let me give you one more image of this wakeful hour, because I think it's the one that best sums up the strange peace of it. Imagine that you get up now, very quietly, and go to the shutter, and open it just a crack. The cold comes in like a knife. And outside, the valley lies silver under the waning moon. Every roof is white with frost. The church stands black against the stars. The mill pond is a sheet of grey glass. And here and there, from the thatch of the other houses down the lane, very faint, you can see the thinnest wisps of smoke still rising, straight up, in the windless air, from fires that have been banked, like this one, and are breathing slowly under their covers through the night. In each of those houses, perhaps, someone is lying awake, too, as you are. Talking softly. Praying. Listening to the animals. A whole village, awake in the middle of the night, together, without a single light.

You close the shutter. The cold retreats. You go back to the pallet.

And here, of course, is where the historians, and the scientists, disagree.

In 2015, a team led by the sleep researcher Jerome Siegel of the University of California, Los Angeles, published a study in the journal Current Biology of three groups of people who still live without electricity: the Hadza of Tanzania, the San of Namibia, and the Tsimane of Bolivia. They found that these people did not, in general, sleep in two distinct blocks. They tended to sleep in a single stretch, often going to bed some hours after dark. Ekirch replied, in a letter published in the journal Sleep in 2016, that the study was a welcome counterpoint, but that he had never claimed segmented sleep was the norm among all pre-industrial peoples everywhere, only in pre-industrial Europe, with its long, cold winter nights. More recently, in 2023, the writer and editor Niall Boyce published a careful critique in the journal Medical History, arguing that the phrases "first sleep" and "second sleep" could be read in other ways, perhaps simply as different phases of the same night's sleep, with any waking very brief. Ekirch replied in 2024, defending his evidence. The conversation goes on.

So it's a real and lively debate. But tonight, in this house, at one in the morning, I'll let the evidence of Chaucer and the diaries guide us. And so here, at last, is the answer to the third of our three mysteries.

I asked you, at the very beginning of the night: why might every person in this house wake up in the very middle of the night, in the deepest dark, and think nothing strange about it at all?

Because, for them, it was not strange. It was simply how a winter night was shaped. They went to bed soon after the fire was covered, perhaps four or five hours after sunset, at a time we'd think of as early evening. They slept their first sleep, deep and heavy, until around midnight. And then, often, they woke, gently, without alarm, into a quiet hour in the middle of the night that had its own name, its own customs, its own small tasks and pleasures. And then, when they were ready, they lay down again, and drifted into their second sleep, which carried them through until the dawn.

With fifteen hours of darkness, there was time for both. And far from being a problem to be solved, that quiet hour in the middle of the night may have been one of the most peaceful parts of their day.

Maud's beads have stopped clicking. Robert and Agnes have gone quiet. William has stopped whispering. The house is settling again, slowly, like snow settling after it's been disturbed.

The second sleep is coming.

## Part Eleven — Around Two in the Morning: The Second Sleep

Here is a small, gentle question for this hour of the night. Now that everyone has woken and the house is settling again, who will be the first to fall back asleep, and who will be the last?

Let's find out together, slowly.

Imagine that you are lying back down on the edge of the children's pallet, and pulling the heavy coverlet up to your chin. The wool is warm from the bodies beside you. The straw beneath you crackles softly and then is still. Your feet, which got cold when you went to the shutter, are warming again now, slowly, the warmth creeping back into your toes. The room is very dark. The moonlight on the wall has climbed higher and grown fainter. The glow on the hearth is a thin, deep red ring around the lid, like the last light in a lantern.

And everything is very, very quiet.

Thomas, of course, never really woke at all. He sat up for his cup of ale, and drank it with his eyes closed, and lay down again, and he's been asleep ever since, curled up on his side in the warm middle of the pallet, his knees drawn up, one hand under his cheek. He's six. When you're six, sleep is easy. So Thomas is first, and he's already far away, dreaming whatever six-year-olds dream about. The dog, perhaps. Or the hens. Or the fountain of sparks that rose from the fire after supper.

Let's set this beside your own home for a moment. Today, many people fall asleep to some kind of sound: a fan, a white-noise machine, an app on a phone playing the sound of rain on a roof, or waves on a shore, or, perhaps, a calm voice telling a long, slow story about a medieval village on a winter night. All of these are, in a way, trying to recreate something that people in this house never had to think about. They had the sound of other people breathing. The creak of a cradle. The slow chewing of a cow beyond the partition. The tiny shifting of the ash on the hearth. These are the oldest sleep sounds there are.

Alice goes next. She's lying on her back, with her eyes open, looking up into the dark at the place where the roof must be, though she can't see it. She is thinking about the boy who fell through the ice. And about what she'll be when she's grown. And about the spindle, and how her thread broke twice tonight and her mother didn't scold her. Her thoughts get slower, and slower, and more and more muddled, the way thoughts do at the edge of sleep, until the boy and the ice and the spindle and the thread are all tangled up together into something soft that doesn't make sense any more. Her eyelids close. Her breathing deepens. Her hand, which was resting on Thomas's shoulder, goes slack.

Alice is asleep.

There's a painting I'd like to describe to you now, very gently, because it's the most famous picture of a winter's night by the fire that survives from the Middle Ages, and I think it belongs in this quiet hour. It's in a book called the Très Riches Heures du Duc de Berry, the Very Rich Hours of the Duke of Berry, one of the most beautiful manuscripts ever made. It was painted in France, mostly in the 1410s, for a great royal duke. And it begins with a calendar, one painting for each month of the year. The painting for February shows a small farmstead in deep snow. In the foreground, there's a little wooden house, with its front wall cut away, so that you can see inside, as if it were a doll's house. And sitting inside, in front of the fire, are three people warming themselves, their clothes pulled up a little to let the heat reach their legs. Outside, in the snow, a man is chopping wood, and another person, wrapped up against the cold, is hurrying away along a path, perhaps towards the village. There are sheep huddled in a fold. There are birds pecking at grain in the snow. It's one of the first paintings in European art to show a landscape covered in snow.

It's a duke's book, made for a very rich man, and painted by artists who may never have spent a night in a house like Robert's. But they looked. They saw. And what they chose to put at the very heart of the winter was not a castle, and not a feast, but a small house, a fire, and a few ordinary people trying to keep warm.

And there's a quiet scholarly puzzle about this painting, too, which I'll share with you now, because it's the gentle kind. The book was begun by three brothers from the Netherlands, known as the Limbourg brothers, Paul, Herman and Johan, who were among the finest painters of their age. But all three brothers were gone by 1416, with the book unfinished. Decades later, in the 1480s, another painter, Jean Colombe, was hired by the Duke of Savoy to complete it. And between those two moments, some art historians believe, another unknown painter worked on some of the pages, and many have suggested that this was an artist named Barthélemy d'Eyck. So which hand painted which month? For the February scene, many experts credit the Limbourg brothers, but the precise number and identity of all the artists who worked on the book remains a matter of debate, and scholars still look closely at every brushstroke to try to tell them apart. It's a lovely, unhurried kind of mystery, the kind that can wait.

William goes next. He's lying on his side, facing the hearth, watching the thin red ring of light, the way his grandmother always does. He is thinking that next year, he'll be big enough to walk behind the plough. He is thinking about the men's voices at the view of frankpledge, and how he stood among them. He's thinking that he's not tired at all. And then, between one thought and the next, he isn't thinking anything any more. His mouth falls a little open. He breathes slow and deep.

William is asleep.

Maud's beads lie still in her lap. She's still sitting up, her coverlet around her shoulders, her chin dropping slowly towards her chest. Then she rouses herself, and lies down, carefully, the way old people do, one stiff joint at a time, onto her side, facing the fire, as she has every night for forty years. She pulls the coverlet up over her shoulder. She looks one last time at the faint red ring around the lid. It's there. It's always there. She murmurs the last few words of a prayer, so softly that nobody could hear them, not even you, lying just a few feet away.

And Maud is asleep.

Let me tell you one small, comfortable, true thing about wool, while the house goes quiet. Wool is a remarkable fibre. Each strand has a natural crimp, a waviness, that traps tiny pockets of air between the fibres, and still air is one of the best insulators there is. Wool can also absorb a good deal of moisture before it starts to feel wet, and it goes on keeping you warm even when it's damp, which is why shepherds and sailors have trusted it for thousands of years. The heavy woollen coverlets on these beds, spun by Agnes and Maud and woven by the village weaver, are doing exactly what modern outdoor clothing does with synthetic fleece. They're holding still, warm air close to the body, all night long. Under that wool, the whole family is lying in a little layer of their own warmth.

In the bed by the partition, Robert has rolled onto his back. He has one arm under his head, and the other around Agnes. He's thinking about the strip in the north field again, oats or beans, beans or oats. And then about Brownie, and the calf she'll have in the spring. And then about the warmth of Agnes beside him, and how good it is to lie here, in the dark, with the work of the day done, and the beasts safe, and the fire alive, and everyone he loves within the reach of his arm. He lets out a long breath, the same long, deep sigh he gave when he sat down by the fire at six o'clock.

And Robert is asleep.

Now only Agnes and Joan are left. Joan is asleep already, of course, deep in her swaddling bands, but she's not settled. She stirs. She makes a small sound. Agnes's hand, almost by itself, goes out to the cradle. Back and forth. Back and forth. Lollai, lollai. The same rhythm. Very slow now. Slower than at midnight. Slower than at supper. The coral bead on its thread swings gently, back and forth.

Joan sighs, a long, shuddering baby sigh, and her little body goes completely limp, and her face goes smooth and peaceful.

Joan is truly asleep.

And Agnes is the last. Of course she is. Mothers often are. She lies there for a while longer, with her hand on the edge of the cradle, listening to the house. She can hear them all. Thomas's quick, light breathing. Alice's slow, soft breathing. William's deeper breath, almost a man's. Maud's faint, whistling, old woman's breath. Robert's slow, heavy breath beside her. Joan's tiny breaths in the cradle. Six people breathing in the dark. And beyond the partition, Brownie, breathing and chewing, and the hens, silent on their beam. And by the hearth, Bran, with a little whimper in his sleep, chasing something through a dream.

She counts them, one by one, the way her mother-in-law counts her beads. All here. All warm. All safe.

Imagine you are lying there too, in the dark, a guest in this house on this one night of all the nights in history, listening with her. The coverlet is heavy and warm. The straw smells of summer. The smoke smells of autumn wood. The air on your face is cold, but the rest of you is warm, wrapped up and held close in a nest of wool and straw and other people's warmth. Your breathing slows. It begins to match theirs. In, and out. In, and out.

Agnes's hand slips from the edge of the cradle and comes to rest on the coverlet.

And Agnes is asleep.

The whole house is sleeping now. Seven people. The second sleep has come, and it will carry them, quietly, deeply, through the last long hours of the dark, until morning.

On the hearth, under its mound of ash and its old cracked lid, the fire sleeps too. Slowly breathing. Faintly glowing. Keeping the warmth of the evening folded up small and safe in its heart, waiting for morning, the way it has done every night in this house for longer than anyone can remember.

Outside, the night goes on without them, in its own slow, unhurried way. The moon is climbing steadily towards the top of the southern sky, where it will stand a little before dawn, and the shadows of the houses are swinging slowly around beneath it, long and blue on the white ground. The frost is still growing. On a clear, still night like this one, the ground goes on losing its warmth to the open sky hour after hour, and the coldest moment of the whole night usually comes not at midnight, but just before dawn. All along the lane, the thatch is furring over with hoarfrost, tiny white feathers of ice growing on every straw, so that by morning every roof in Wharram Percy will look as though it has been dusted with sugar.

Down by the mill pond, the ice is thickening, a fraction of an inch an hour, with small creaks and pings that nobody hears. In the churchyard on the rise, the frost silvers the grass over the graves. An owl drifts across the face of the moon, soundless, its wings spread wide, and settles in the elm by the church. The fox from earlier is curled in a hollow under the hedge, its tail wrapped over its nose. Even the stream seems to be running more quietly, the edges of it locked in ice, only a narrow black ribbon of water still moving down the middle.

And in the houses, one after another, the banked fires breathe under their ash, and the families sleep.

If you could rise up now, above the roof of Robert's house, and look down on the whole valley from the height of the church tower, you would see almost nothing. No lights. No movement. Just the pale roofs, and the black lines of the hedges, and the frozen pond, and the long white swell of the Wolds rolling away in every direction under the moon. You'd hardly know anyone lived there at all. And yet, under every one of those roofs, there are people breathing, and animals breathing, and fires breathing, all through the long cold dark. Dozens of small, warm, living worlds, tucked into the folds of the hills, waiting for the morning.

And outside, very slowly, the stars turn.

## Part Twelve — Around Half Past Seven in the Morning: The Fire Wakes

The last small question of our night is the simplest of all. On a frozen winter morning, in a house like this, what is the very first thing anybody does?

Let's wait and see.

The long dark is ending. It's around half past seven in the morning, and the sun won't properly rise over the eastern ridge until close to eight o'clock. But already, something has changed. The cracks around the shutters, which were black all night, and then silver with moonlight, have turned a soft, faint grey. The moon itself is still up, high in the south-western sky, pale and fading, like a thumbprint on glass. Out in the valley, the cockerels are crowing, one after another, from every yard in the village, the last cockcrow of the night, the one that means morning.

Inside the house, everyone is still asleep. Thomas is sprawled across the pallet now, having kicked half the coverlet off himself and onto his sister. Alice is curled up tight under what's left. William has his arm over his face. Maud is exactly where she was, on her side, facing the hearth. Robert is snoring, gently. Joan is a small, warm bundle in her cradle, her cheeks pink, one fist escaped from her swaddling bands and pressed against her mouth.

And Agnes opens her eyes.

She doesn't need the cockerels to wake her. She has woken at this grey hour every morning of her married life. She lies still for a moment, listening, as she did last night. Everyone's breathing. Everyone's here. Then she pushes back the coverlet, and the cold of the room touches her like a hand, and she pulls her gown over her shift, and wraps a shawl around her shoulders, and goes, barefoot and quick, across the cold floor to the hearth.

That's the answer to our last small question. The very first thing anybody does, on a winter morning in a house like this, is wake the fire.

Imagine you are kneeling beside her now, on the hearth stones, in the grey half-light. The stones are still faintly warm under your knees, after all these hours. In front of you is the low mound of ash, and the old cracked earthenware lid sitting on top of it, exactly where Maud set it down last night. Agnes lifts the lid away and puts it to one side. Then she takes the iron rake, and very gently, the way you might brush snow off something precious, she draws the grey ash back from the top of the mound.

And there they are.

At the heart of the ash, still glowing, are the embers. A small heap of them, not much bigger than two cupped hands. Dull red at the edges, and in the centre, where the air reaches them now for the first time in eleven hours, slowly brightening, orange, then gold. They've been alive all night. All through supper's end and the covering of the fire, through the first sleep and the baby's cry and the midnight moon, through the waking hour and Maud's beads and the second sleep, they've been here, breathing very slowly under their blanket of ash, waiting for this.

Agnes reaches for a handful of dry straw from the basket, and a few thin, dry twigs of hazel. She lays the straw gently on the embers. Then she leans down close, so close that you can feel the heat on her face, and on yours, and she blows. A long, slow, steady breath.

The embers brighten. The straw begins to smoke, a thin white curl of it. She blows again. The smoke thickens, and curls up towards the roof. She blows a third time. And then, with a soft sound, almost like a sigh, the straw bursts into flame.

It's a small flame at first, yellow and flickering. Agnes feeds it the hazel twigs, one by one, and the twigs catch, and crackle. Then a few thicker sticks. Then the half-burned ends of last night's logs, the ones Maud set aside before she banked the fire. They're dry and charred and they catch at once. And within a minute or two, the fire is burning again in the middle of the floor, just as it was at suppertime last night, low and orange and steady, and the light of it is moving on the walls, and on the soot-black rafters, and on the faces of the sleeping children.

The fire is awake. And, one by one, so is the house.

Maud first. She opens her eyes and sees the flames and smiles, the creaky, comfortable smile of an old woman who has seen this fire wake ten thousand times, and has never once stopped being glad of it. Then Thomas, who sits bolt upright and asks if it's morning. Then Alice, who tells him of course it's morning, and to give her back the coverlet. Then William, groaning. Then Robert, who sits up, and rubs his face, and looks at the fire, and looks at Agnes, and nods, as if to say, there, we've done it again. Another night. And Joan, last of all, wakes in her cradle and pulls herself up on its edge and peers out over the rim at the flames with round, astonished eyes, as if she had never seen a fire before.

Let's set this beside your own home, one last time. Perhaps your heating is set on a timer, so that the house begins to warm itself before you wake. Perhaps your coffee machine starts brewing on its own at seven. You might wake to an alarm, or to the light of your phone, and walk into a kitchen that's already warm. Agnes's morning has none of that. But she has something we've mostly lost. She has the knowledge, in her hands and in her bones, of how to keep a small fire alive through the longest night of the winter, and to wake it, with her own breath, in the morning.

The pot goes back on its hook over the fire, with the last of last night's pottage in it, and a little water to loosen it. There's bread from yesterday, and ale. Today, because it's Saint Agnes' day, Agnes cuts a few more slices of bacon from the flitch in the rafters than she usually would, and sets them to sizzle at the edge of the fire on a flat stone.

It makes me think, one last time, of Chaucer's poor widow, in her sooty cottage beside the grove. Chaucer tells us what her meals were like. Her table, he says, was mostly served with milk and brown bread, and in his Middle English, "Seynd bacoun, and somtyme an ey or tweye." Singed bacon, and sometimes an egg or two. That's not so very far from what's on Robert's table this morning, though Brownie is giving no milk this winter, and the hens no eggs.

There's a small, friendly disagreement about mornings, too, that I'll leave you with. You may sometimes read that people in the Middle Ages didn't really eat breakfast, and that churchmen frowned on eating early in the day as a kind of greed. And it's true that some moralists did write that way, and that the main meal of the day, dinner, came at around midday. But other historians, looking at household records and at the lives of working people, point out that men and women who were going out to labour in the cold commonly took something to eat and drink early in the morning, bread and ale, or the warmed-up remains of last night's pottage, and that nobody could plough a field on an empty stomach. As so often, the answer seems to depend on whose life you look at. For this family, on this morning, the answer is simple. There's pottage, and bread, and a little bacon, and the fire to eat it by.

While the bacon sizzles, the house fills up with the ordinary sounds of morning. Thomas wants to know if he can feed the hens. Alice is folding the coverlets and laying them over the bedframe to air. William has gone to the corner to fetch the ale jug and has found a thin skin of ice on the water bucket by the door, the bucket Agnes carried up from the stream at dusk, and he breaks it with his knuckle and shows it to Thomas, a little round window of ice, and Thomas holds it up to the firelight and looks through it, and then licks it, and then drops it. Maud is combing out her long grey hair with a wooden comb, slowly, by the fire, before she pins her headcloth over it for the day. Joan, on her mother's hip, is reaching for everything.

And the house itself is changing, too, as the fire grows. The smoke is gathering again under the thatch, in its soft blue layer, finding its slow way out through the roof. The warmth is spreading out from the hearth, little by little, pushing back the cold into the corners. The soot on the rafters gleams faintly in the new firelight. Another thin layer of it will be laid down today, on top of all the others, the record of one more winter morning in this house, added quietly to the record of all the mornings before.

Up on the rise, the bell of Saint Martin's begins to ring. Not loudly. A small, clear, single bell, carrying across the frosted valley in the still air. It's the feast of Saint Agnes, and the priest will say Mass this morning, and some of the women of the village will climb the frozen path to hear it, with their hoods pulled up and their breath steaming. Agnes, if she can leave the pottage to Maud and the baby to Alice for an hour, means to be one of them. She'll stand at the back of the little stone church, in the cold, in the smell of beeswax and old stone, and say her prayer to the girl whose name she carries, and then hurry home again to the fire.

Robert pulls on his shoes and his hood and his cloak.Robert pulls on his shoes and his hood and his cloak. He goes to the door, and lifts the latch, the same simple wooden bar Agnes checked last night, and swings the door open. And the morning comes in. A blast of bright, bitter cold, and with it, a flood of pale gold light. Because the sun, at last, is lifting over the eastern ridge.

Outside, the whole valley is white with hoarfrost. Every roof, every hedge, every blade of grass, every twig, is furred with tiny feathers of ice, glittering in the low sun. The church on the rise is gold on one side and blue on the other. The mill pond is a sheet of white. And from the thatch of every house in the lane, one by one, as the other families wake their fires too, thin threads of blue smoke are rising, straight up, into the cold, clear, windless air of the morning.

Robert stands in the doorway for a moment, breathing it in. Then he goes out to see to Brownie, and the door swings shut behind him.

And inside, the fire burns on. In the middle of the floor, on its flat hearth of stones, just as it did at dusk last night, just as it will tonight, and tomorrow night, and through all the nights of this long winter. The fire that was covered and kept, and that waited through the dark. The children are gathering around it now, holding out their hands to the warmth. Maud is pulling her bench up close. Agnes is stirring the pot. And the flames rise up, low and orange and steady, and the smoke drifts up through the thatch, and the light of the fire moves softly on every face in the room.

They have survived the night. They knew how. And now, so do you.

Thank you for spending this long winter night with me, and with Robert and Agnes, and Maud, and William and Alice and Thomas and little Joan, in their small, warm, smoky house in the valley of Wharram Percy. If this story helped you rest, or helped you drift off somewhere along the way, I'm truly glad. And if you'd like to come back for more nights like this one, you might consider liking this video and subscribing to the channel, gently, whenever you're ready. There's no hurry at all.

Next time, we'll travel to the other end of the year. We'll spend Midsummer's Eve in a medieval village, the shortest night of all, when the sun barely sets and the bonfires are lit on the hills, and we'll find out how ordinary people kept cool, kept safe, and kept their crops and animals alive through the brief, bright, warm hours of a summer night. I hope you'll join me there.

Until then, sleep well. Keep warm. And may your own fire, whatever it is, still be glowing in the morning.

## How the sources were checked

- **The family is fictional.** Robert, Agnes, Maud, William, Alice, Thomas and Joan, and the animals Brownie and Bran, are invented, and the script says so in Part One. No invented person is presented as appearing in any record.
- **The setting is real.** Wharram Percy deserted medieval village, on the Yorkshire Wolds (North Yorkshire), now in the care of English Heritage. The date is 20 January 1340 (Julian calendar), the eve of the feast of St Agnes.
- **How quotes were checked.** In this session, direct page fetches were blocked by the network policy, so each quotation and record was checked through web-search results that show or summarise the text on the pages listed below. Every verbatim quotation in the script matched at least one edition or transcription in those results. Anything that could not be matched word for word is either paraphrased and labelled as a paraphrase in the narration, or left out. Before publishing, it is still worth checking each verbatim line against the print edition named here.
- **What was cut for lack of verification.** These were researched but left out: a Walter of Henley line about the old horse and the ox (only paraphrased); a cradle-fire case from the coroners' rolls; Hanawalt's statistics; a Bartholomaeus Anglicus passage on swaddling; and the "carde and kembe" and "russhes to pilie" phrases from Piers Plowman.
- **Computed, not quoted.** Sunset (about 4:36 pm), sunrise (about 7:57 am), moonrise (about 11:22 pm) and the moon's phase (waning gibbous, about 73% lit) for Wharram Percy on 20–21 January 1340 (Julian) were calculated with the PyEphem astronomical library and given in UTC, which is within about three minutes of local solar time there. The weekday (20 January 1340 was a Thursday, so the first Monday after Twelfth Night fell on 10 January) was computed the same way. The narration says the moon was worked out with a modern model.
