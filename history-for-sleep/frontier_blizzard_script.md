# How Frontier Families Survived Deadly Blizzards in Log Cabins | History for Sleep

## Part One — Around Noon: The Warm Morning

The sun is out, and the snow is softening.

Along the edge of a low log roof, a row of icicles has begun to drip, one bright drop after another, falling onto a crust of old snow that has turned grey and glassy under the warm light. The sky is a pale, washed blue. From a short black stovepipe a thread of woodsmoke rises, and fades. Behind the house, bare oak and ash trees climb a steep bluff. Below it, the Minnesota River lies frozen and white in its wide valley. And beyond the bluffs, the prairie runs out flat and white and endless, like a sheet laid over the whole world.

A clothesline sags under wet laundry, shirts and woollen stockings and a baby's little gowns. A woman in a shawl is pinning up the last of them, her hands red and steaming in the mild air. She stops, and tips her face up to the sun, and closes her eyes, the way you might on the first warm day of spring.

But it is not spring. It is the twelfth of January, 1888. A Thursday. And this is Renville County, Minnesota, on the north side of the Minnesota River valley, near the little prairie town of Sacred Heart.

When most of us picture a log cabin in winter, we picture the old prints and greeting cards. Snow on the roof like icing. Golden lamplight in a window. A sleigh by the door, everything soft and silent and safe.

That is not where we are going today. Not the cozy cabin of the Christmas cards. The house we are about to enter is a real kind of house, on what may be the most famous winter day in the history of the northern plains. Before this afternoon is out, something will come across the prairie that the people who lived through it would spend the rest of their lives trying to describe.

And I should be honest with you, before we go in. If you were set down on this hillside in the clothes you are wearing, and stepped outside at the wrong moment this afternoon, you would not have survived until morning. Not because the people here were tougher than us, but because they knew things. Small, practical, life-saving things.

So today, and tonight, we are going to learn those things. Slowly. One at a time.

And while we do, I'd like you to carry three small mysteries with you.

The first is this. Why would a strong, sensible man, who has walked to his own barn ten thousand times, tie a rope to himself before he crosses a yard he could cross with his eyes shut?

The second. Why might a family be glad, truly glad, to watch the snow pile up against their walls until it buries their windows?

And the third. In a warm house, with the stove burning all night, why would the nails in the roof above the children's beds grow little white beards of frost?

I'll answer each of them in its own time. The first before the afternoon is out. The second in the early part of the night. And the third when the house is very nearly asleep. There's no hurry. We have the whole night.

Now, before we lift the latch, I want to be clear with you about something, because it matters on this channel. The family you are about to meet is invented. Their names, their ages, their habits and their kindnesses, I have made up. But almost everything around them is real. The valley is real. The storm is real, and so is the date, and so are the weather records, the newspapers, the laws and the letters and the memoirs that I'll tell you about as we go, with names and dates, so that you can look them up yourself, if you ever want to, on some quiet afternoon. And I'll tell you now, so that you can rest easy: this family is going to be all right. This is the story of how they got through the night.

So. Let's meet them.

The woman at the clothesline is Ingeborg Moen. She's thirty-six years old. She was born in a valley in Norway, between mountains so steep that the sun did not reach her parents' farmyard for weeks in the dead of winter, and she came to America as a girl of fifteen, on a crowded steamship, with a wooden trunk painted with flowers. She has been married for fifteen years, and she has never once seen a mountain since.

Her husband, Halvor, is thirty-nine. He's down in the river bottom this morning, with their eldest son, cutting and hauling firewood, because a mild day in January is a gift, and you don't waste a gift. They have a pair of oxen, a patient red-and-white pair called Store and Lille, which means Big and Little, though in truth there is not much difference between them. They have a cow called Dagros, a few hens, a pig, and a big shaggy farm dog called Bamse, which is Norwegian for something like Teddy Bear, and which suits him perfectly.

Inside the house, sitting nearest to the stove, as she always does, is Halvor's mother, Gunhild. She's sixty-six, a widow, small and straight-backed, with a face as brown and creased as a walnut. She came over the ocean late in life, at fifty-five, to be with her son, and she has never quite forgiven America for being so flat.

Then there are the children. Ole is fourteen, and he is with his father in the woods, swinging an axe that is a little too big for him and pretending it isn't. Marit is eleven, quick and watchful, her mother's right hand. Knut is seven, and he is the one who talks to the dog as if the dog might answer. And in a wooden cradle beside the stove, wrapped up snugly in wool, is Sigrid, who is ten months old and has only just learned to pull herself up on the edge of the cradle and look out at the world.

Seven people. Two rooms, more or less, and a loft. One stove.

Normally, on a Thursday in January, Marit and Knut would be at the little country schoolhouse a mile and a half away across the fields. But this morning, when Ingeborg woke and felt the softness in the air, she made a decision that mothers have made since the beginning of time. It was washday weather. The kind you might not see again until March. So she kept the two of them home, to haul water and stir the boiler and turn the wringer. Marit was delighted. Knut was not. And neither of them has the faintest idea yet how much that small decision is going to matter today.

Here is a small question to begin with, one that we'll answer before this part is done. Out here, where most of the land is open prairie, why would a family choose to build their house on a steep, awkward hillside above the river, instead of out on the good flat farmland?

Imagine, now, that you are standing beside Ingeborg at the clothesline. You're warm. That's the strangest part. You came expecting a Minnesota January, and instead the air on your face feels like April. It smells of wet wood and woodsmoke and thawing earth. Somewhere down in the trees, you can hear the steady chock, chock of an axe, and the creak of oxen in their yoke. Water is trickling under the snow crust at your feet. A chickadee is calling from the oaks. And when you turn to look at the house, you see that it is not the house of the Christmas cards at all.

It's built of logs, yes. But they are not the smooth, straight, golden logs of the pictures. They are oak, mostly, cut from the bluff behind the house, some of them left round with the bark peeled off, some of them hewn flat on the inside with a broad axe. They are thick and dark and a little crooked, the way oak trees are. At the corners, the ends of the logs are notched and locked together, each one cut to fit the one below, so that the weight of the walls holds the whole house tight. And between the logs, every gap has been stuffed and smeared with chinking, a mixture of clay and lime and a little straw, grey and cracked in places, patched every autumn.

I can tell you that this is a real kind of house because a house very much like it still stands, or stood within living memory, in this same township. When it was documented for the National Register of Historic Places, the National Park Service described a settler's cabin in Sacred Heart Township, in a hilly, wooded area near the Minnesota River, about fifteen feet by eighteen feet, built of round oak logs joined at the corners with dovetail joints. That's not the Moens' house. Theirs is invented, and it's a little larger, with a lean-to kitchen built onto the back. But the wood, the place, and the way the logs were laid come straight from that record.

And that is the answer to our first small question. Wood.

Out on the open prairie, there was almost none. Small groves of cottonwood might grow along a creek, but nothing like enough to build a house. And so, across a great deal of the northern plains, settlers built their first homes out of the ground itself, cutting the tough prairie sod into long bricks and stacking them into walls. Not far from here, near the town of Sanborn, a local farmer has rebuilt a sod house on the prairie, and he has said that there were thirteen sod houses in that one neighbourhood alone, just counting the ones that people remember. But here, on the bluffs above the Minnesota River, the valley sides were wooded, with oak and ash and elm and basswood. A man with an axe and a strong pair of oxen could build a house of logs. He would have firewood all winter, just a short haul down the slope. The bluff would break the worst of the northwest wind. And the river would give water, if the well ever failed. So a hillside that looks awkward to us, looked to Halvor Moen like the best piece of land in the county.

Now, how did he come to have it at all?

On the twentieth of May, 1862, in the middle of the Civil War, President Abraham Lincoln signed a law that changed the lives of millions of people, including, a generation later, the Moens. It was called the Homestead Act. Its terms were simple, and astonishing. Any adult citizen, or anyone who had declared their intention to become one, who had never borne arms against the government of the United States, could claim a quarter section, a hundred and sixty acres, of surveyed public land. They paid a fee when they filed, ten dollars, the Act says, plus some small office charges on top. Then they had to live on the land, and work it, for five years. The law says the land was for, and I'm quoting the Act itself here, "actual settlement and cultivation." At the end of five years, if they could show that they had built a home and broken the soil and stayed, the land was theirs, for good.

The first claim under the new law was filed on the first of January, 1863, by a man named Daniel Freeman, near Beatrice, in Nebraska. His land is now part of the Homestead National Historical Park. And over the following decades, homesteaders across the country would file something like one and a half million successful claims, covering roughly ten percent of all the land in the United States.

And here is our first place where historians disagree.

For a long time, the Homestead Act was told as a simple, shining story, free land for free people, the American dream written into law. But in 1936, the historian Paul Wallace Gates published a famous essay arguing that the law had been badly undermined. Much of the best land in the West, he pointed out, had gone not to homesteaders at all, but to railroads, to speculators, and to land companies, and many homestead claims were made fraudulently, by people who never meant to farm. For decades after, that sceptical view was the standard one. Then, more recently, a team of historians, Richard Edwards, Jacob Friefeld and Rebecca Wingo, in a 2017 book called Homesteading the Plains, went back to the land records and argued that the Act worked far better than Gates had believed, especially out on the Great Plains, where a great many real families did prove up their claims and stay. The argument is still going on. But tonight, I'll lean towards the second view. Halvor filed his claim in the spring of 1878, built this house that summer, and proved up five years later. The paper that says so is in the trunk with the painted flowers.

Let's look at all this the way a modern visitor would. In your own home, the walls are probably built around insulation, and the windows are double-glazed, and a thermostat on the wall quietly keeps the air at sixty-eight degrees or so, all winter long, without anyone thinking about it. The Moens' house has walls of solid oak about a foot thick, which hold heat well once they are warm, and three small windows of real glass, each one a single thin pane, which do not hold heat at all. There is no insulation in the modern sense. There is no thermostat. There is one black cast-iron stove, standing in the middle of the main room, and everything in this house, every warm hand, every hot meal, every dry stocking, comes from that stove.

Remember it. We'll be spending a great deal of time with it before the night is through.

Ingeborg pins the last stocking to the line. From the trees below, she hears Halvor shout to the oxen, and the creak and scrape of the loaded sledge starting up the trail towards the house. It's time for dinner. Out here, dinner is the big meal of the day, and it comes at noon.

She picks up the empty basket, and turns towards the door. Above the bluffs, the sky is still blue. The air is still warm. And the icicles are still dripping, one bright drop after another, onto the snow.

## Part Two — Around One O'Clock: Dinner by the Black Stove

Here is the small question for this part of the day. In 1888, with no radio, no television and no telephone in the house, how did a family like this one find out what the weather was going to do tomorrow?

Hold that thought. Because first, it's dinnertime, and the whole family is coming in.

Halvor and Ole have left the oxen standing in the yard, still yoked to the sledge, with its load of oak and ash logs, and they come stamping in at the door, knocking wet snow off their boots. Halvor is a big, quiet man, with a reddish beard and enormous hands that have cut down, by his own reckoning, more trees than there are people in the county. Ole is pink in the face and very pleased with himself, because today, for the first time, his father let him fell a tree on his own, a young ash, and it went down exactly where he said it would.

Bamse the dog comes in behind them, wet to the belly, and is sent straight back out again.

Imagine you are sitting at the table now, on the long bench against the log wall, with Knut squeezed in beside you. The room is warm, almost too warm, because Ingeborg has had the stove going hard all morning, heating water for the wash. The air is damp and smells of lye soap and woodsmoke and boiled potatoes. The windows are fogged. And there, in the middle of the room, black and solid and ticking softly to itself as it heats and cools, is the stove.

Let's look at it properly, because it is the heart of this house.

It's a cast-iron cooking range, about as long as a kitchen table and a little lower. Its whole body is made of heavy iron plates, cast in sand moulds in a foundry somewhere far to the east, and bolted together. On the left, behind a small iron door, is the firebox, where the wood burns. Under the firebox is the ash pan, which slides out like a drawer. Across the top are four round iron lids, set into holes in the cooking surface, and you lift them off with a separate little iron handle, called a lid lifter, which hooks into a notch in each lid. Take a lid off, and the fire itself is right there, under your pot. Leave it on, and the iron surface heats evenly, hottest over the firebox and cooler towards the right. In the middle of the stove, behind a larger door, is the oven. On the right-hand end there is a deep iron tank called a reservoir, which holds several gallons of water, and which warms whenever the stove is lit, so that there is always hot water in this house, as long as there is fire. Above the cooking surface, on two iron legs, is a little shelf with a door, the warming closet, where bread rises and plates are kept warm.

From the back of the stove, a black pipe rises straight up through the ceiling and the loft and out through the roof. Partway up that pipe, there is a small flat iron plate inside it that turns on a handle, called the damper. Turn it one way, and the smoke rushes up and the fire roars. Turn it the other, and the draught slows, and the fire settles and burns low and long. That handle, as we'll see, is one of the most important things in this house tonight.

The stove has some brass and nickel trim along its edges, and a maker's name cast proudly into the oven door. Once a week, Marit rubs the whole thing down with black stove polish, until the iron shines like a beetle's back.

This stove is the newest and most expensive thing the Moens own. When they first came to this hillside, in the spring of 1878, they cooked over an open fire in the yard, and then on a small, cheap box stove, hardly more than an iron crate with a lid on top. This range came later, after a good wheat harvest, hauled home on the wagon from the railroad, wrapped in sacking. Ingeborg cried when it was set up. Gunhild sniffed at it, and said that in Norway her own mother had cooked for forty years on an open hearth in the corner of the room, and nobody had ever starved. Then, within a week, she had claimed the chair nearest to the oven door, and she has never given it up since.

Here is a small, true thing about stoves like this one. By the 1880s, a family on a farm in western Minnesota did not need to travel to a city to buy almost anything. Since 1872, a Chicago merchant named Aaron Montgomery Ward had been selling goods by mail, starting with a single printed sheet and growing, year by year, into a thick catalogue of hundreds of pages. A family could choose stoves, lamps, tools, cloth and buttons by post, and have them sent by railroad to the nearest station. A few years after that, in 1886, a young railroad station agent named Richard Sears, working at North Redwood, a little way down this same river valley, began selling watches by mail too, and his company would one day grow into one of the largest catalogue businesses in the world. Out here, the catalogue was a window onto the whole of America.

On the shelf above the table, beside the family Bible, the hymnbook and a Norwegian almanac, a little wooden clock ticks. And that clock is telling a time that would have puzzled Halvor's own father. Until a few years ago, every town in America kept its own local time, set by the sun, so that noon in one town might be several minutes different from noon in the next. Then, on the eighteenth of November, 1883, the railroads of the United States and Canada switched all at once to a system of standard time zones. People called it the Day of Two Noons, because in many towns the clocks struck twelve, and then were set back, and struck twelve again. The Moens' clock keeps railroad time, Central time. And by that clock, the sun reaches its highest point in the sky over this valley a little before half past twelve, because Sacred Heart lies well to the west of the line that Central time is measured from.

Now, dinner.

Ingeborg serves it straight from the stove. There's a big pot of boiled potatoes in their skins, and a pan of fried salt pork, and a jug of buttermilk, and dark rye bread, and a stack of flatbrød, the thin, dry, crackling flatbread of Norway that keeps for months and that Gunhild bakes on the stovetop every autumn. There's coffee, too, strong and black, from the pot that sits on the back of the stove from dawn to dark. If you ask almost any Norwegian-American family of this time what they could not live without, the answer might well be coffee.

They pray, all together, in Norwegian. And then they eat, and they talk about the weather. Because everybody, on this day, is talking about the weather.

The warmth has been strange. It came on in the night, a soft southerly air after a long run of bitter cold, and this morning the snow on the south side of the roof was sliding off in slabs. Halvor says it feels like a thaw. Gunhild says she doesn't like it. She says that in her girlhood, in the mountains, the old people used to say that a warm day in the heart of winter was the weather taking a breath, and that you should watch to see what it did next. Ole says that's superstition. Gunhild says perhaps it is, and that she'll watch anyway.

And that brings us back to our small question. How did a family like this one know what the weather would do?

The honest answer is that, mostly, they didn't. They read the sky, as Gunhild does. They watched the wind, and the behaviour of the animals, and the colour of the sunset. And they remembered.

But there was something else, and it was quite new. Since 1870, the United States Army had run a national weather service, under its Signal Corps. Trained observers in towns across the country took readings of temperature, pressure, wind and sky, several times a day, and sent them by telegraph to the central office in Washington, and later to regional offices as well. From those reports, officers drew up forecasts, which the Signal Service called indications, and sent them back out again by telegraph, to be posted in railway stations and post offices, printed in newspapers, and sometimes flown as coloured flags. There was even a special signal for a sudden severe drop in temperature, which was called a cold wave.

So the answer to our small question is this. You read the sky yourself. And if you lived in a town with a telegraph office, or you went in for the mail, you might read what the Army thought the sky would do. On a hillside farm, a mile and more from the nearest neighbour, with no telegraph and no newspaper delivered that morning, the Moens would know nothing at all about any forecast for today. They have only the sky.

And the forecast for today has its own story.

A few months before this day, the Chief Signal Officer of the Army, a man named Adolphus Greely, had sent a young officer, Lieutenant Thomas Woodruff, to open a new, experimental forecasting office in St. Paul, Minnesota, so that predictions for the northwest could be made closer to where the weather actually was. Woodruff had already written a study of cold waves and how they moved, published in 1885. On the eleventh and twelfth of January, 1888, he was watching a powerful storm system come down out of western Canada, and the forecasts that went out from St. Paul did predict colder weather. One version of his wording, as it's often quoted, says that a cold wave was indicated for Dakota and Nebraska, tonight and tomorrow. I'll treat that wording as approximate, because I haven't been able to check it against the original telegram.

And here is our point of disagreement, and it's a sharp one.

The Minnesota Historical Society's account of the storm says plainly that the Signal Corps did not issue a cold wave warning for Minnesota on the night before, and it counts that lack of warning, together with the mild morning, as one of the main reasons so many people were caught outdoors. The National Weather Service's own heritage pages say much the same, and they note that the failures of the Signal Service in this storm, and in another great storm later that same year, helped persuade Congress to take weather forecasting away from the Army altogether. In his 2004 book The Children's Blizzard, the writer David Laskin gives a long, careful account of Woodruff's decisions, and he is especially hard on Greely, the Chief Signal Officer, who comes across in many readings of the book as a rigid, difficult commander who made the forecasters' work harder. Other readers and reviewers point out that Laskin leaves the deepest question open: whether any forecast, in 1888, could have reached the farms and schoolhouses in time to make a difference. The telegraph lines themselves went down in many places as the storm arrived. And a forecast posted at a railway depot does nothing for a boy walking home across a field. So, did the forecasters fail? Partly. Perhaps. It depends on what you think a forecast could have done, in a world with no telephones in the farmhouses.

Let's pause here and think about your own life. If you want to know tomorrow's weather, you probably take a small glass rectangle out of your pocket, and within a second you have hourly temperatures, wind speed, and a moving radar map of every cloud for a thousand miles, updated every few minutes from satellites in orbit. You may even get a loud alarm on your phone, without asking for it, if a dangerous storm is on its way. Every one of those things was invented, in part, because of days like this one.

Out to the west of the Moens, in western Minnesota, in a place called Fortier, there was a teenage boy named Carl Saltee. Many years later, he remembered this very afternoon, and the Minnesota Historical Society has preserved his recollection. I'll paraphrase it. He remembered that it was so warm that the snow and ice melted off the window, and that it stayed warm until after one o'clock in the afternoon. Then, by about half past three, he saw something building up in the northwest. A dark, heavy wall, coming fast.

But that is later. For now, at the Moens' table, it's still warm, and the coffee is hot, and the clock on the shelf says ten minutes past one.

Halvor finishes his coffee and pushes back his chair. He and Ole will go back down for one more load of wood before the light starts to go, he says. Ingeborg nods. The washing should be dry by three, in this sun. Marit begins to clear the plates. Knut slips a crust of bread under the table, where Bamse, who has crept back in when no one was looking, takes it very gently from his fingers. Little Sigrid, in her cradle by the warm side of the stove, has fallen asleep with her fist against her cheek.

Gunhild has gone to the window. She wipes a clear patch in the fog on the glass with the side of her hand, and looks out to the northwest, over the bluff. The sky there is still blue. But it is a different blue now, she thinks. A little paler. A little whiter. As though somebody very far away had breathed on it.

She says nothing yet. She simply goes back to her chair beside the oven door, and picks up her knitting, and watches the window.

## Part Three — Around Half Past Two: The Wall in the Northwest

Here is the small question for this part of the afternoon. What, exactly, is a blizzard? And how is it different from an ordinary heavy snowstorm, of the kind that most of us have seen and even enjoyed?

We'll have our answer before this hour is out. But first, the sky is changing.

It has been changing, very slowly, for about an hour. Gunhild noticed it first, at the window after dinner. Now Ingeborg notices it too, when she goes out to feel the washing on the line. The sun is still shining, but it has gone pale and small, and there is a faint ring of light around it, like a halo. The blue in the northwest has turned milky. The air is still mild, but it has gone very still. The icicles have stopped dripping. The chickadees in the oaks have gone quiet. And the hens, which were scratching in the thawed mud by the woodpile half an hour ago, have gone into the henhouse of their own accord, in the middle of the afternoon, which hens do not do.

That ring around the sun is a real thing, and it has a real cause. It appears when a thin, high veil of cloud spreads across the sky, so high that it is made not of water droplets but of tiny six-sided crystals of ice. Sunlight passing through those crystals is bent by a particular angle, about twenty-two degrees, and so it forms a pale circle around the sun, always the same size, as if someone had drawn it with a compass. Those high icy veils are often the very first sign of a large storm system approaching, sometimes hundreds of miles ahead of it. Country people all over the northern world noticed this long before anyone could explain it. There is an old English saying, in many versions, that a ring around the sun or the moon means rain or snow is coming soon. Gunhild knows a Norwegian version of the same idea. She has never owned a barometer in her life. But she has owned a sky.

Ingeborg stands by the clothesline with a stocking in her hand, and she looks for a long moment at the northwest. Then she begins to unpin the washing. Not hurriedly. Just steadily, one piece after another, folding each one over her arm, damp or not.

Marit comes out to help her, and asks why. Ingeborg says she doesn't know why. She just has a feeling. And Marit, who is eleven and has lived her whole life on this hillside, looks at the sky, and doesn't argue.

Imagine you are standing beside them now, on the trampled snow by the line. Turn your face to the northwest. Above the bluff, along the whole edge of the sky, there is something that wasn't there before. It's low and long and grey, and at first you might take it for a distant range of hills, or a bank of fog lying out on the prairie. But there are no hills out there. There is nothing out there, for a hundred miles, but flat white land. And the grey thing is moving. As you watch, its top edge seems to boil and curl, very slowly, like smoke rising from a fire that is too far away to see. The sun is still on your face. The air is still soft. And the grey wall is growing taller.

Down in the river bottom, Halvor has seen it too. He has stopped work, and is standing with his axe on his shoulder, looking up through the bare trees at the light. He doesn't like the stillness. He doesn't like the way the oxen are shifting and lowing in their yoke. He has lived through ten Minnesota winters, and he has learned, the hard way, to trust the animals' opinion about the weather. So he tells Ole to leave the last log where it lies, and they turn the oxen for home, with half a load.

On the steep trail up the bluff, Ole asks his father whether it's a storm. Halvor says he thinks so. Ole asks if it'll be a bad one. Halvor says that he doesn't know, and that the best thing to do with a storm you don't know is to be indoors before it arrives. Then he puts his hand on the boy's shoulder, and they walk faster.

Now, our small question. What is a blizzard?

Today, the National Weather Service in the United States has a precise definition. A blizzard is a storm with sustained winds, or frequent gusts, of thirty-five miles an hour or more, together with falling or blowing snow that cuts visibility to less than a quarter of a mile, for three hours or longer. Notice what is in that definition, and what is not. It says nothing about how much snow falls. It says nothing, even, about how cold it is. A blizzard is not really about snow at all. It's about wind. It's about the air itself filling up with ice, so thickly and so violently that you cannot see, and you cannot breathe easily, and you cannot tell up from down, or north from south, or your own barn from the open prairie.

In a heavy snowstorm, the flakes come down softly and pile up. In a blizzard on the open plains, the wind picks up the snow that is falling, and the snow that fell last week, and the snow that fell last month, and drives all of it sideways, ground into tiny, hard, sharp grains of ice, as fine as flour, or as sand. One writer on the Great Plains described the blown ice crystals as being more like little knives than snowflakes. They get into everything. Into your eyes and your nose and your mouth. Into the gaps around a door, and the cracks in a log wall, and the keyhole of a lock.

So that's the answer to our small question. A snowstorm is weather that falls. A blizzard is weather that blows.

And the blizzard that is coming now, across the northwest horizon towards the Moens' hillside, has been travelling for a day and a half.

Meteorologists who have studied it since describe a powerful area of low pressure that came down out of Alberta, in western Canada, on the eleventh of January. Ahead of it, warm air was drawn up from the south, which is why this morning has felt like spring. And behind it, pouring down from the Arctic, came an enormous mass of bitterly cold air, held back by nothing, with no mountains and no forests to slow it, only the open, level plains. Where the warm air and the cold air met, there was a line, a front, and along that line the wind rose to a gale and the temperature collapsed. The storm struck Montana in the early hours of the twelfth. It crossed Dakota Territory from the middle of the morning to the early afternoon. It reached Lincoln, Nebraska, at about three o'clock. One account says it travelled seven hundred and eighty miles in seventeen hours. And the cold air behind it was so deep and so heavy that, before it finally faded away, it had pushed all the way south through Texas, as far as Veracruz, on the coast of Mexico.

We have a record of it passing over one small Signal Service station in the Black Hills of Dakota, and I want to tell you about it, because it's the kind of plain, everyday record that this channel loves. The Army's weather office there had just moved from the mining town of Deadwood to Rapid City, and had only begun taking observations on the first of January, less than two weeks before. It was in a building on the southwest corner of Main and Seventh Streets. The National Weather Service office in Rapid City has gone back to the old station log for that day. According to their account of it, snow began falling there in the early hours of the twelfth, and stopped at about twenty past eleven in the morning. Then the temperature began to fall, steadily, hour after hour, with sustained north winds reaching thirty miles an hour. By eight o'clock that evening, it was ten degrees below zero. By five the next morning, twenty-one below. Somewhere in Rapid City, a soldier with a pencil wrote those numbers down, three or four times a day, because that was his job, and because he did not yet know that anyone would care.

Let's think for a moment about how this compares with your own life. If you have ever driven a car at seventy miles an hour on a highway, you have travelled about as fast as the strongest winds in this storm. Now imagine that wind, not inside a car, but on your skin, and loaded with sand-fine ice, and at a temperature far below zero. A modern house in the northern states and Canada is built, by law, to withstand winds like these. Its walls are wrapped, its windows are sealed, and its builders have tables and codes that tell them exactly how strong every joint must be. The Moens' house was built by Halvor and two neighbours in a single summer, by eye and by experience. It is going to have to be enough.

And here is our point of disagreement for this hour. Because it is fair to ask why so many families were out here at all, on the open northern plains, living in places where storms like this could happen.

Part of the answer is land, which we talked about earlier. But part of it was promises. Through the middle of the nineteenth century, railway companies, land agents and state boosters wrote glowing descriptions of the western prairies, and Minnesota, in particular, was advertised for years as a wonderfully healthy place, with a dry, clear, bracing winter air that was said to be good for people with weak lungs. Further west, on the drier plains, a more startling idea took hold. In 1881, a land promoter and amateur scientist named Charles Dana Wilber put into print a phrase that would become famous: "rain follows the plow." The idea was that breaking the prairie and planting crops and trees would actually change the climate itself, and bring more rain to the plains. It was supported, for a time, by some genuine scientists, including Cyrus Thomas, who worked on the government's survey of the western territories. But others, most famously the explorer and geologist John Wesley Powell, warned that this was wishful thinking, and in his 1878 Report on the Lands of the Arid Region of the United States he argued that much of the West was simply too dry to farm in the ordinary way. History, in the end, has largely sided with Powell. The plough did not bring the rain. But for a generation, the argument was fierce, and it helped to draw hundreds of thousands of hopeful families onto land whose weather they did not yet understand.

The Moens did not come because of any promise about rain. They came because Halvor's cousin wrote to them from here, in a letter full of spelling mistakes and good cheer, saying that there was wood and water and land, and that the winters were cold but the air was so dry that you hardly felt it. Gunhild still has that letter. She has opinions about the sentence on the winters.

It's nearly half past three now.

The oxen come up over the lip of the bluff, steaming and blowing, with Halvor walking at their heads and Ole behind the sledge. And as they reach the yard, the light changes all at once. The sun goes out. Not behind a cloud exactly. It simply dims, from gold to brass to a dull white coin, and then it is gone, and the whole hillside is in a strange grey half-light, like dusk at the wrong time of day. The wall in the northwest is no longer on the horizon. It is enormous. It fills half the sky. Its face is dark grey, almost blue, and it is coming over the prairie towards them faster than any horse could run.

And then, for the first time, they hear it. A low sound, very far off, like a train on a distant track, or like the sea. It's the wind.

Halvor moves quickly now, but he doesn't run. He unhooks the sledge and leaves it where it stands. He leads the oxen straight across the yard to the barn, a low building of logs and sod set into the slope about forty paces from the house, and Ole runs ahead to swing the door open. Ingeborg has the last of the washing in her arms. Marit has the basket and the clothes pins. Knut is standing in the doorway of the house with his mouth open, staring at the sky, until Gunhild takes him firmly by the collar and draws him inside.

And then Halvor does a curious thing. He comes out of the barn, and before he goes to the house, he stops at a stout post by the barn door, and he looks at the coil of heavy rope that hangs on a wooden peg there, the rope he bought in town last autumn, and has never yet used.

He looks at it for just a moment. Then he looks at the sky. And he reaches up and takes it down.

## Part Four — Around a Quarter to Four: The Rope

Here is the small question for this hour. When a storm like this one arrives, how quickly does a warm afternoon disappear? Minutes? Hours? We'll find out together, before this part is done.

Halvor has the coil of rope over his shoulder. It's thick hemp rope, the kind used for hauling and for tying down loads, and there are a good many yards of it. He walks quickly to the corner of the house nearest the barn, where an iron ring is driven deep into the end of a log at about the height of his chest. He bought that ring and set it there two winters ago, after a neighbour told him a story he didn't like. He has never used it until now. He knots one end of the rope through the ring, pulls it hard, and pulls it again. Then he walks backwards across the yard towards the barn, paying the rope out as he goes, keeping it off the ground, until he reaches the stout post by the barn door. He passes the rope around the post, draws it tight, so that it hangs across the yard in a long, slightly sagging line, about as high as a man's waist, and ties it off.

Ole watches all this from the barn door, with the oxen safely inside behind him. He doesn't ask why. He's fourteen, and he has decided that this is a day for not asking questions.

And then the wind arrives.

It does not come gradually, the way a breeze picks up on a summer afternoon. It comes like a door slamming. One moment, the air in the yard is still, and grey, and strangely quiet. The next, there is a roar, and a blow like a hand in the chest, and the whole world goes white.

Imagine you are standing in the doorway of the house now, with Gunhild's hand on your arm, looking out. A moment ago you could see the barn, forty paces away, and the woodpile, and the sledge, and the bare oaks on the bluff. Now you can see nothing. Not the barn. Not the woodpile. Not the corner of the house a few feet from your own shoulder. There is only a seething, roaring wall of white, moving so fast that it seems to stand still, like the water of a waterfall. Snow is pouring in through the doorway past your legs, as fine as flour, and settling on the floor in little hissing drifts. The wind is so loud that you could shout, and Gunhild, standing beside you, would not hear a word. The cold comes in with it, and it is not the cold of this morning, or of this afternoon. It is a different cold entirely, a hard, dry, biting cold that seems to go straight through your clothes and into your bones. And somewhere out there, in all that white, are Halvor and Ole.

Gunhild pulls you back, and closes the door, and drops the wooden bar across it.

And in the barn, Halvor and Ole are fastening the barn door shut. Halvor checks the oxen. He checks Dagros the cow, and her calf. He forks hay into the mangers, fast and rough, and makes sure there is water in the trough. And then he takes Ole by the wrist, and puts the boy's hand on the rope, just outside the barn door, and closes his own big hand over it.

Don't let go, he tells him, his mouth close to Ole's ear. Whatever happens. Don't let go.

They step out into the white.

Ole will tell his own grandchildren about the next minute, many years later. How he could not see his own hand on the rope in front of his face. How the wind was so strong that it pushed him sideways off his feet, and he hung from the rope with both hands while his father hauled him upright. How the snow packed into his eyes, and froze his eyelashes together, and filled his nose so that he had to breathe through his mouth, and then filled his mouth. How he could not tell, for those forty paces, whether he was walking towards the house or away from it, or whether he was walking at all. And how, at the end, the rope simply stopped, at the iron ring, and his father's hand found the door, and beat on it, and the bar lifted, and there was firelight, and his mother, and the smell of coffee, and Gunhild brushing the snow from his face with her apron, and saying something in Norwegian that he had never heard her say before and never would again.

So here, at last, is the first question I asked you at the beginning of the day. Why would a strong, sensible man, who has walked to his own barn ten thousand times, tie a rope to himself, or to his house, before crossing a yard he could cross with his eyes shut?

The answer is that in a prairie blizzard, a man with his eyes open is blind, and a yard he knows by heart becomes as strange to him as the open sea.

In a whiteout like this one, there is no up or down, no near or far. There are no shadows, because the light comes from every direction at once. There is no horizon. The snow is moving so fast and so thickly that the eye has nothing to hold on to. The wind is so loud that you cannot hear the barn animals, or a voice calling from the house, or even the crunch of your own feet. You lose your sense of direction in a matter of steps. People who have lived through storms like this one, on the plains, describe turning around once, to shield their faces from the wind, and then not knowing which way they had been walking. And a man who walks the wrong way from his barn, on the open prairie, may walk past his own house by ten yards and never see it, and keep on walking, out into the empty white, until he can walk no more.

The rope is the answer to that. It does not need eyes. It goes from the door of the barn to the wall of the house, and as long as your hand is on it, you cannot be lost. A strong rope, strung waist-high across the yard before the storm comes, is the difference between a short, frightening walk, and something far worse.

We know this was a real practice, and we know it from the people who did it. Families all across the northern plains strung lines like this between their houses and their barns. A woman named Birdie McDonald, who grew up on a homestead in Dakota, remembered it from her own childhood, and her recollection is quoted by the National Park Service at Wind Cave National Park, in South Dakota. I'll quote a few of them exactly, with her own spelling. She remembered "one terrible blizzard," and her father "tieing the clothes line to one corner of the sod house," and carrying the other end out to the sod barn, so that he and the older boys could get back and forth to look after the milk cows and the oxen. In Kansas, local historians writing about another great storm, the blizzard of January 1886, noted that farmers who had strung a rope line out to their barns came through it, and that many of those who were caught out in the open did not. On another homestead in South Dakota, the Brown family remembered blizzards so blinding that they had to use a rope tied between the house and the barn, so that they would not get lost and freeze on the way to do the chores. Different families. Different years. The same rope.

That's the first of our mysteries answered.

And now, our small question for this hour. How quickly does the warm afternoon disappear?

The answer is: astonishingly quickly. In Rapid City, as we heard, the cold came on steadily over the afternoon and evening. But further east and south, where the front arrived at full strength, people remembered it as almost instantaneous. History Nebraska's account of the storm describes the wind there rising, shortly after noon, to fifty-six miles an hour, and the temperature dropping within a few hours to thirty-six degrees below zero. One Minnesota account says that in the west of the state, and in eastern Dakota, temperatures fell from above freezing to somewhere around forty below. And some accounts claim that, between the warmest moment and the coldest, the temperature fell by nearly a hundred degrees in a single day. I'd treat that last figure with some caution, because it comes from witnesses' memories and later retellings, not from any one thermometer. But even the official readings tell the same story. The warmth of this morning is gone, not in hours, but in minutes. That's the answer to our small question.

And now I want to speak, gently, about something real, because it would not be right to tell this story without it.

This storm came in the middle of a school day, on a mild morning, after a long spell of cold. Across Dakota, Nebraska, Minnesota and Iowa, thousands of children had walked to their little country schoolhouses that morning in light coats, or no coats at all, because the day had seemed so fine. And when the storm struck, in the early or middle afternoon, some teachers kept their pupils inside, around the schoolhouse stove, all through the night, and they all came through. But some children were sent home, or set out on their own, and were caught in the open. Others who died were grown men and women caught in the fields or on the roads. That is why this storm has come to be called the Children's Blizzard, or the Schoolhouse Blizzard. In southwestern Minnesota, the Minnesota Historical Society remembers, among others, a ten-year-old farm boy from Avoca, in Murray County, named Johnny Walsh, who had set out to visit a neighbour on that mild afternoon, and who did not find his way home.

I'll say no more about it than that. But I want you to know that the Moens' warm, lit house, tonight, was not everyone's. And that is part of why we remember this day at all.

Which brings us to our point of disagreement. How many people died in the storm of the twelfth of January, 1888?

The truthful answer is that nobody knows. The National Weather Service, and many other accounts, give a figure of about two hundred and thirty-five. The Minnesota Historical Society, and the weather service's own heritage pages, give a wider range, of between two hundred and fifty and five hundred, and they explain that a precise number has never been settled, partly because not every death was reported, and some were not discovered until the spring thaw. A few estimates go higher still. At the time, the newspapers argued about it furiously. On the sixteenth of January, four days after the storm, the St. Paul Dispatch ran a headline that read, "130: That Is the Awful Total of Dead and Missing." On the same day, the Minneapolis Journal printed, "Nearly 140 Dead." In his book, David Laskin describes how the differing counts turned into what one reviewer of his book called an unseemly brawl between newspapers, with each town and each paper suspected of either exaggerating its losses for sympathy or hiding them to protect its reputation, because no one wanted their county known as a place where people froze. So the toll is still an estimate, and it may stay one forever.

Let's think about our own world for a moment. Today, if a sudden storm like this one swept across the plains, schools would be warned hours in advance, by radio and phone and the internet. School buses would be held. Parents would be sent messages. Farmers would see it coming on radar from their kitchen tables. And if someone were caught out, they could call for help, and a snowplough or a rescue vehicle with tracks and powerful lights could reach them. In 1888, the only warning was the sky, and the only help was a neighbour who knew the way.

Back in the log house on the bluff, it is suddenly, completely dark. Not night yet, though the clock says only half past four. The storm has blotted out the last of the daylight, and the three small windows are nothing now but grey-white squares, shuddering and rattling in their frames, with snow packed against the glass on the outside.

And the house is talking. That is the only way to describe it. Every log in the walls creaks and ticks as the wind leans on it. The stovepipe, where it passes up through the roof, gives a long, low moan, rising and falling, like someone blowing across the mouth of a bottle. And everywhere that the chinking between the logs has cracked, even a little, the wind is finding its way in. Fine snow sifts through the gaps in thin white threads, and lies along the floor beneath the north wall in tiny ridges, like sand on a beach after the tide. Marit and Knut go along the wall with a bundle of old rags and a table knife, pushing rags into every crack they can find, while Ingeborg holds a candle up to show them where the threads of snow are coming in. Gunhild fills the stove's firebox with oak, right to the top, and opens the damper wide, and the iron begins to tick and roar. Halvor sits on the bench by the door with his boots still on, his hands held out towards the stove, flexing his fingers slowly, saying nothing at all. Ole sits beside him, and leans against his father's shoulder, the way he has not done since he was a small boy.

Ingeborg takes down the lamp from its shelf.

## Part Five — Around Five O'Clock: Coal Oil and Lamplight

Here is the small question for this part of the evening. Why does the Moens' lamp have a tall glass chimney on top of it? It's fragile, it's awkward to clean, and it's one more thing to break. What is it for?

Ingeborg sets the lamp down on the table. It's the best lamp in the house, and there are only two. The base is a heavy glass bowl, clear and slightly greenish, about the size of a large grapefruit, and it's about two-thirds full of a pale, thin liquid that smells sharply of something halfway between turpentine and tar. Screwed into the top of the bowl is a brass burner, with a flat cotton wick rising up through a narrow slot, and a tiny brass wheel on the side that winds the wick up and down. And around the burner, sitting in a ring of little brass prongs, there is a tall, slender, curving glass chimney, wide at the bottom, pinched in like a waist above the flame, and open at the top.

Imagine you are sitting at the table now, beside Marit, while her mother gets the lamp ready. It's very dark in the room, except for the red glow leaking around the edges of the stove's firebox door, and the small, nervous flame of the candle. Ingeborg lifts the chimney off and hands it to Marit, who holds it the way you might hold a newborn bird. Then Ingeborg winds the wick up a little, and looks at it closely. The top edge is black and crusty from last night. She takes a small pair of scissors from her apron and trims it, very carefully, straight across, so that the edge is perfectly even. A ragged wick makes a ragged flame, and a ragged flame makes smoke. Then she strikes a match on the iron of the stove, and touches it to the wick.

A small, yellow, flickering flame appears, a bit sooty, a bit uncertain. Ingeborg takes the chimney back from Marit, and sets it gently over the flame, into its brass prongs.

And you see the answer to our small question happen right in front of you. The flame jumps. It stands up straight, and steadies, and turns from a smoky orange to a clear, bright, golden yellow, two or three times brighter than before. The smoke vanishes. The light spreads out across the table and up the log walls, and suddenly you can see everyone's faces, and the grain of the wood, and the frost beginning to form in the corners of the windowpanes.

The chimney is not decoration. It is an engine. As the flame heats the air inside the glass, that hot air rises up and out of the open top, and as it does, it pulls fresh, cool air in through small holes at the base of the burner, steadily, all around the wick. The flame gets a constant, even supply of oxygen, so it burns hotter, cleaner and brighter, and the glass shields it from every draught in the room. Without the chimney, the lamp smokes and flickers. With it, it gives a steady light that you can read by. That's the answer.

The liquid in the bowl is kerosene, though almost everyone out here calls it coal oil. The name is older than the stuff itself. In the middle of the century, before anyone was drilling for petroleum, lamp oil in North America was being distilled from coal and other minerals, and a Canadian geologist named Abraham Gesner gave a name to one such oil, kerosene. Then, on the twenty-seventh of August, 1859, near Titusville, in Pennsylvania, a man named Edwin Drake struck oil with a drilled well, and within a few years, cheap kerosene distilled from petroleum was pouring out across the country, and the world. It was one of the great changes in ordinary human life. For the first time, a farm family, far from any city, could buy a light brighter than many candles, for a few cents a week. People went on calling it coal oil anyway, out of habit, for generations.

Coal oil has rules, and in this house everybody knows them, even Knut. You never fill a lamp while it is lit, or near the open firebox of the stove. You fill it in daylight, if you can, out of the can by the door, using a small tin funnel, and you wipe the bowl dry afterwards. You never let the bowl run nearly empty, because then the wick dries out and chars. You never turn the wick up too high, or the flame will climb, and blacken the chimney with soot, and crack the glass. And you never, ever carry a lit lamp up the ladder into the loft, where the children sleep among the quilts and the straw ticks. Kerosene was far safer than many of the lamp fuels that came before it, but it was still a flame, in a house made of wood, and lamp fires were a real fear in every farmhouse in America. So the rules are simple, and they are kept. Halvor fills the lamps. Ingeborg trims them. Marit washes the chimneys, once a week, in warm soapy water from the stove's reservoir, and polishes them with a soft rag until they squeak.

Here is a small, true thing about the light of this lamp. A single flat-wick kerosene lamp of this kind gives off, by most modern estimates, somewhere around the light of a dozen candles. That sounds like a lot. But a single ordinary modern light bulb in your kitchen gives off very roughly five or six times as much as that. If you sat at the Moens' table tonight with this one lamp burning, the room would feel to you like a cave, golden and shadowy, with the corners lost in darkness. To Ingeborg, who grew up in Norway with candles and the firelight of an open hearth, it is a blaze of light.

And here is another thing about tonight, which I've worked out with a modern astronomy program. On this day, at Sacred Heart, the sun set at about three minutes past five, by railroad time. So it is setting right now, somewhere behind that roaring white wall, though no one in this valley can see it go. And there will be no moon tonight at all. The moon is new, and it will be at its darkest phase in the early hours of tomorrow morning. Even if the sky were clear, the night would be black.

In the corners of the windowpanes, as Marit noticed when the lamp was lit, the frost is growing. Each pane is a single thin sheet of glass, with the roaring cold of the storm on one side and the warm, damp air of the kitchen on the other. Water from the steam of the washing, from the coffee pot, from seven people breathing, drifts against the glass, and freezes onto it. And as it freezes, it doesn't simply make a smooth, flat sheet of ice. It grows. It sends out long, branching, feathery fronds, curling across the glass like ferns, or palm leaves, or the frozen veins of some silver plant, each one spreading outward from a scratch or a speck of dust where the first crystal formed. Knut gets up from the rug to look at them, and puts his fingertip on the glass, and watches a little round hole melt in the forest of ice, and then slowly begin to close over again. By morning, every pane in the house will be thick with frost ferns, so thick that you will not be able to see through them at all.

The stove, meanwhile, has been working hard. With the damper open and the firebox crammed with oak, the iron top is beginning to glow a faint dull red over the firebox, and the heat rolls off it in waves you can feel on your face from across the room. Cast iron is very good at this. It soaks up heat from the fire, and gives it off again, steadily, in every direction, warming the air, and the walls, and the people. The stove is the reason the inside of this house, tonight, will stay a world apart from what is happening outside.

Now, I want to tell you about another set of records from this very day, because, like the Rapid City log, they come from ordinary people doing ordinary work.

In 1881, the United States Signal Corps set up a weather station at the observatory of Carleton College, in Northfield, Minnesota, more than a hundred miles to the east of the Moens. Readings there were taken by hand, three times a day, and written into logbooks, and those logbooks have survived. Today they are kept in the Carleton College Archives. In 2023, the archivists chose one particular page to put on public display, and it is the page for the twelfth of January, 1888, the day of the Children's Blizzard. It is not a beautiful thing, they say. It's a page of columns and numbers, written in pen. But they chose it, as they put it, because it shows how even a seemingly routine item can give a unique look into a time and a historic event. I like that very much. On the day of one of the great storms of American history, somebody at a little college observatory in Minnesota walked out three times to look at the thermometer, and the barometer, and the sky, and came back in, and wrote down what they saw.

And here is our point of disagreement for this hour. It's about the stove, and about the women who worked at it.

For a long time, the story of household technology, the stove, the lamp, and later the washing machine and the electric iron, was told as a simple story of liberation. Each new invention, the story went, saved women hours of drudgery, and gave them time back. But in 1983, the historian Ruth Schwartz Cowan published a book called More Work for Mother, and she argued something quite surprising. When the iron cookstove arrived in American homes, she pointed out, it did make cooking easier in some ways. But it also made it possible to cook far more complicated meals, with many dishes at once, baking and boiling and frying side by side, and expectations rose to match. Men and boys, who in the days of the open hearth had spent hours chopping and hauling the wood for the fire, now had less of that work to do, while women's work at the stove grew. So the new machines, Cowan argued, often shifted work, and multiplied it, rather than reducing it. Other historians have pushed back on parts of her case, arguing that the gains in comfort and health were real and large, whatever the hours. The argument has gone on for forty years. If you asked Ingeborg, tonight, I think she would say that both sides have a point. And then she would ask you to fetch more wood.

Let's think about our own homes once more. If you live in a town or city, your light, and probably your heat, come in through wires and pipes from somewhere far away, and you almost never see the fuel itself. Out here, every bit of warmth and light in the Moens' house has a source you can touch. The oak logs, from the bluff. The coal oil, in its can by the door, bought in town for cash. The matches, in their tin box on the shelf. Electricity would not reach most farms in Minnesota for another fifty years, until the rural electrification programmes of the 1930s.

It's a quarter past five now. Ingeborg sets the second, smaller lamp in the window, the north window, facing the barn. Halvor asks her why, since nobody could see it through that storm. She says that she knows. She sets it there anyway. Out on these plains, in storms like this, families did that, set a lamp in a window towards the barn or the road, in the hope that someone who was lost might see it. Tonight, as far as Ingeborg knows, nobody is out there. But the lamp burns in the window all the same.

Gunhild is knitting. She has been knitting all afternoon, and she will go on knitting until bedtime, a pair of thick grey woollen mittens for Knut, who has lost his last pair somewhere between the house and the schoolhouse. Her needles click steadily, a small, bright, regular sound under the roar of the wind. Sigrid has woken, and is sitting in her mother's lap, staring at the lamp flame with enormous eyes. Knut is lying on his stomach on the rag rug by the stove, as close as he's allowed to get, with Bamse stretched out beside him. The dog was allowed in for good when the storm came, and nobody, tonight, is going to send him out again.

And Halvor is pulling his boots back on, and his heavy coat, and his cap with the flaps over his ears, and his thick mittens. Ingeborg watches him. Because the cow still needs milking, and the animals still need seeing to, storm or no storm. And the barn is forty paces away, across the white.

Halvor takes a second, shorter length of rope from the peg by the door, and ties one end firmly around his own waist.

## Part Six — Around Half Past Five: Milk, Hay and a Book Not Yet Written

Here is the small question for this part of the evening. If a family out here ran out of firewood in the middle of a long, hard winter, and there were no trees for miles, what on earth would they burn?

We'll come to that. But first, Halvor is going out to the barn.

He has tied the short rope around his waist, and now, at the door, he ties its other end, with a loose loop, around the long rope that runs from the iron ring to the barn. That way, the loop will slide along the guide line as he walks, and even if his hands are knocked off the line by the wind, or if he trips and falls in a drift, he will still be fastened to it. He'll be like a bead on a string. He takes the milk pail in one hand and a tin barn lantern in the other. The lantern won't help him see a thing on the way across. But inside the barn, it will be the only light.

Ingeborg lifts the bar, and Halvor goes out into the roar, and the door slams shut behind him, and the bar drops again. And for a little while, nobody in the house says anything.

Imagine you are Ingeborg now, standing with your back against the door, listening. You can hear nothing but the wind. It booms against the north wall and shrieks around the corner of the house and moans in the stovepipe, and under all of it there's a long, steady hiss, the sound of countless tiny grains of ice racing past the logs. You know that it is forty paces to the barn. You know the rope is good, and the knots are good, and your husband is careful. You know he'll be in there now, in the warm, dark, hay-smelling barn, hanging up the lantern, talking to Dagros, sitting down on the little three-legged stool, the milk ringing into the pail. You know all of this. And still you stand by the door with your hand on the bar, and you do not sit down.

Gunhild, by the stove, does not look up from her knitting. But she has stopped knitting.

It is about twenty minutes before the knock comes. Three hard blows on the door, the signal they had agreed. Ingeborg lifts the bar, and Halvor comes in out of the white, like a man made of snow. His beard is solid ice. His eyebrows are white. The pail in his hand has a lid of frozen milk on top, a skin of ice already, in the time it took to walk forty paces. He sets it down on the stove, and stands there, steaming, and grins at his wife, and says that Dagros sends her regards, and that the oxen are lying down in their straw as calm as two bishops, and that the barn is as snug as he has ever known it.

And then, at last, Ingeborg sits down.

Halvor tells them about the barn while he warms his hands. It is built partly into the slope of the hill, with log walls in front and the earth of the bluff itself at its back, and its roof is thatched thick with slough hay and weighted down with poles. It has no stove, and it needs none. When Halvor stepped in out of the storm and closed the door behind him, he said, it was like stepping into a different country. Dark, and quiet, and still, and warm with the breath of the animals. A big animal like an ox or a cow gives off a remarkable amount of heat, simply by being alive, by modern farm estimates something close to the warmth of a small electric heater, every hour of the day and night. Two oxen, a cow and her calf, a pig, and a dozen hens, shut up together in a small, low, earth-backed barn, are a furnace of their own. The frost was growing on the inside of the barn door, Halvor said, but the water in the trough was not even skinned with ice. Knut asks whether the hens are frightened. Halvor says the hens are asleep, all in a row along the roost pole, with their heads under their wings, and have not given the storm a single thought.

They eat supper not long afterwards. It's a simple, warm, comforting meal, the kind you eat on a night like this one. There is milk porridge for the children, made with the fresh milk, thick and sweet, with a lump of yellow butter melting in the middle of each bowl. There's a potato and onion soup, left simmering on the back of the stove since dinner. There's more flatbrød, and rye bread, and a soft, pale cheese that Ingeborg made in the autumn. And there's coffee, of course, again.

Now, our small question. What would you burn, out here, if the wood ran out?

The Moens are lucky. They have the woods of the river valley on their doorstep, and a long woodpile against the barn wall, and a sledge in the yard with half a load of oak on it, now buried under the snow. But a great many families on the open prairie had no woods at all. And when the wood or the coal ran out, they burned what they had. They burned dried corn cobs, which burn hot and fast. They burned dried dung from their cattle, which they called cow chips, and before that, out on the plains, the old buffalo chips that the great herds had left behind. And in the hardest winters of all, they burned hay. Not loose hay, which flares up and is gone in a moment. They twisted it. They took handfuls of long prairie hay and twisted them round and round, tight and hard, into sticks as solid as wood, and tucked the ends in, and fed those into the stove, one after another, all day and all night.

And that is the answer to our small question. Out here, if you have to, you burn the grass.

That last answer is one that millions of people around the world know, because of a book. And I want to tell you about it now, because it was written by a woman who knew this part of the country, and these kinds of winters, as well as anyone ever has.

Her name was Laura Ingalls Wilder. She was born in 1867, in Wisconsin, and as a girl she lived with her family in a whole series of places across the frontier. For several years in the 1870s, the Ingalls family lived on Plum Creek, near Walnut Grove, in southwestern Minnesota, only a few dozen miles south of the Moens' valley, first in a dugout house cut into the creek bank, and later in a little frame house. Then, in 1879, they moved west, into Dakota Territory, to the new railroad town of De Smet. And there they lived through the winter of 1880 and 1881, which people in the region called the Hard Winter.

It began, according to the weather historians, on the fifteenth of October, 1880, with a blizzard in southeastern Dakota, and it did not truly let go until April. Storm followed storm all winter long. The railroad, which was the town's only lifeline, was blocked by snow so deep in the cuts that the trains stopped running, and the Chicago and North Western Railway suspended its service, so that the town was cut off for months from coal, from kerosene, from flour and from food. The families of De Smet twisted hay to keep warm. They ground wheat, a few handfuls at a time, in their little coffee mills, to make enough flour for the day's bread. And two young men of the town, Almanzo Wilder, who would one day be Laura's husband, and his friend Cap Garland, really did drive out across the snow in the dead of winter to find and bring back a load of seed wheat from a settler living miles away, to keep the town from going hungry.

Laura was thirteen and fourteen that winter. Some sixty years later, she wrote about it, in a book called The Long Winter, published in 1940. In the novel, Pa and Laura twist hay into sticks in the cold lean-to, to burn in the stove, and the family grinds wheat in the coffee mill for their daily bread. When the kerosene runs out in the novel, Ma makes what she calls a button lamp. She takes a saucer of axle grease, and a button, and a small square of calico cloth. She wraps the cloth around the button, and twists the corners together into a little wick standing up from the middle, and sets the button down in the grease, and lights the tip. It gives a tiny, steady flame, about as bright as a candle, and it burns for hours. It is one of those details that stays with readers all their lives, a small, brave light made out of almost nothing. It is a wonderful book. If you've never read it, I hope that one day you will.

But I need to be careful here, because this channel is about what's real. The Long Winter is a novel. It is not a diary, and it is not a history. Laura wrote it when she was in her seventies, from her own memories, which were vivid but sometimes imperfect, and she shaped it for young readers, changing some things, leaving out others, and smoothing the story. So it's a beautiful window onto that time. But it is a window with some colours in the glass.

And there is something else. Before she wrote any of the novels, around 1930, Laura wrote out the story of her own life, by hand, in pencil, on cheap school tablets, as a plain memoir for adults. She called it Pioneer Girl. It was never published in her lifetime. It was published at last in 2014, by the South Dakota State Historical Society Press, edited by the historian Pamela Smith Hill, with careful notes that compare what Laura wrote with letters, photographs, newspapers and land records. And the memoir is often blunter and darker than the novels. Things that were softened, or left out, when the books were written for children, are there in the pencilled pages.

And that brings us to our point of disagreement. Who really wrote the Little House books?

Laura's only daughter, Rose Wilder Lane, was a successful professional writer and journalist in her own right, and she worked closely with her mother on the manuscripts, typing them, revising them, and sending them to publishers. Just how closely is the question. In 1993, the historian William Holtz published a biography of Rose called The Ghost in the Little House, and he argued that Rose's contribution was so great that she was, in effect, the books' hidden author, or at the very least a ghostwriter who turned her mother's rough drafts into polished prose. Other scholars, Pamela Smith Hill among them, have read the same manuscripts and letters and come to a different conclusion: that Rose was a strong and skilled editor, yes, but that the voice, the memories and the shape of the books were Laura's own. The argument has been going on for more than thirty years, and it is not over. What nobody disputes is that the winter itself was real, and the hay was real, and the hunger was real.

Let's think about our own world for a moment. If the trains stopped running into your town for a week, never mind a whole winter, most of the shelves in your local supermarket would probably be empty within days. Modern cities hold only a few days' worth of fresh food at any one time, and depend on an endless stream of trucks and trains. In some ways, a farm family like the Moens, with their own cow, their own pig, their own potatoes in the cellar and their own flour in the bin, was far better prepared for a long storm than most of us are today.

The supper is over now, and the dishes are washed, in hot water from the stove's reservoir, in the big tin dishpan. The clock on the shelf says six. Outside, it is black night, and the storm is, if anything, louder than before.

Gunhild has gone back to her knitting. Knut and Bamse are back on the rag rug. Marit is drying the last of the plates. Ole, warm through at last, and fed, is beginning to look very sleepy, though he would rather die than admit it at six in the evening.

And Halvor goes to the stove. He opens the firebox door and looks in at the fire, a deep, roaring bed of red and gold, and he adds two more oak logs, and closes the door. Then he reaches up to the stovepipe, and turns the damper handle a little, just a quarter turn. The roar of the fire drops, slightly, to a deep, steady, contented purr. He is not putting the fire to bed. Not yet. He is settling it in for a long night's work.

## Part Seven — Around Half Past Six: The Snow Against the Walls

Here is the small question for this part of the night. Why is the door of the Moens' house on the south side, facing down the slope towards the river, and not on the side facing the barn, which would be so much more convenient?

The answer is out there, in the dark, building up against the walls.

Because the snow is drifting. All the snow that the wind is carrying, the snow that is falling and the snow that it has torn up off the prairie for miles around, is racing across the open land at the top of the bluff, and pouring over the edge of it, and when it meets anything at all that slows the wind down, a fence post, a woodpile, a sledge, a barn, a house, it drops. It piles up. It builds, behind every obstacle, a long, smooth, curving drift, shaped like a wave about to break, and the drift grows, and grows, all night.

On the north side of the Moens' house, the side that faces the storm, the drift is already higher than the window sill.

Imagine you are going up into the loft now, with Marit, to fetch down the extra quilts. You climb the wooden ladder that stands against the wall beside the stove, rung by rung, with Marit going first holding a candle in a tin holder, very carefully, well away from anything that might catch. The loft is low, and you have to stoop under the rafters. The air up here is noticeably colder than downstairs, but it is not freezing, because the warm stovepipe runs right up through the middle of it, on its way to the roof, and the heat rising from the room below has gathered under the boards. There are two straw-filled mattresses up here, on the floor, one for the boys and one for Marit, piled with quilts. There are the trunks. There are bunches of dried herbs hanging from a beam, and a sack of seed potatoes, and a box of Christmas things, put away just a few days ago.

And at the north end of the loft, under the peak of the roof, there is a tiny square window, no bigger than a book. Marit holds up the candle to it. And where, on any other night, you would see blackness, or stars, you see, pressed flat against the glass, a smooth, pale, grey-white wall. The snow. It has risen right up the north wall, and it is already halfway up the gable end of the house, and still rising.

Marit is not frightened. She grew up here. She simply says, the way you might remark on the rain, that the house will be warm tonight. And she gathers up two quilts in her arms, and you follow the candle back down the ladder.

So, our small question. Why is the door on the south side?

Because out here, the worst winter storms almost always come from the northwest, and the north. Every settler on the northern plains learned this, often the hard way. A door on the north side of a house would be buried by morning, under a drift you might not be able to dig through from the inside. A door on the south side, sheltered by the house itself, stays clear. Halvor built his house with its back to the northwest, against the bluff, and its door and most of its windows looking south over the valley, towards the winter sun, and towards the river. It is less convenient, every day of the year, for the walk to the barn. But on a night like this one, it means that in the morning, the family will still be able to open their own front door. That's the answer.

Now, while Marit spreads the quilts over the bench by the stove to warm, let me tell you about one of the most famous documents in American history. Because it was written, in part, about places like this one, and at almost exactly this time.

In 1890, two years after our storm, the United States took its eleventh census. And when the results were analysed, the Superintendent of the Census reported something striking. Up to and including 1880, he wrote, in words I'm paraphrasing closely, the country had had a frontier of settlement, a line beyond which the land was empty of farms and towns. But by 1890, the unsettled areas had been so broken up by isolated pockets of settlement that there could hardly be said to be a frontier line any more. The frontier, in the census's sense, was gone.

Three years later, on the twelfth of July, 1893, a young historian from Wisconsin named Frederick Jackson Turner stood up in Chicago, at a meeting of the American Historical Association held during the great World's Columbian Exposition, and read a paper called The Significance of the Frontier in American History. He began with that very line from the census report. And then he argued that the frontier, the endless meeting point between settled land and open land, had been the single most important force in shaping the American character. It had made Americans, he said, independent, inventive, democratic, restless and practical. And now it was closing.

It became one of the most influential essays ever written by an American historian. And it is also our point of disagreement for this hour.

For half a century, Turner's frontier thesis dominated the way Americans thought about the West. But from the 1980s onward, a group of historians who came to be called the New Western Historians challenged it at almost every point. The best known of them, Patricia Nelson Limerick, in her 1987 book The Legacy of Conquest, argued that Turner's picture left out far too much. It left out the Native peoples, for whom the frontier was not an empty line but an invasion of their homelands. It left out the women, and the immigrants who spoke no English, and the Mexican and Chinese and Black Americans of the West. It left out the government money, the railroads and the big companies that made settlement possible. And she argued that the West was not a process that ended in 1890 at all, but a real place, with a continuous history, whose struggles over land and water and belonging went right on into the present. Turner's defenders reply that he saw something true about the way new land shaped those who settled it, even if he saw only part of it. The argument is still very much alive.

The land that the Moens farm, it's worth saying, was Dakota homeland, long before it was a homestead, and only a generation before this night, this very valley had been at the heart of the war of 1862 between the Dakota people and the United States. That is a long and serious story, and it deserves a night of its own. But it would not be honest to sit in this house and pretend that the land was empty when the first log was laid.

Gunhild, by the stove, has begun to talk. She often does, in the evenings, when her knitting has reached a plain stretch where she doesn't need to count. And tonight she talks about Norway.

She talks about her valley, where the mountains stood so close on either side that the sun disappeared behind them in November and didn't come back over the ridge until February, and how on the day it first returned, the whole family would go out into the yard to stand in it. She talks about the farm's summer pasture high in the mountains, and the long, light, endless nights of midsummer. And she talks about the snow, which in her valley came down soft and heavy and windless, and lay deep and blue for months, and was nothing at all like this screaming white wind. Snow in Norway, she says, was a quilt. Snow here is a wolf.

Knut, from the rug, asks her whether the nisse in the barn will be cold tonight. The nisse, in the old farm stories of Norway, is a little grey-bearded man, no taller than a child, in a red cap, who lives in the barn and watches over the animals, and who must be given a bowl of porridge with a lump of butter in it on Christmas Eve, or he will play tricks on the household for the whole year after. Gunhild left the bowl out in the barn this Christmas, as she always does, and in the morning it was empty, which Knut considers proof, and Ole considers Bamse. Gunhild tells Knut that the nisse is snug in the hay with the oxen, and that he has seen far worse storms than this one in the mountains, and Knut is satisfied.

There's a famous novel about exactly that feeling. It's called Giants in the Earth, by Ole Edvart Rølvaag, a Norwegian immigrant who became a professor in Minnesota, at St. Olaf College, in Northfield. It was first published in Norwegian in the mid-1920s, and in English in 1927. It tells the story of a family of Norwegian settlers in Dakota Territory in the 1870s, and of a mother named Beret, who is filled with dread by the sheer emptiness of the prairie, the endless flat land with nothing to hide behind. It's a novel, of course, and not a record. But many readers whose families came from Norway have said that Beret's fear was their grandmothers' fear, exactly.

And now, at last, we come to the second of our three mysteries. Why might a family be glad, truly glad, to watch the snow pile up against their walls, until it buries their windows?

Because snow is warm.

That sounds like a riddle, but it's simply true. Fresh, fallen snow is mostly air. Depending on how it falls and how hard the wind packs it, newly fallen snow can be something like ninety percent air, or more, trapped in countless tiny pockets between the crystals of ice. And trapped, still air is one of the best insulators there is. It's the same reason that a down quilt keeps you warm, or a woollen sweater, or the double glazing on a modern window. The air can't move, so it can't carry the heat away. A thick drift of snow, piled against a wall, works like an enormous, free, white blanket, wrapped around the house.

And more than that, a snowdrift stops the wind. That is the most important thing of all. Most of the heat that a house like this one loses on a stormy night is not lost slowly through the logs. It's stolen, by the wind, rushing through every crack in the chinking, and around the window frames, and under the door, and carrying the warm air away with it. Once the drift is up against the north wall, the wind can no longer reach those cracks. The house goes quiet on that side. The draughts stop. And the stove's heat stays in.

Settlers out here knew this very well. In the autumn, before the first snow, many families banked their houses, piling earth, or straw, or manure, against the bottom of the walls, all the way round, to keep the wind from blowing in under the floor. Halvor did it in October, with straw and earth from the garden, along the north and west walls. People who live in snow country have always understood what the snow can do for them. The snow houses of the Arctic are built on exactly the same principle. And to this day, people who are caught out in the mountains in winter are taught that, if they cannot get to shelter, they can dig into a snowbank, because the inside of a snow cave stays far warmer than the open air, and out of the wind entirely. Even small animals know it. Grouse and other birds of the northern woods will dive straight into soft snow on the coldest nights, and sleep there, tucked under the surface, safe in their own little snow rooms until morning.

So that's the second mystery answered. The family is glad of the snow against the walls, because tonight, the snow is on their side.

Let's think about your own home one last time in this part. Modern builders measure how well a material holds in heat with a number called its R-value. And some builders in snowy countries say, as a rough rule of thumb, that every inch of fresh snow on a roof adds a little insulation, a little like an extra layer in the attic. Many people living in the north today are quietly glad, on the coldest nights, to see a thick, smooth blanket of snow on the roof, for exactly the same reason the Moens were. Without knowing it, they are thinking like homesteaders.

On the north wall of the house, behind the drift, the logs have stopped creaking. Ole notices it first. He stands by that wall and puts his hand flat against the chinking, where an hour ago he could feel the cold coming through like water. Now it is still. Cool, but still. He takes his hand away, and looks at it, and grins.

It's seven o'clock. Outside, the storm is still roaring over the top of the bluff, and around the south side of the house, and against the roof. But here, in the lamplight, with the stove purring and the snow wrapped against the north wall like a quilt, it is beginning to feel almost peaceful.

## Part Eight — Around Half Past Seven: Boots, Hymns and the Blizzard Club

Here is the small question for this part of the night. With no radio, no television, no telephone and no screen of any kind, what did a family like this do with the long hours of a stormy winter evening, between supper and bed?

Let's watch them, and find out.

Halvor has brought Ole's boots over to the bench by the stove, and he has one of them upside down across his knees. The seam where the upper meets the sole has split along one side, from a winter of tramping through snow and slush, and he is mending it, with a long needle and a waxed linen thread, punching holes through the stiff leather with an awl, drawing the thread through, pulling it tight. It is slow, steady, careful work, the kind that men in farmhouses all over the northern world did on winter nights, when the fields were frozen and there was nothing to be done outdoors.

Ole is sitting on the floor beside him, with his back against the warm end of the woodbox, carving. He has a block of basswood, soft and pale and easy to cut, and a small folding knife, and he is making a wooden spoon for his grandmother, whose old one cracked at Christmas. He has been making it for three weeks. It is very nearly a spoon.

Ingeborg is mending. There is always mending. Tonight it's a pair of Halvor's woollen trousers, worn through at the knees from kneeling in the snow, and she is darning the holes, weaving new wool across them in a tiny, tight grid, back and forth, until the patch is almost as strong as the cloth around it.

Gunhild is knitting, of course. Knut's new mittens are nearly done. They're grey, with a pattern worked into the backs in white wool, an eight-pointed star, the kind of star that knitters in Norway have worked into mittens and sweaters for generations. One of the best-loved traditions of patterned mittens in Norway comes from the district of Selbu, near Trondheim, where, the story goes, a young woman named Marit Emstad knitted a pair of patterned mittens for a church service in the 1850s, and set off a fashion that spread across the whole country. Knut's mittens are knitted double, with two strands of wool carried behind every stitch, so that they are twice as thick, and twice as warm, as a plain pair. Gunhild says they would keep out the wind on the top of a mountain. Tonight, she adds, they may have to.

And Marit is reading aloud. She has the newspaper on the table in front of her, under the lamp. It's a Norwegian-language weekly, printed in the United States, one of the many papers that served the hundreds of thousands of Norwegian immigrants in the Midwest. Papers like Skandinaven, published in Chicago since 1866, and Decorah-Posten, published in Decorah, Iowa, since 1874, travelled by mail to farmhouses all across the northern prairies, carrying news from the old country, and from the new, and serial stories, and letters from readers, and advertisements for everything from patent medicine to steamship tickets home. It's no surprise that there were so many such papers. In the nineteenth century, Norway sent a larger share of its own people to America than almost any other country in Europe, with only Ireland, by most reckonings, sending more in proportion to its size. Whole valleys in Norway lost a third of their young people to the emigrant ships, and whole townships in Minnesota, Wisconsin, Iowa and Dakota filled up with Norwegian voices, Norwegian churches and Norwegian names. A farmer here could go for weeks without needing a word of English. This copy is three weeks old, and has been read already by everyone in the house, but Marit reads it aloud again anyway, slowly, a letter from a reader in Wisconsin about a church quarrel, while her grandmother corrects her pronunciation.

Imagine you are sitting there with them now, on the end of the bench, with a warm quilt over your knees, which Marit insisted you take. The lamp is lit on the table. The stove is purring. The wind is still booming outside, but the sound of it has changed since the drift built up on the north wall. It's further away now, and muffled, like the sea heard from inside a house by the shore. Halvor's needle squeaks through the leather. Gunhild's needles click. Ole's knife makes little whispering cuts in the basswood, and the pale curls of wood drop onto the floor around him. Marit's voice goes on, quiet and careful, in a language you don't understand, and you find that it doesn't matter, because the sound of it is so peaceful. Sigrid is asleep on her mother's lap. Bamse is asleep on Knut. And Knut is very nearly asleep on Bamse.

So, our small question. What did they do with a long winter evening?

They worked. Quietly, and together, with their hands. They mended and darned and knitted and carved and patched, the hundred small jobs of a farm household that piled up all year and waited for winter evenings. They read aloud, from the newspaper, the almanac, or the Bible. They told stories. They sang. And they talked, or didn't talk, as they pleased, in the comfortable silence of people who have spent all their lives in the same few rooms. That's the answer.

Let's think about how different that is from our own evenings. Many of us spend our evenings each in our own small pool of light, looking at our own screen, in separate rooms, or even side by side on the same sofa, each of us in a different world. In the Moens' house, there is only one pool of light, around one lamp, on one table, and everyone sits inside it. If you want to see, you have to be together.

Now, after a while, Ingeborg says that it's time for a hymn, and the family sings. They sing in Norwegian, a slow Lutheran evening hymn that Gunhild learned in her parish church more than half a century ago, in the valley under the mountains. Halvor has a deep, rough, unexpectedly gentle voice. Ingeborg sings the melody. Marit and Ole sing along, a little self-consciously. Gunhild sings in a thin, wavering, perfectly true old voice, with her eyes closed. And Knut, who doesn't know all the words, hums. Their voices go up into the dark rafters, and mingle there with the moan of the wind in the stovepipe, and it is one of those moments that, if you were a child in this room, you would remember for the rest of your life.

When the hymn is over, Gunhild tells them, as she does every year at about this time, that tomorrow is the thirteenth of January, and that in Norway the thirteenth was the twentieth day of Christmas, the day when Christmas was finally over, and the last of the Christmas food was eaten, and the decorations were put away, and the old people would say that Christmas was being danced out of the house. In Sweden, she says, they call it Knut's Day, after a king called Knut who lived long ago. Knut, half asleep on the rug, opens one eye at the sound of his own name, and asks whether that means it is his day. Gunhild says that, in a manner of speaking, it does. Knut says that in that case he would like the last of the Christmas cookies, the thin, crisp, rolled ones in the tin on the top shelf. Ingeborg says tomorrow. Knut says that tomorrow is very nearly here. Everyone laughs. And Knut, satisfied with his evening's work, closes his eye again.

Then Gunhild, still in the mood for remembering, tells the story she tells least often, of the crossing. She came over in 1877, on a steamship from Bergen, by way of England, eleven days on the grey Atlantic, sharing a narrow bunk in steerage with a woman from her parish who was sick from the first day to the last. She came ashore at Castle Garden, the old round stone fort at the tip of Manhattan that served as New York's immigrant station for most of the century, before Ellis Island was opened in 1892. And then she rode a train, for days, west and west and west, across a country that seemed to her to have no end at all, until Halvor met her on the platform with the oxen and the wagon, and she did not recognise him, because he had grown a beard.

And people did remember this night for the rest of their lives. That is our primary record for this hour.

Many years after the storm, a man in Nebraska named W. H. O'Gara began gathering together the memories of people who had lived through it. Survivors of the January twelfth blizzard had formed a kind of society, known as the Blizzard Club, to share their stories, and O'Gara collected their letters and accounts and eventually published them in a book called In All Its Fury: The Story of the January Twelfth, 1888, Blizzard. The book's publishing history is a little tangled. It's usually dated to the 1940s, and it was reprinted in later decades. But the letters themselves are still there. History Nebraska, the state historical society, holds a collection of the correspondence sent to O'Gara by the members of the Blizzard Club. In one letter, from 1942, O'Gara writes of his hope of publishing a book of the survivors' stories. In another, from 1944, a man named P. W. Holst sends him his own personal account of the day. And in a third, from 1949, a woman named Ora A. Bohrer compares the hardships of surviving the storm of 1888 with what people had just been through in the great blizzard of 1949, more than sixty years later. Think of that. People who had been children in 1888 were still writing to each other about it, as old men and women, in the age of radio and the motorcar, because it was the day they never forgot.

And one of the stories they told most often was about a teacher.

Her name was Minnie Freeman, and she was a young teacher at a country school near Ord, in Nebraska. When the storm struck that afternoon, the story goes, the wind tore at her little sod schoolhouse, and damaged it so badly that it could no longer give the children shelter. And so she led her pupils out into the storm, and across the open country, to a farmhouse, and every one of them came through alive. The newspapers seized on the story, and within weeks she was a national heroine. Letters and gifts came to her from all over the country. And a songwriter named William Vincent wrote a song in her honour, called Song of the Great Blizzard: Thirteen Were Saved.

And here is our point of disagreement for this hour, though it's a gentle one. What exactly did Minnie Freeman do? The accounts don't all agree. Some say that the wind tore the door from its hinges. Some say that part of the roof was ripped away. Some versions of the story, especially the later ones, say that she tied the children together with twine, in a line, so that none of them could be lost in the whiteout, and led them by the hand to safety. Others don't mention the twine at all. Even the number of children differs a little between tellings. The newspapers of 1888 were not always careful with details, especially in a story that people so badly wanted to hear. So, as with so much from this day, the heart of it is surely true: a young teacher kept her head, and her pupils, and brought them all home. But the details have been polished by a great many hands over the years, the way a stone is polished by a river.

There were other teachers, too, all across the plains that afternoon, whose names are far less well known. Teachers who decided not to send anyone home, and kept their pupils inside the schoolhouse, around the stove, through the whole long night, until the storm blew itself out and the fathers came with sleighs in the morning. I haven't been able to read the Blizzard Club letters for myself, so I won't put words into the mouths of the people who wrote them. But it is worth remembering that for a great many of the children of that day, the story of the twelfth of January ended as the Moens' story is going to end. With a long, cold, strange night indoors, and a morning, and home.

Halvor finishes the boot. He holds it up to the lamp, and bends it, and tugs at the seam, and nods. Ole holds up his spoon. Gunhild takes it from him, and turns it over in her old hands, and runs her thumb along the curve of the bowl, and tells him gravely that it is the finest spoon she has ever owned, which is true, because it is the only spoon anyone has ever made for her.

The clock on the shelf says it is a quarter past eight. Knut is asleep on the rug, with his head on Bamse's side. Sigrid is asleep in her mother's arms. And it's time, Ingeborg says, for the children to go up.

## Part Nine — Around Half Past Eight: Hot Bricks and the Loft

Here is the small question for this part of the night. How do you warm a bed that, by morning, will be in a room very nearly as cold as the inside of a refrigerator?

The answer has been sitting on the back of the stove all evening, though you might not have noticed it.

Three bricks. Ordinary red clay bricks, the kind used for building chimneys, left over from when the stove was first set up. And beside them, two heavy flatirons, the solid iron irons, shaped like the bottom of a boat, that Ingeborg uses on Tuesdays to press the washing. All of them have been warming slowly on the iron, at the cool end of the stovetop, since supper. Now Ingeborg lifts them off, one at a time, with a thick folded cloth, and wraps each one in an old piece of flannel, and then another, until they are warm bundles that you can hold against your chest without being burned.

That's the answer to our small question. You heat the bed with the stove. You carry a little of its warmth up the ladder, wrapped in flannel, and you push it down between the sheets to the place where your feet will go. People had been doing something like this for centuries, in cold houses all over the northern world. The wealthy used special brass warming pans with long handles, filled with hot coals and passed between the sheets. The rest made do with bricks, and stones, and irons, and anything else that would hold heat. And it works beautifully. A hot brick, well wrapped, will keep a bed warm for hours.

Marit goes up the ladder first, with her own brick under one arm. Ole follows, with two more, one for himself and one for Knut. And then Halvor carries Knut up himself, still fast asleep, limp and heavy and warm, over his shoulder like a sack of flour, with Bamse whining anxiously at the foot of the ladder, because Bamse is not allowed in the loft and has never understood why.

Imagine you are lying in the loft now, beside Knut, under the low slope of the roof. The mattress beneath you is a big cloth bag stuffed with straw, freshly changed in the autumn, and it crackles and rustles every time anyone moves. On top of it there's a feather tick, a thinner bag stuffed with goose and duck feathers, saved over years from the farm's own birds. And over you there are quilts. Four of them, heavy and soft, pieced together from worn-out shirts and dresses and the good parts of old blankets, each one with a story in every square. Knut is curled up against your side, warm as a loaf of bread. At your feet, the wrapped brick is giving off a deep, steady heat that spreads slowly up through the straw and the feathers. You can hear the wind on the roof, close above your face, roaring and hissing over the shakes. The roof is covered with them, long, thin, rough shingles of oak, split by hand from short lengths of log with a heavy blade called a froe, and laid in overlapping rows on boards nailed across the rafters, like the scales of a fish. Halvor and Ole split more than a thousand of them, the summer the house was built. Each one is nailed down with square-cut iron nails, and here and there, where a nail was a little too long for the board beneath it, its point pokes through into the loft, a small dark spike of iron in the underside of the roof, just above the beds. Knut likes to count them. There are nineteen above his pillow. Tonight, in the light of Marit's candle, before she blew it out, there was something odd about them. They looked, she thought, as if each one had been dipped in sugar. She meant to look more closely, and then she fell asleep. And you can hear the warm stovepipe, a few feet away from you in the dark, ticking softly as it heats and cools. You are warmer than you would ever have believed possible, in a loft, in a blizzard, in a house made of logs.

Marit is lying on her own mattress a little way off, with her quilts pulled up over her nose. She whispers to Ole, across the dark, asking if he was frightened on the rope this afternoon. Ole is quiet for a while. Then he says yes. Marit says that she was frightened too, watching from the window. And then neither of them says anything more, and after a while Ole hears his sister's breathing slow, and lengthen, and soften into sleep.

Sharing a bed like this, three children in a row, or a whole family under one great heap of quilts, was perfectly ordinary in farmhouses of this time, and in most of the houses that have ever existed. A bed to yourself was a luxury, and on a cold night it was not even a luxury anybody wanted. Each sleeping body gives off its own steady warmth, all night long, and two or three together under one set of covers make a small, private, glowing world, which is much harder for the cold to reach.

Before she goes down again, Ingeborg climbs halfway up the ladder with the small lamp held carefully out to one side, and looks into the loft. She counts the heads, the way mothers have always counted heads at night. Three. She tucks the top quilt more firmly around Knut's shoulders, where it has slipped. She touches Marit's hair. She tells Ole, in a whisper, that she is proud of him, and Ole, who is pretending to be asleep, says nothing at all, and goes red in the dark. Then she climbs down, and the light goes with her.

Downstairs, Ingeborg lays Sigrid in her cradle, close to the warm side of the stove, but not too close, and tucks the wool blankets around her, and rocks the cradle with her foot for a little while, until the baby's fists unclench.

Let's think, for a moment, about how you probably go to bed. You walk into a bedroom that's at more or less the same temperature as the rest of the house, sixty-something degrees, all night long. If you feel cold, you might switch on an electric blanket, or turn up the thermostat, or pull on a pair of socks. You do not usually need to plan your sleep around the fire. And here's a curious thing. Sleep researchers today often recommend a bedroom that is quite cool, somewhere in the sixties Fahrenheit, roughly sixteen to nineteen degrees Celsius, because the body sleeps best when its temperature can drop a little during the night. The Moens' loft will be colder than that by morning, much colder. But under those quilts, with the bricks and the feathers and each other, the children are lying in exactly the kind of cool air and warm bed that a modern sleep scientist might approve of. In the Moens' house, and in every house like it, bedtime is a kind of careful operation, worked out over generations. Hot bricks. Shared beds. Layers of quilts. Nightcaps and woollen stockings. And above all, the stove, which must be fed and banked and set so that it will burn all night without going out, and without overheating, and without setting the house on fire.

Now I want to tell you what happened, after this storm, to the people whose job had been to warn of it.

As we heard earlier, in the 1880s the national weather service belonged to the Army, as a part of the Signal Corps. And it had been under growing criticism for years, for its cost, for its military style of management, and for forecasts that did not always reach the people who needed them. The storm of the twelfth of January in the west, and then another great storm that same winter, the great blizzard of March 1888 in the eastern states, which buried New York and much of New England under deep snow and killed hundreds of people, added to the pressure. Congress acted. On the first of October, 1890, President Benjamin Harrison signed a law that took the weather service away from the Army altogether, and created a new civilian Weather Bureau, under the Department of Agriculture, where it would serve farmers above all. That law is our primary record for this hour. And the Weather Bureau it created is the direct ancestor of today's National Weather Service.

As for Lieutenant Thomas Woodruff, the young officer in St. Paul, he did not disappear from history. His 1885 study, Cold Waves and Their Progress, prepared under the Chief Signal Officer, survives in libraries and in the hands of collectors to this day, a careful, early attempt to understand exactly the kind of sudden, deadly cold that swept over the plains on this January afternoon. He lived until 1899.

And now, our point of disagreement for this hour. It's about a number you've probably heard on a winter weather forecast: the wind chill.

We all know, from experience, that a cold day feels far colder when the wind is blowing. The air takes heat away from your skin faster when it is moving, the way blowing on hot soup cools it down. But how much colder? In the early 1940s, in Antarctica, two American explorers, Paul Siple and Charles Passel, set out to measure it. They hung a small plastic cylinder full of water outside their hut, and timed how long it took to freeze, again and again, at different temperatures and different wind speeds. From those measurements, published in 1945, came the first wind chill index, which weather forecasters used for more than half a century. But other scientists criticised it for years. A plastic bottle of water, they pointed out, is not a human face. It has no blood flowing through it, and no skin, and it doesn't walk into the wind. Critics argued that the old index made the cold sound worse than it really was. And so, in 2001, the weather services of the United States and Canada replaced it with a new formula, based partly on the work of the scientists Randall Osczevski and Maurice Bluestein, which used measurements of real human faces in a chilled wind tunnel, and produced numbers that were generally less extreme than the old ones. Some scientists still argue that no single number can capture how cold a person really feels, because it depends on what you are wearing, and how you move, and whether the sun is out, and who you are. So the wind chill is a real effect. But the number on the weather forecast is, and always has been, a kind of informed estimate.

Out on the prairie tonight, nobody needs a number. Anyone caught out there would feel it at once.

But nobody in this house is out there. And that is the thing to hold on to now. The doors are barred. The animals are bedded down. The children are asleep in the loft, in a nest of straw and feathers and quilts, with warm bricks at their feet. The baby is asleep by the stove. Bamse has given up whining at the foot of the ladder, and has lain down across the bottom rung instead, with his nose on his paws, so that nobody can go up or come down without stepping over him, which, as far as Bamse is concerned, is the next best thing to being up there himself.

Halvor goes to the north window and wipes a hole in the frost, and looks out. There's nothing to see, of course. Only the pale glow of the lamp in the window, reflected back from the snow pressed against the glass. He listens. The wind is as loud as ever. But to Halvor's ear, it is not quite as fierce. Not as sharp. As if the storm, too, were beginning to tire.

Ingeborg comes to stand beside him. Neither of them says anything. After a while, she takes his hand.

Gunhild, by the stove, finishes the last stitch of Knut's second mitten, and bites off the wool, and lays the pair side by side on her knee, and smooths them flat. Then she sets them on the warming shelf above the stove, where they will be warm and dry for Knut in the morning.

Then Ingeborg does something she does every evening, though tonight it takes her a little longer than usual. She takes down the Norwegian almanac from the shelf, a thin paper-covered book with a page for every week of the year, and a little blank space beside every day. She dips her pen in the ink bottle, and she writes. Most days, the entries are only a few words long. The cow calved. Snow. Halvor to town. Butchered the pig. Tonight she writes the date, the twelfth of January, and under it, in Norwegian, a single line. Warm in the morning, washing out. Terrible storm in the afternoon from the northwest. All safe. Then she looks at it for a long moment, and underlines the last two words. Families all across the plains kept little records like this one, in almanacs and account books and the back pages of Bibles, and some of them survive, in attics and historical societies, still. Ingeborg's is invented. But hundreds like it are not.

Halvor, meanwhile, takes the key from behind the clock and winds it, as he does every night at this hour, twelve slow turns, counting under his breath. It's an eight-day clock, which means it will run for more than a week on a single winding. But Halvor's father wound his clock every night of his life, back in Norway, and so Halvor does too. The clock gives a small, satisfied whirr, and settles back into its ticking.

The clock on the shelf says nine.

## Part Ten — Around Ten O'Clock: Putting the Stove to Bed

Here is the small question for this part of the night. When everyone in the house is asleep, and nobody is awake to feed it, how do you keep a fire alive until morning?

Because that is what Halvor must do now. And it is, in its way, the most important job of the whole night.

The house is very quiet. The children are asleep in the loft. Sigrid is asleep in her cradle. Gunhild has gone to her own small bedroom, a narrow room partitioned off at the back of the house, behind the warm wall of the stove, with a bed, a chair, a chest, and a little shelf for her Bible and her spectacles. Before she went, she kissed her son on the forehead, as if he were still a boy, and told him not to let the fire go out, as if he might. And she took one of the hot bricks with her.

Now there are only Halvor and Ingeborg, and the stove, and the lamp, and the wind.

Imagine you are sitting with Halvor now, on a low stool in front of the stove, close enough to feel the heat of it on your knees and your face. He has opened the firebox door. Inside, the evening's fire has burned down to a deep, glowing bed of embers, orange and red, shimmering, with little blue flames running over them here and there like water. He takes the iron poker, and he begins, very gently, to rake the embers together. Not to stir them up, but to gather them. He pushes them into a thick, compact heap at the back of the firebox, where they will hold their heat the longest. Then he opens the ash pan underneath, just a crack, and shakes the grate, so that the loose ash falls through and the air can reach the coals from below.

Then he chooses the wood. This is not the same as feeding the fire in the daytime. In the daytime, you want dry, split wood that burns fast and hot. Now, for the night, he wants the opposite. He wants big, heavy, solid pieces of oak, the densest wood he has, unsplit, as large as the firebox will take. He lays two of them on the bed of coals, side by side, and a third across the top. Oak is the best wood for this that the valley can give him. It's one of the heaviest, densest woods of the northern forests, far heavier than the light cottonwood that grows along the prairie creeks, or the soft basswood that Ole carves his spoons from. A stick of cottonwood will blaze up brightly and be gone in an hour. A log of oak the same size holds much more fuel packed into it, and burns long, and slow, and leaves behind a deep bed of hot coals. Settlers out here learned to know their woods by weight, and smell, and the sound they made when they were split. They don't catch at once. They sit there, dark and heavy, and the coals glow against them, and slowly, slowly, a thin line of flame begins to lick along their undersides.

Then he closes the firebox door. He reaches to the front of the stove and slides shut the little vent in the door that lets air in, until it's open no wider than the thickness of a knife blade. And finally, he reaches up to the stovepipe, and turns the damper handle, three quarters of the way closed.

And the roar of the fire drops away almost to nothing. A soft, deep murmur. Like someone breathing in their sleep.

That's the answer to our small question. You bank the fire. You give it a deep bed of hot coals, and big, slow-burning logs, and you starve it of air, just enough so that it smoulders, rather than blazes. A fire with too much air burns up its fuel in an hour and goes out. A fire with too little air chokes, and smokes, and dies. But a fire with just enough will burn slowly and steadily for six hours, or eight, or even more, and in the morning there will still be red coals at the heart of it, waiting to be woken. Getting it right is a skill that took years to learn, and it was a skill that every farm family in the north depended on, every single winter night.

There's one more thing that Halvor checks, before he leaves the stove. He looks carefully at the stovepipe, all the way up, where it passes through the ceiling into the loft. Where it goes through the boards, there's a wide iron collar, and a gap of clean air around the hot pipe, so that it never touches the wood. He touches the boards beside the collar with the back of his hand. Warm, but not hot. Good. Then he looks at the floor in front of the firebox, where a square of sheet iron lies under the stove, so that a falling ember can't reach the planks. And he moves the woodbox back, a little further from the stove than it was. Fires in farmhouses were one of the great fears of the frontier, especially in winter, when stoves burned hard day and night. And especially on a night like this one, when nobody could ever have come to help.

Let's think about how you might keep warm tonight, in your own home. Perhaps you have a furnace or a heat pump, controlled by a thermostat, which may even be programmed to turn the heat down a little while you sleep, and up again before you wake. It does in a moment, with a click, what Halvor has just done with a poker, a log, a damper and twenty years of experience. And if your heating stops in the night, an alarm may wake you, or you may simply wake up cold and call someone in the morning. If Halvor's stove goes out tonight, there is nobody to call. There is only Halvor.

Now, let me tell you about the cold that is coming down over the plains tonight, behind the storm, because the records of it survive.

The storm was not a single day's event. Behind it came a deep, long cold that lasted for weeks. The Signal Corps office in St. Paul recorded an average temperature for the whole of January 1888 of zero degrees Fahrenheit, according to Minnesota's state climatologists, who have studied that whole bitter winter. That's not the coldest night. That's the average, of every day and every night, for the entire month. And on the twenty-first of January, nine days after our storm, the Signal Corps thermometer in St. Paul fell to forty-one degrees below zero. All of these readings, from St. Paul and from stations all across the country, were gathered up and printed in Washington, in the Signal Service's Monthly Weather Review, in its issue for January 1888. That volume still exists, and it is our primary record for this hour, the official account, in tables and columns and careful prose, of the most famous month in the history of American winter weather.

And now, our point of disagreement for this hour. It's about another winter, and another book. How true is The Long Winter?

For many decades, people read Laura Ingalls Wilder's novel about the winter of 1880 and 1881 and wondered whether it could possibly be accurate. Blizzards one after another from October to April? A town cut off for months, living on twisted hay and coffee-mill flour? Surely, some readers thought, she must have exaggerated it, the way people do with the hardships of their childhoods.

Then, in 2020, three climate scientists, Barbara Mayes Boustead, Martha Shulski and Steven Hilberg, published a study in the Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society, examining that winter with the tools of modern climatology. They went back to the old weather records and the old newspapers, and they set out to sort which parts of Laura's story were grounded in fact and which might have been fiction or exaggeration. And their conclusion, broadly, was that she had not exaggerated much at all. The winter of 1880 and 1881 had been, across a wide swath of the region, one of the most severe ever recorded, ranking among the five worst in the historical record, and in some places the worst of all. Some of the novel's details were shaped, and some of the timings compressed or moved. But the winter itself was every bit as hard as she remembered. Other researchers, working with the regional newspapers of the time, have compared the novel with thousands of local reports, and found much the same thing: a story that was simplified, and shaped for children, but rooted in a winter that was truly terrible. So the disagreement, here, has largely been settled. And it was settled in Laura's favour.

Halvor, still sitting by the stove, has been thinking about something similar. Not about books, but about winters. This is his tenth winter on this hillside, and he has known bad ones. The winter of 1880 and 1881, the Hard Winter, was bad here too, though the Moens had wood, and were never as desperate as the people in the treeless railroad towns further west. But he has never in his life seen anything like this afternoon. The speed of it. The sudden, savage change, from April to the Arctic, in the space of a few minutes. He thinks about the families out on the open prairie, in their sod houses, with no wood but what they hauled from the river. He thinks about the schoolhouse, a mile and a half away across the fields, and the teacher, a young woman from Wisconsin, and the dozen children who would have been sitting in it this afternoon, if their parents had not been tempted to keep them home on washday. He hopes, very much, that the teacher kept them in. He will find out, in time. Tonight, there is nothing he can do but hope.

Ingeborg comes and sits beside him, on the bench, with her shawl around her shoulders. She has been listening to the storm too. She says that she thinks the wind is dropping. Halvor listens. Yes, he says. Perhaps it is. Not much. But a little.

And it's true. The roar has changed again. It's still loud, still steady, still hissing over the roof. But the sudden hard gusts, the ones that made the logs creak and shudder, are coming further apart now. The storm is beginning, very slowly, to blow itself out.

They sit there together for a while, in the warm, dark kitchen, with the lamp turned down low, talking quietly, about small things. About the cow, and whether she'll calve in March. About Ole, and how tall he's getting, and how he'll need new boots by the autumn, mended or not. About Sigrid, and her new tooth. About whether there'll be a good wheat price this year. And about Norway, a little, the way people do when they are tired and safe and far from home.

And they talk about tomorrow. In the morning, if the storm has passed, Halvor will dig out the path to the barn, and see to the animals, and then he and Ole will strap on the long wooden skis that hang in the barn, the kind of skis that Norwegians brought with them to the prairie, and go across the fields to the nearest neighbours, the Tollefsruds, and then on to the schoolhouse, to see how everyone has fared. Out here, after a storm, that is simply what people did. They went to see. They went to help. Ingeborg says that she will send bread with them, and the rest of the potato soup, in the big covered pail, in case anyone has run short. Halvor says that's a good idea. He's already half asleep, sitting up.

Then Ingeborg stands up, and turns the lamp down further, until the flame is only a small blue bead on the wick, and then she cups her hand behind the chimney and blows it out. And the room goes dark, except for the thin, deep red line of light that glows around the edge of the stove's firebox door, and the faint red gleam of the coals through the little vent in the front.

Ingeborg and Halvor's bed is in the corner of the main room, against the warm inner wall, behind a curtain of heavy cloth that Ingeborg hung on a wire. They get into it in the dark, in their woollen nightclothes, under the heavy quilts, and the last warm brick at their feet. Ingeborg can hear Sigrid breathing, softly and evenly, in the cradle beside her. She can hear the stove murmuring. She can hear Bamse, at the foot of the ladder, giving a long, contented sigh, and settling his chin on his paws.

And Halvor, who has done more today than in any week of his life, and walked twice through a blizzard on the end of a rope, closes his eyes. And within a minute, he is asleep.

Ingeborg lies awake a little longer. She listens to the storm, which is quieter now. She listens to her family, sleeping all around her, in the dark, under one roof. She counts them, the way she always does. Halvor. Sigrid. Gunhild, behind the wall. Ole, and Marit, and Knut, above her in the loft. Six. And herself. Seven.

Then she turns over onto her side, and pulls the quilt up to her chin, and closes her eyes.

Above her, in the loft, in the dark, under the iron points of the roof nails, something very small and very quiet is happening, as it has been all evening long.

## Part Eleven — Around One in the Morning: Frost on the Nails

Here is a small, gentle question for this hour of the night. In the middle of a blizzard, what is it that wakes a sleeping mother?

It isn't a sound. It's the lack of one.

Ingeborg opens her eyes in the dark, and listens. The wind has stopped. Not entirely. There's still a soft, steady sighing over the roof. But the long, booming roar that has filled the house since a quarter to four in the afternoon has faded away while she slept, and the silence it has left behind woke her, the way a clock that stops ticking can wake you.

That's the answer to our small question. After nine hours of storm, it's the quiet that wakes her.

She gets up, and puts her shawl around her shoulders, and pads across the cold floor in the dark, past the red glow of the stove, to the ladder.

Imagine you are climbing the ladder behind her now, rung by rung, in the dark. Bamse lifts his head as you step over him, and thumps his tail twice. The air grows colder as you rise into the loft. Ingeborg has brought the little lamp, unlit, and a match. She strikes the match against the ladder, and lights the wick, and turns it down very low, and holds it up, carefully, away from the quilts.

And you see the third of our mysteries, shining.

Above the children's beds, under the slope of the roof, every one of those iron nail points that pokes through the boards is wearing a little white beard of frost. Some of them have tufts as long as your little finger. They hang there in the lamplight, in rows, glittering, feathery, delicate, like tiny white tassels, or like the frost on a winter hedge, each one growing out and down from the dark point of iron at its heart. Knut's nineteen nails, above his pillow, are nineteen small white stars.

So here is the last of our three mysteries, at last. In a warm house, with the stove burning all night, why would the nails in the roof above the children's beds grow little white beards of frost?

The answer is in the children themselves. And in the nails.

All evening, and all night, seven people have been breathing in this house. And every breath they breathe out carries a little water with it, invisible, as vapour. So does the steam from the washing, and the coffee pot, and the soup on the stove. All that moisture drifts in the warm air, and warm air rises. It rises up the ladder and through the gaps in the loft boards, and gathers under the roof, above the sleeping children.

And there, waiting for it, are the nails.

Each nail is a short spike of iron, and iron carries heat, or cold, very easily, far more easily than wood. Its head is hammered into the oak shakes on the outside of the roof, where tonight the air is far below zero. And its point sticks through into the loft. So the cold of the night outside flows straight down the iron, like water down a pipe, and the point of each nail, hanging there in the loft, becomes a tiny, bitterly cold spot, much colder than the boards and the air around it. When the warm, damp air of the house touches that cold iron, the water in it freezes straight onto the nail, not as a drop of water, but as ice, crystal by crystal, building out into a feathery tuft. In fact, on a night as cold as this one, the water in the air doesn't even stop to become a drop of liquid first. It goes straight from invisible vapour to solid ice, in a single step, the way frost always forms on a cold, still night, building its delicate branches one molecule at a time. Scientists call that deposition. Gunhild calls it the frost growing. They are both right. That's frost. It's the same thing that grows on the windowpanes downstairs, and on the inside of a barn door, and on the grass on a clear autumn morning. And it grows on the nails because the nails are the coldest things in the warm room.

That's the answer. The nails grow beards of frost because the children are alive and warm, and breathing, all night long, right underneath them.

People in houses like this one saw it all the time. Teachers' guides to Laura Ingalls Wilder's The Long Winter point out frost on the nails there too, in a little house in the grip of the cold. And you can still see it today. Home inspectors in the northern states and Canada are often asked by puzzled homeowners about frost on the nails in their attics on very cold nights, and the explanation they give is exactly the same one: warm, moist air from the house below, meeting nails as cold as the outdoors. Indoor air, and outdoor cold, meeting on the point of a nail. Nothing has changed but the house.

Ingeborg holds the lamp up for a moment longer, and looks at the frost, and at her children, sleeping under it. Then a small voice beside her says, very sleepily, what is it, Mamma? It's Knut, half awake, peering up at the light. She shows him. She holds the lamp a little closer, and he looks up at his nineteen white nails, and his eyes go very wide. He says they look like the nisse's beard. She says that perhaps they do. Then she tells him to go back to sleep, and that in the morning, when the stove is going again, they will melt, and drip, and in the evening they'll grow back. Knut says he'll count them. He's asleep again before he gets to three.

So Knut is the first to fall asleep again tonight. Seven years old, curled on his side under the quilts, with one hand still reaching up towards the nails. When you're seven, sleep comes back easily.

Ingeborg tucks the quilt around him. And then, as she turns to go, there's a stir from the other mattress. Marit, eleven, light sleeper, her mother's daughter, has woken too. Is the storm over, Mamma? she whispers. Almost, Ingeborg says. Go to sleep. And Marit sighs, and turns over, and pulls the quilts up over her ear. And because she knows now that it's nearly over, she's asleep again within a minute. Marit is the second.

Ole doesn't wake at all. He's fourteen, and he walked through a blizzard today, holding onto a rope, and he has not moved since he lay down. He sleeps on his back, with one arm flung out over the side of the mattress, his mouth a little open. Ingeborg looks at him for a long moment, at the boy who isn't quite a boy any more. Then she blows out the lamp, and goes carefully down the ladder in the dark.

Now, while the house settles again, let me tell you something gentle and curious, about the word we've been using all night long. Blizzard.

It's a surprisingly young word, at least in its present meaning. Before the 1870s, in American English, the word blizzard seems to have meant something quite different: a sudden blow, or a volley of shots, a burst of gunfire, as in giving someone a blizzard. And then, in 1870, a small newspaper in the town of Estherville, in northwestern Iowa, the Northern Vindicator, used the word to describe a fierce snowstorm, and it seems to have caught on, first across the northern prairies, and then everywhere. That newspaper is usually credited with the first use of blizzard in its modern sense, and it's our primary record for this hour, a small-town weekly, in a prairie town not so very far to the south of the Moens.

And here is our point of disagreement. Is that really where the word came from? Word historians are not entirely sure. Some have pointed out that the word seems to have been used for storms in spoken English on the frontier before anyone printed it, and the Estherville paper may simply have been the first to write down a word that people were already saying. Others have suggested that it might have come from a dialect word in English or German, carried west by settlers. And the old meaning, a blow or a burst of shots, may have been part of the story too, since a blizzard really does feel like being struck. No one has proved any of these for certain. What's clear is that by the winter of 1888, every newspaper in America knew the word, and after the twelfth of January, nobody on the northern plains would ever need it explained.

Downstairs, by the stove, Ingeborg finds that Gunhild is awake too. The old woman is standing in the doorway of her little room, in her long nightgown and shawl, with her white hair in a plait over her shoulder. The quiet woke her as well. Is it over? she asks. Nearly, Ingeborg says. Gunhild nods, as if she had expected nothing else, and says that she told them so, though she didn't. And she goes back to her bed, and lies down, and pulls up her quilts, and folds her hands on top of them, the way she has slept every night of her life, as straight as a soldier. In a few minutes, she's breathing slowly and evenly. Gunhild is the fourth.

Sigrid stirs in the cradle, and makes a small, questioning sound. Ingeborg lifts her out, and sits down on the edge of the bed in the dark, and feeds her, quietly, with the shawl around them both. Sigrid feeds sleepily for a little while, and then her mouth goes slack, and her little hand, which was holding a fold of her mother's nightgown, opens. Ingeborg lays her back in the cradle, and tucks the wool around her, and rocks it twice with her hand. Sigrid is the fifth.

Halvor half wakes when Ingeborg comes back to bed. He mutters something about the stove. Then he gets up, in the dark, in his long underwear and woollen socks, and opens the firebox door. The oak logs have burned down into a long, glowing, red-gold heap. He lays one more big log on top of it, and closes the door, and leaves the damper as it is. Then he comes back to bed, and lies down, and is asleep again almost before his head is on the pillow. Halvor is the sixth.

And Ingeborg lies awake, last of all, in the warm dark, listening to the silence where the storm used to be. A faint sighing of wind over the roof. The soft tick of the clock on the shelf. The murmur of the fire in the stove. Seven people breathing. And far above her, in the cold of the loft, the frost growing quietly on the nails, crystal by crystal, over the heads of her children.

Outside, beyond the logs and the drifts, the last of the wind is dying. The clouds are thinning, and breaking, and drawing away to the southeast. And through the gaps, for the first time since the middle of the afternoon, the stars are coming out. There's no moon tonight. It's new, as we said, and it lies somewhere below the eastern horizon, dark and invisible. So the stars have the sky to themselves, more of them than you have probably ever seen, thick and sharp and glittering in the dry, still, freezing air, all the way down to the edge of the white prairie, from one side of the world to the other. Nobody in the Moens' house is awake to see them. But they are there, over the bluff, over the barn and the buried sledge and the long rope across the yard, already rimed with frost, over the snow piled smooth against the north wall, and over the thin grey thread of smoke that still rises, straight up now, into the windless air, from the stovepipe on the roof.

Inside, the stove murmurs on in the dark. It is the only thing in the house that is awake. It has been working since dawn yesterday, and it will work until dawn today, without a moment's rest, its iron ticking softly as it gives up its heat, a little at a time, to the room, and the walls, and the sleeping people. In the cradle beside it, Sigrid sighs, and settles. On the rug at the foot of the ladder, Bamse's paws twitch, chasing something through a summer field in his sleep.

Ingeborg closes her eyes. And she is the seventh.

## Part Twelve — Around Eight in the Morning: The Stove Wakes

Here is the last small question of the night. When a fire has been banked and starved and left to sleep for hours, how do you wake it up again?

Let's watch Halvor do it, because he is the first one up.

It's still dark when he wakes, a little after seven by the clock. The house is cold, colder than it has been at any time since the storm began. Not freezing, down here, by the stove. But cold enough that he can see his breath, faintly, in the grey half-light that is just beginning to show through the frosted windows. He pulls on his trousers and his thick woollen shirt, and his socks, and he goes straight to the stove, the way he does every winter morning of his life.

He opens the firebox door. And there, at the back of the firebox, under a soft grey blanket of ash, the coals are still glowing. Not many. A dull, deep red heap, about the size of two fists, pulsing very faintly, like something breathing in its sleep. The big oak logs have burned away to almost nothing in the night. But the heart of the fire is still alive.

First, he opens the damper in the stovepipe, all the way. Then he slides open the vent in the firebox door, wide. And at once, you can almost hear the stove take a breath. Air rushes in under the coals, and up the pipe, and the red heap brightens, from dull red to cherry, to orange. Then Halvor takes a handful of kindling from the woodbox, thin, dry sticks of split ash and a twist of birch bark that Ole gathered in the autumn, and he lays them, gently, crossways, right on top of the coals. He blows on them, once, softly, the way you might blow on a cup of hot coffee. And the birch bark curls, and blackens, and catches, with a sudden little crackle and a bright yellow flame. The kindling catches from the bark. And within a minute, there is a fire in the firebox again, small and quick and dancing, and Halvor feeds it, stick by stick, then split pieces, then a log, until the stove begins to roar, and to tick, and to warm.

That's the answer to our small question. You give it air, and something small and dry to eat, and a little patience. A fire that's been well banked doesn't need to be lit again in the morning. It only needs to be woken.

Then he fills the coffee pot from the water pail, cracking a thin skin of ice on top of the water with his knuckle, and sets the pot on the front of the stove, over the firebox, where it's hottest.

Imagine you are coming down the ladder from the loft now, behind Knut, who has woken at the very first crackle of the kindling, the way children always seem to do on special mornings. Your feet are cold on the rungs. Your nose is cold. You have your quilt still wrapped around your shoulders like a cloak. And as you come down into the room, the smell of the fire meets you, woodsmoke and hot iron, and then, a few minutes later, the smell of coffee. The windows are white, every pane thick with frost ferns, so thick now that they glow in the dawn light like panes of milky glass. And everywhere you look, someone is waking. Gunhild, in her doorway, with her shawl. Marit, coming down the ladder after you. Sigrid, in the cradle, pulling herself up by the rail and announcing that she's awake, in the loud, wordless way of babies. Ingeborg, behind the curtain, putting up her hair.

Knut goes straight to the warming shelf above the stove, and finds his new mittens there, where his grandmother put them last night, warm and dry and grey and white, with the eight-pointed stars on the backs. He puts them on, there and then, before he has his boots on, or his shirt. And he holds up both hands to show everyone, and turns them this way and that in the firelight. Gunhild nods, satisfied. Then he asks whether it is Knut's Day. It is, Gunhild says. Then he would like, he says, the Christmas cookies. And this time, Ingeborg laughs, and fetches the tin down from the top shelf, and gives him one, and one each to the others too, for breakfast, before the porridge, which nobody can remember ever happening before.

Above the beds in the loft, as the stove warms the house, the frost on the nails is beginning to melt. It goes slowly at first, the little white tassels shrinking and turning clear, and then, one by one, they drip, small cold drops falling onto the quilts, so that Marit, who was the last one up, comes down the ladder complaining that it's raining in the loft. By the middle of the morning, the nails will be bare and dark again. And by tonight, the frost will be growing on them once more.

Now let me tell you about one more record, a gentle one, to finish with, because the Moens are about to open their door onto a world of snow.

Three winters before our storm, on the fifteenth of January, 1885, on a farm in the little town of Jericho, in Vermont, a young farmer named Wilson Bentley did something that no one had ever done before. He fitted a microscope to a bellows camera, and he caught a single snowflake on a piece of black velvet, and carried it, very quickly, onto a cold glass slide, and photographed it. It was the first photograph ever taken of a single snow crystal. Bentley was nineteen years old. And he went on photographing snowflakes, winter after winter, for the rest of his life, until he had made more than five thousand pictures of them, each one a perfect, tiny, six-sided star or plate or column of ice. People called him Snowflake Bentley. His photograph of the fifteenth of January, 1885, is our primary record for this last part of the night, and I think it's a lovely one.

Bentley became convinced, from all his years of looking, that no two snowflakes were ever exactly alike. And that idea, which almost all of us learned as children, is our last point of disagreement.

Because scientists who study snow are not quite so sure. In 1988, an atmospheric scientist named Nancy Knight, working at the National Center for Atmospheric Research in Colorado, was studying snow crystals collected from a cloud over Wisconsin, and she found two crystals that, under the microscope, looked identical. They were simple ones, small hollow columns, not the elaborate branching stars of Bentley's photographs. And the physicist Kenneth Libbrecht, at the California Institute of Technology, who has spent much of his own career studying and growing snow crystals, has said that while very simple crystals can look alike, the large, complicated, feathery ones, with all their branches, are so intricate that the chance of two being exactly alike is, for all practical purposes, nothing at all. So the answer to the old question, are no two snowflakes alike, seems to be: almost certainly true, for the beautiful ones. And perhaps not quite true, for the plainest ones of all.

Let's think about our own mornings, just once more. You might wake to an alarm on your phone, and press a button on a coffee machine, and walk into a kitchen that has been kept warm all night by a thermostat, and look out of a double-glazed window at the weather forecast for the day, already on your screen. The Moens wake to the crackle of kindling, and coffee boiled on iron, and a window too thick with frost to see through. They have no forecast. But this morning, they don't need one.

Because Halvor has his coat on, and his cap, and his mittens, and he has gone to the door. The south door. The one that faces the valley, and the river, and the morning. He lifts the wooden bar. He takes hold of the latch. And he pulls the door open.

The cold comes in first. A bitter, dry, perfectly still cold, so sharp that it makes your eyes water and catches in your throat. And then, the light.

The sun is rising. By the reckoning of a modern astronomy program, it's coming up at about four minutes to eight this morning, over the bluffs to the southeast, into a sky so clear and so pale and so blue that it seems to go on forever. And everything, everything, as far as anyone can see, is white. The whole valley is white. The river is gone under the snow, a long smooth curve of white between the white bluffs. The oaks on the slope are black against it, every branch and twig traced in a line of frost. And the drifts. The storm has carved the snow into shapes like nothing you have ever seen, long smooth waves and curling crests and hollows, sweeping and swirling around the woodpile and the sledge and the corner of the house, sculpted by the wind as if by a great careful hand, and every one of them glittering in the low gold light of the sun.

The yard between the house and the barn is a field of drifts, some of them as high as Halvor's waist. And across it, from the iron ring on the corner of the house to the post by the barn door, runs the rope. It's half-buried in places now, and furred all along its length with white frost, glittering like a string of tiny diamonds in the sunrise. Halvor looks at it for a long moment. Then he picks up the snow shovel from beside the door, and he begins to dig, following the line of the rope, towards the barn, where already, faintly, he can hear Dagros lowing for her breakfast.

Ole comes out to help him. Then Marit. Knut stands in the doorway in his new mittens, and is told, firmly, to stay where he is until there's a path. Bamse does not stay where he is. Bamse bounds out into the deepest drift in the yard, and disappears completely, and comes up again, white from nose to tail, and barks at the sun.

And far across the valley, on the far side of the river, and out along the edge of the prairie to the east, one by one, thin grey threads of smoke are rising, straight up, into the still, clear, freezing air. From the Tollefsruds' house. From the farm beyond theirs. And from the little chimney of the schoolhouse, a mile and a half away across the fields, where somebody, this morning, has lit the stove.

Ingeborg sees it from the doorway, and puts her hand over her mouth. Gunhild, beside her, says nothing at all. She just takes her daughter-in-law's other hand, and holds it.

And inside, behind them, the stove burns on. In the middle of the room, on its square of sheet iron, black and solid and ticking softly to itself, just as it did at noon yesterday, when the icicles were dripping, and just as it will tonight, and tomorrow night, and through all the long nights of this long winter. The stove that cooked the dinner, and warmed the bricks, and dried the mittens, and kept the frost on the outside of the walls where it belonged. The stove that was banked and starved and left to sleep, and that waited, all through the storm, glowing in the dark, for someone to wake it. The coffee pot is beginning to rattle on its lid. Sigrid is reaching for the warm iron from her mother's hip, and being gently turned away. And the flames move behind the little vent in the firebox door, gold and orange and steady, and the warmth spreads out from the black iron, slowly, into every corner of the house.

They have survived the night. They knew how. And now, so do you.

Thank you for spending this long winter night with me, and with Halvor and Ingeborg, and Gunhild, and Ole and Marit and Knut and little Sigrid, and Bamse, too, in their warm log house on the bluff above the Minnesota River. If this story helped you rest, or helped you drift off somewhere along the way, I'm truly glad. And if you'd like to come back for more nights like this one, you might consider liking this video, and subscribing to the channel, gently, whenever you're ready. There's no hurry at all.

Next time, we'll go back a little in Gunhild's story, to the long journey that brought so many families like hers to this side of the world. We'll spend a night below decks on an emigrant steamship crossing the North Atlantic in the 1880s, in the crowded, rocking, lamplit world of steerage, and we'll find out how ordinary people, with all they owned in a single painted trunk, slept, ate, kept warm and kept hope alive on the way to a new country. I hope you'll join me there.

Until then, sleep well. Keep warm. And may your own fire, whatever it is, still be glowing in the morning.

## How the sources were checked

- **The family is fictional.** Halvor, Ingeborg, Gunhild, Ole, Marit, Knut and Sigrid Moen, the dog Bamse, the oxen Store and Lille, the cow Dagros, the neighbours (the Tollefsruds), the cousin's letter and Ingeborg's almanac entry are all invented. The script says so in Part One. No invented person is presented as appearing in any record.
- **The setting is real.** A log house on the wooded north bluffs of the Minnesota River valley, Sacred Heart Township, Renville County, Minnesota, on Thursday 12 January 1888 (the "Children's" or "Schoolhouse" Blizzard). The house is modelled on a real log cabin in the same township described in a National Park Service National Register nomination (round oak logs, dovetailed corners, about 15 × 18 ft, in a hilly wooded area near the river). The Moens' house itself is invented and is said to be larger.
- **Why logs and not sod.** Search results confirmed that on the open prairie of southwestern Minnesota settlers often built of sod (e.g. a reconstructed sod house near Sanborn, where the builder counted 13 remembered sod houses in one neighbourhood), while timber grew in river valleys. Lake Shetek (Murray County) was considered and rejected: the state park history notes that its only native trees were on the islands and were soon cut.
- **How quotes were checked.** Direct page fetches are blocked in this environment, so each item was checked through web-search result summaries of the pages listed below. **The session's shared web-search budget ran out partway through research** (after about 30 searches by this agent). Items that could not be searched are marked "standard reference, not re-checked this session" in the tables below. They are widely documented facts that I am confident of, but they should be checked against a print or library source before publishing.
- **Verbatim quotations kept in the script** (all short): the Homestead Act's "actual settlement and cultivation"; Charles Dana Wilber's phrase "rain follows the plow"; Birdie McDonald's "one terrible blizzard" and "tieing the clothes line to one corner of the sod house"; the newspaper headlines "130: That Is the Awful Total of Dead and Missing" and "Nearly 140 Dead". Everything else is paraphrased, and the narration says so where wording matters (Woodruff's forecast, Saltee's memory, the 1890 Census statement).
- **Things cut or softened for lack of verification:** a claim that teachers burned school desks for fuel (cut; replaced with "kept their pupils inside around the stove"); any description of what the Blizzard Club letters say beyond their catalogue summaries (the narration says the letters could not be read directly); exact Long Winter quotations such as "It can't beat us" (not used); a precise arrival time of the storm at Sacred Heart (none found; the script's quarter-to-four arrival is set from Carl Saltee's account of the wall approaching Fortier by about 3:30 pm and is part of the fictional story); 1880s stove catalogue prices and brand names (not used); the design of the Signal Service cold-wave flag (not used); the death or fate of named children other than the MNHS-reported Johnny Walsh (not used).
- **Computed, not quoted.** For Sacred Heart, Minnesota (44.78° N, 95.35° W), PyEphem gives sunset on 12 January 1888 at about 5:03 pm and sunrise on 13 January at about 7:56 am (Central Standard Time, UTC−6), and new moon at about 2:38 am CST on 13 January 1888 (moon about 4% lit or less). 12 January 1888 was a Thursday. Local solar noon falls about 12:30 pm CST. The narration says these were worked out with "a modern astronomy program".
