# How Neanderthals Survived the Ice Age | History for Sleep

## Part One — Around Half Past Four in the Afternoon: The Last Light

The light is going now, and it is going early.

Over a narrow river valley in the hills of what we now call Catalonia, the winter sky has turned the colour of pale flint, and a long band of rose and amber lies low along the western ridges, as if someone had drawn a warm finger across the edge of the world. The pines on the slopes are dark already, dark green going to black. Down below, a river runs quick and grey between stones, and the sound of it rises up the valley, steady and endless, like breathing.

On one side of the valley there is a cliff of pale, honey-coloured stone, full of tiny holes, like a sponge turned to stone, with water seeping down its face in glistening threads. And at its foot, where the rock leans out like the brim of a great hat, there is a long, shallow shelter, open to the valley, with a roof of rock overhead and a floor of dry, ashy earth beneath.

And in that shelter, there is a fire.

It is a small fire, low and orange, burning on the bare ground, with no stones around it at all. Around it, moving quietly in the last of the daylight, there are people. A woman with grey in her hair is kneeling beside the flames, feeding them with a piece of pine branch. A small child is sitting on a hide close by, watching her. And a little way off, a man is standing at the very edge of the shelter, looking out down the valley, the way people have always looked out at the end of the day, waiting for someone to come home.

This place is real. Today it is called the Abric Romaní, the Romaní rock shelter, on the edge of the town of Capellades, about forty miles west of Barcelona. But the town does not exist yet. Nor does Barcelona, nor Spain, nor any town or field or road anywhere in the world. Because the time we have come to is roughly fifty-five thousand years ago. It is the Ice Age. And the people around this fire are Neanderthals.

When most of us hear that word, a certain picture comes to mind. A hunched, hairy figure with a heavy brow and a club in his fist, shambling out of a cave, grunting. A brute. A dead end. Something less than us.

That is not who we are going to meet tonight. Not the brutish caveman of the museum dioramas of a century ago. The people by this fire walked as upright as you do. Their brains, on average, were at least as large as ours. They kept fire, and very probably made it. They made tools, and glue, and clothing. They cared for one another when they were hurt. And over hundreds of thousands of years, through some of the coldest and harshest stretches of time our planet has known, they survived, again and again, winter after winter, in places like this one.

I should tell you something honestly, before we sit down with them. If you were taken from your own warm bed tonight and set down here, at the mouth of this shelter, in your nightclothes, with the dark coming and the frost already beginning to form on the stones, you would not have survived until morning. Not because the people here were stronger than you, though in fact they very probably were. But because they knew a thousand things about fire and cold and food and the dark that you and I have never had to learn. Without those things, on a night like this one, you would simply not be alive to see the sunrise.

So tonight, we are going to learn those things. Slowly. One at a time.

And while we do, I'd like you to carry three small mysteries with you, the way you might carry three smooth river pebbles in a pocket.

The first is this. Why would a hunter sit by the fire, holding a lump of hard, golden tree-sap and a sharp flake of stone, and warm them both, patiently, at exactly the distance from the coals that he can bear on the back of his hand?

The second. Why would a man who cannot hunt, who can barely hear, and who has not been able to use one of his arms for years, be given the warmest place at the hearth tonight, and some of the best of the food?

And the third. When it is time to sleep, why will this whole group lie down in a single line along the back of the shelter, each with a tiny fire of their own, each fire about one long stride from the next?

I'll answer each of them in its own time. The first a little before the evening is out. The second in the deep of the night. And the third when the shelter is very nearly asleep. There's no hurry. We have the whole night.

Now, before we go any closer, I want to be clear with you about something, because it matters on this channel. The group you are about to meet is invented. We do not know what Neanderthals called themselves, or each other. We don't know their language, or even, for certain, whether they had a language anything like ours, and we'll come back to that question later in the night. So the names I'll use tonight are made up, short and plain, chosen only so that you and I can tell these people apart. Their ages, their habits, their small kindnesses, those are invented too. But almost everything around them is real. The shelter is real. The hearths are real; you can see their ashes in the excavated layers. The tools, the bones of the animals they ate, the marks of their fires, the impressions of their wooden objects, those were dug out of the ground and measured and published by scientists whose names I'll tell you as we go, so that you can look them up yourself, if you ever want to, on some wakeful afternoon.

So. Let's meet them.

The woman kneeling by the fire is Mora. She's about forty-five, which makes her the oldest woman in the group, and very possibly one of the oldest people anyone here has ever known. Her face is broad and strong, with the heavy curve of bone above the eyes that every Neanderthal had, and her hands are cracked from cold and fire. She is the keeper of this hearth. She lit it this morning from the embers of last night, and she will be the one who watches it longest tonight.

The small child on the hide beside her is Pim, her grandson. He's about five. He is watching the flames with the complete seriousness that small children bring to fire.

The man at the edge of the shelter, looking down the valley, is Orrin, Mora's brother. He's about forty. He stands a little crookedly, favouring one leg, and his right arm hangs thin and still at his side. He is waiting for the hunters to come back, because he was a hunter once himself, and he still can't help it.

Out there, somewhere down the valley in the dusk, are three more of the group: Ket, Mora's son, who is about twenty-seven; Dun, his cousin, who is about thirty; and Hesk, who is fifteen and on one of his first real hunts, the son of Mora's daughter, who died some winters ago. Back in the shelter, sitting on a log near the fire with a baby at her breast, is Sela, Ket's partner, who is about twenty-four. She was not born into this group. She came here from another band, over the hills to the north, when she was about Hesk's age. Her baby, a girl, is called Lu, and she's a little more than a year old. And beside Sela, sorting a pile of pine cones into two heaps for no reason that anyone else can see, is her elder daughter, Tavi, who is about ten.

Nine people. One shelter. One fire.

Here is a small question to begin with, one that we'll answer before this part is done. What, exactly, is this strange, spongy, honey-coloured cliff? And why does it matter so much, for us, that these people chose to make their fire beneath it?

Imagine, now, that you are standing beside Orrin at the edge of the shelter. You're cold already. The air smells of pine resin and woodsmoke and wet stone. Behind you, you can hear the fire ticking and the baby making small contented noises. In front of you, the valley is filling up with blue shadow. And if you reach out and lay your palm against the rock beside you, you'll find it rough and gritty and full of tiny tubes and pores, and, here and there, faintly damp.

This stone is called travertine. It forms where water rich in dissolved limestone bubbles up out of the ground in springs. As the water trickles out over moss and plants and twigs, it gives up some of that dissolved stone, a thin film of calcium carbonate, which hardens around whatever it touches. Year after year, film after film, the springs build up a rock out of moss and leaves and water. That's why it's full of holes. Many of those tiny tubes were once the stems of plants. And here, in this valley, the springs had been building for so long that geologists measure the travertine beneath these cliffs in tens of metres. Beneath the shelter alone, the travertine layer is about seventeen metres thick.

That's the answer to our first small question. The cliff is a cliff of stone made by water. And it matters because that same water, dripping and seeping, kept on laying down its thin films of stone right over the floor of the shelter, over the ashes of the fires and the bones and the tools that the people left behind, sealing each layer under a crust before the next people came. It's one of the reasons this place is so precious to archaeologists. The travertine preserved things that, almost anywhere else, would simply have rotted away.

Now, a few words about how we know any of this.

On the ninth of August, 1909, a businessman from Capellades named Amador Romaní, who made his living in the town's paper industry and spent his spare time hunting for antiquities, found stone tools and bones in the sediment beneath these cliffs. People in the town had known the place by an older name, which meant something like the shelter of the old burial ground. Romaní understood how important the find was, and within a few days, he'd told the authorities. The shelter has carried his name ever since. In the 1950s and 1960s, a second campaign of excavation was led by the archaeologist Eduard Ripoll. And in 1983, a team under the Catalan archaeologist Eudald Carbonell began a new project here, which has gone on, season after season, for more than forty years, carried on in recent years by a team including Palmira Saladié, Gema Chacón and Josep Vallverdú, of the Catalan Institute of Human Palaeoecology and Social Evolution. They have identified twenty-seven archaeological levels in the shelter, one on top of the other, almost all of them left by Neanderthals, and they've given the levels letters of the alphabet. Tonight, we're in the level they call N, which their team has dated to roughly fifty-five thousand years ago.

And here is our first place where scientists have disagreed, and it is about the very picture of the Neanderthal I asked you to set aside.

In 1908, near the village of La Chapelle-aux-Saints in south-western France, a nearly complete skeleton of a Neanderthal man was found in a small cave. At the time, it was the most complete Neanderthal skeleton anyone had ever seen. It was studied by the French palaeontologist Marcellin Boule, one of the most respected scientists of his day, who published a long monograph on it between 1911 and 1913. And Boule's reconstruction was the one that shaped the popular image for half a century. He gave the old man a stooped, slouching posture, with bent knees and a head thrust forward, and he read the low skull and heavy brow as signs of a primitive, unintelligent creature, closer to the apes than to us. An illustration that Boule commissioned showed a hairy, gorilla-like figure.

But the skeleton Boule studied belonged to an old man, for a Neanderthal, who had lost many of his teeth, and whose spine and joints were badly deformed by arthritis. In 1957, two anatomists, William Straus and A. J. E. Cave, re-examined the bones and argued that his disease might have bent him somewhat, but that a healthy Neanderthal would have stood and walked fully upright, much as we do. Later studies went further. In 2019, a team led by Martin Haeusler of the University of Zurich published a new reconstruction of the old man's spine and pelvis, and concluded that Neanderthals stood with a balanced, upright posture, very much like our own. The brute in the diorama was, in large part, an old man with a bad back, misread.

Let's set this shelter beside your own home for a moment. Your house was probably built to a plan, by people who had studied for years how to keep water out and heat in. Its walls are full of insulation, its windows sealed, its roof pitched to throw off the rain. A modern thermostat is often set to about twenty degrees Celsius, around sixty-eight Fahrenheit. This shelter has one wall and a roof, and the whole of its front is open to the night. It has no door. And yet it has one thing your house shares: it faces the right way. The open side looks out across the valley, the back is solid rock, and the overhang keeps off rain and snow. The rock itself, warmed a little by a day of winter sun, will give back that warmth slowly through the evening. People have been choosing places like this for as long as there have been people.

Down the valley, the rose light on the ridges has gone grey. The river sounds louder in the dusk, the way rivers always do. Mora lays another piece of pine on the fire, and a little burst of resin crackles and spits, and Pim jumps, and then laughs.

Orrin is still standing at the edge of the shelter, watching the path. No hunters yet. He turns and limps back towards the warmth, and lowers himself carefully onto a hide near the fire, and holds out his good hand to the flames.

The sun has set. The night has begun.

## Part Two — Around a Quarter Past Five: Feeding the Hearth

Here is the small question for this part of the night. If you had to keep a fire alive through nearly fifteen hours of winter darkness, with no matches, no lighter, no fireplace and no chimney, what would you burn, and how would you build it?

Because that is exactly the task in front of Mora.

At this time of year, in this valley, the sun sets at around half past four in the afternoon and doesn't rise again until well after seven the next morning. I worked that out, by the way, using a modern astronomical program, for the latitude of Capellades at midwinter. The sky fifty-five thousand years ago was not quite the same as ours, because the tilt of the Earth slowly wanders over tens of thousands of years, but the difference to the length of a winter night here is small, a matter of minutes. So we can say, with reasonable confidence, that the people in this shelter face something close to fifteen hours of darkness. In your own home, that would mean almost nothing. You'd turn on a light, and turn up the heating, and the night would pass without a thought. Here, every one of those fifteen hours has to be fed.

Imagine you are kneeling beside Mora now, at the edge of the hearth, close enough to feel the heat on your face and knees and the cold of the shelter on your back. The fire is built right on the floor. There's no ring of stones around it, no pit dug deep into the earth, no grate. Just a shallow, flat patch of ground, blackened and reddened by burning, with the fire sitting on top of it. If you look closely, you'll see that the earth around its edges has been baked to a reddish crust, and that underneath the glowing heart of the fire there's a layer of black, and under that, grey ash.

This is what the hearths of Abric Romaní look like when archaeologists find them. Flat. Simple. Mostly without any stones at all. And there are a great many of them. Across all the levels of this shelter, the excavators have uncovered something approaching two hundred hearths. In our level alone, level N, researchers have reported around nineteen. Some are larger, some smaller. Some overlap, one burned on top of the ashes of another, which tells us that people came back to the same spots and lit their fires again, perhaps after weeks, or seasons, or years.

Mora reaches into a pile beside her and takes out a length of dead pine branch, dry and grey and light, and lays it across the fire, not in the middle, but along one edge, so that it will catch slowly. Then another. She's not building a bonfire. She's building a fire to last.

And what she's burning is mostly pine. We know that because when archaeologists sift the ashes of these hearths under the microscope, they find tiny fragments of charcoal, and wood charcoal keeps the structure of the wood it came from, cell by cell, so that a specialist can tell one tree from another. In the hearths of Abric Romaní, the charcoal is overwhelmingly pine, of the kind botanists call the Scots pine type, a hardy tree of cold climates. In a study of the hearths of level J, a little younger than our night, the researchers described wood as the basic fuel of these Neanderthal fires, and pine as the wood that was most valued. They also found traces of grasses, which may have had particular uses, perhaps to get a fire going, or to make it flare.

That's the answer to the first half of our small question. You burn what grows around you, and here, what grows around you is pine. Dead branches, fallen limbs, perhaps a dry trunk dragged in from the slope. Pine is not the perfect fuel. It burns quickly, and it spits, and its resin makes a thick, black, sooty smoke if it's green. But it's everywhere, it lights easily, and dead pine, dried by wind and sun, burns hot and bright.

There's another small detail that the microscopes have shown us, and I love it. When wood begins to rot, fungi grow through it, and they leave tiny marks in the walls of its cells. Those marks survive, even after the wood has been burned to charcoal. So by looking for fungal damage, researchers who study Neanderthal hearths at other sites in eastern Spain, places like Abric del Pastor and El Salt, have tried to tell whether people were gathering fresh-cut wood, or dead and decaying wood lying on the ground. Their answer, in many cases, is dead wood. Wood that the forest had already let go of. Which makes perfect sense, if you think about it. Dead wood is drier. It's lighter to carry. And you don't need to chop it down. You only need to pick it up.

Now, the second half of the small question: how do you build it? Mora's way is simple. She keeps a small, hot core of embers in the middle, and she feeds it from the edges, one branch at a time, pushing the ends in as they burn down. Thick pieces for slow heat. Thin pieces when she wants light. A handful of dry needles and grass if the flame sinks too low. And all the time, she watches. That's the real secret. The fire is built not once, but again and again, every few minutes, for fifteen hours.

Let's think about that in modern terms for a moment. Somewhere in your home, there's probably a thermostat on the wall, and a boiler or a furnace, which turn themselves on and off all night without anyone watching them. If you have a gas stove, you can make a flame in a second with the turn of a knob. If you want to light a candle, there's a box of matches in a kitchen drawer, or a plastic lighter that cost you less than a cup of coffee. Every one of those things is a solution to a problem that Mora solves with her hands, her eyes, and a pile of dead branches. She is the thermostat. She is the boiler. And she does not get to sleep through the night until she's handed the fire on to someone else.

And that brings us to one of the great questions about the Neanderthals, a question that scientists are still arguing about today. If Mora's fire went out tonight, could she light it again?

You might think the answer is obvious. These people lived through ice ages. Surely they could make fire. But it turns out to be surprisingly hard to prove. A hearth tells you that someone used fire. It doesn't tell you where the fire came from. It could have been made, by striking stones together or by rubbing sticks. Or it could have been carried in from a natural fire, a tree struck by lightning, a smouldering patch of grass after a summer storm, and then kept alive, carefully, for as long as possible.

In 2011, a team including the archaeologists Dennis Sandgathe and Harold Dibble published a study of two Neanderthal sites in south-western France, Pech de l'Azé IV and Roc de Marsal. They looked for traces of fire, things like burned flint and burned bone, layer by layer, through tens of thousands of years. And they found something puzzling. In the layers from warmer periods, there was clear evidence of fire. But in the layers from the colder periods, when you'd expect people to need fire most of all, the evidence almost disappeared. Sandgathe and Dibble suggested that these Neanderthals may not have been able to make fire whenever they wanted, and may have depended, at least in part, on natural fires, which would have been more common in warmer, stormier times.

Then, in 2018, an archaeologist named Andrew Sorensen, with his colleagues Emilie Claud and Marie Soressi, published a paper in the journal Scientific Reports with the title "Neandertal fire-making technology inferred from microwear analysis." They had looked very closely at the surfaces of a number of stone hand-axes from Neanderthal sites in France, about fifty thousand years old. And on some of them, they found small, distinctive marks: little C-shaped scars, as if the stone had been struck again and again, and fine parallel scratches, and a kind of mineral polish. Sorensen then made replica hand-axes and tested them. The marks that matched best came from striking or forcefully rubbing a lump of the mineral pyrite against the stone. Strike pyrite against flint, and you get sparks. Catch those sparks in dry tinder, and you get fire. The authors argued that this was the first direct evidence from the tools themselves that Neanderthals made fire regularly. Sorensen was careful, though. He acknowledged that some other mineral, one they hadn't thought of, might leave similar traces.

And the story didn't stop there. In December 2025, a team led by Nick Ashton of the British Museum published a paper in the journal Nature about a site called Barnham, in Suffolk, in the east of England. There, in sediments around four hundred thousand years old, they found a patch of heated clay, fire-cracked flint hand-axes, and two small fragments of iron pyrite, a mineral that is rare in that landscape, and which seems to have been carried there on purpose. The team argued that this was the earliest evidence anywhere in the world of people deliberately making fire, pushing the record back by some three hundred and fifty thousand years. Who were the people at Barnham? Most likely early Neanderthals, or their close ancestors, though the team was cautious about that too.

So the debate is alive. Some scientists believe Neanderthals could make fire whenever they chose, and had been able to for hundreds of thousands of years. Others suspect that some Neanderthal groups, at some times, could only keep it. Tonight, in this shelter, I'll lean towards the newer evidence. I'll imagine that Mora knows how to strike a spark, if she must. But I'll also imagine that she would very much rather not have to. Because a fire kept is always easier than a fire made. And so, like every fire-keeper in every age, she keeps it.

Pim has crept closer to watch. He's not allowed to touch the fire, not yet, but he's allowed to hand his grandmother the small sticks, one at a time, when she holds out her hand. He takes this job very seriously. He chooses each stick with great care, and examines it, and offers it to her as if it were a gift. She takes each one without looking, and lays it exactly where it needs to go.

Above them, the smoke is rising against the underside of the overhang and spreading out, like water poured onto the ceiling, and then rolling slowly out over the lip of the rock and away into the valley. There's a reason the fire sits where it does, a few steps in from the edge of the shelter and not deep at the back. Archaeologists who study the hearths of caves and rock shelters, among them Yafit Kedar and Ran Barkai of Tel Aviv University, have used computer models of airflow to argue that Palaeolithic people placed their fires where the smoke would clear best, so that the shelter could be warm without filling up with smoke. Mora doesn't need a computer model. She learned it from her own mother, and from a lifetime of stinging eyes.

Tavi has come over now, too, and is sitting cross-legged on the other side of the fire, her pine cones forgotten. Her cheeks are pink from the heat. Sela, behind her, has finished feeding the baby, and is holding Lu up on her shoulder, patting her back, and Lu is looking at the fire over her mother's shoulder with wide, solemn eyes.

Outside, the last grey light is almost gone. The cold is sinking down into the valley like water filling a bowl. If you stepped out of the shelter now, you'd feel it at once, the bite in your nose, the ache in your fingers. Fifty-five thousand years ago, this part of the world was colder than it is today. The great ice sheets of the north lay over Scandinavia and the far north, and even here, in the south, the winters were long and hard, and the forests were the dark, cold-loving forests of pine, not the oak and olive of the Mediterranean you might know.

Mora sits back on her heels for a moment and looks at the fire. It's burning well. Low and steady, in its shallow black bed on the floor of the shelter, giving out a circle of warmth perhaps two or three strides across. Inside that circle, a person can live through the night. Outside it, the cold is waiting.

And somewhere in the dark down the valley, there's a sound. A stone turning underfoot. A low call. Orrin lifts his head.

The hunters are coming home.

## Part Three — Around Six O'Clock: The Hunters Come Home

The small question for this hour is a hunter's question. What have they brought back with them tonight? And, just as important, how did they get it?

Hold that thought. Let's go and meet them.

Three shapes come up out of the dark below the shelter, climbing the slope from the river, and step one by one into the circle of firelight. The first is Ket. He's broad and heavy-shouldered, as all the men of his people are, with a deep chest built like a barrel, short, thick forearms, and legs that seem made for walking all day over broken ground. Across his shoulders, held by its legs, he's carrying the hindquarters of a red deer, a hind, still wrapped in its winter coat. Behind him comes Dun, carrying another great piece of the same animal, a foreleg and part of the ribs, and a long wooden spear held upright in his other hand, its point dark with blood. And last of all, out of breath, his face shining with sweat and cold and something that looks very much like pride, comes Hesk, who is fifteen, carrying the head.

Mora rises to meet them. Sela comes forward with the baby on her hip. Tavi runs. Pim tries to run too, and trips over a hide, and gets up again. Orrin stays where he is by the fire, but he leans forward, and his face, in the firelight, is very bright.

There's no shouting. No great celebration. But there is a warm, low murmur of sound, the sounds people make when the people they love have come home safe from danger, with food. Ket sets down the hindquarters on a hide near the fire with a long breath of relief. Dun lays the spear against the rock. Hesk puts down the deer's head, very carefully, as if it were made of glass, and stands looking at it.

It's worth pausing to think about what they've just done. A grown red deer hind is a big animal. Depending on the place and the season, she might weigh as much as a grown man, or more, and the three hunters have carried most of her, in pieces, up a frozen slope in the dark. They will have butchered her roughly where she fell, taking the heaviest, richest parts, the haunches and the ribs and the head with its tongue and brain, and leaving behind what wasn't worth the carrying. Archaeologists who study the bones from shelters like this one spend a great deal of time asking exactly which parts of each animal were brought home, because the answer tells them how far away the kill was, and how many people did the carrying.

So that's the first half of our small question. Tonight, they've brought back red deer.

That's no accident. When the archaeologists of Abric Romaní looked at the animal bones from layer after layer of this shelter, they found the same pattern again and again. In every level that's been excavated, the people here showed a clear preference for two kinds of animal above all others: red deer, and horses. Other creatures turn up too, now and then, but deer and horse were the heart of life in this valley, the meat, the fat, the hides, the bone, the sinew. If the people of this shelter had a word for food, there's a fair chance it would have meant one of those two.

And the bones tell us more than that. By looking at the teeth of the animals, which wear down in a predictable way as an animal gets older, scientists can estimate how old each one was when it died. In 2017, a team including Juan Marín, Palmira Saladié and Eudald Carbonell published a study in the journal PLOS ONE looking at the ages of the deer and horses killed in level after level here. They found that the horses were mostly prime adults, animals in the strength of their lives, as if the hunters had been choosing them deliberately. But the deer were more varied, young and old and in between. The authors concluded that the Neanderthals of Abric Romaní used more than one way of hunting. Sometimes they picked out particular animals. Sometimes they took whatever the hunt gave them.

Earlier work, led by Carmen Fernández-Laso with Florent Rivals and Jordi Rosell, looked at the fine scratches on the animals' teeth, which change with the seasons as an animal's diet changes, from soft spring grass to tough winter browse. They found that in different levels of this shelter, the animals had been hunted at different times of the year, and they argued that the people came here seasonally, following the movements of their prey. So the group we're spending the night with may not live here all year round. They may have come to this valley for the winter, because the deer come down here, out of the high hills, when the snow lies deep above.

Now the second half of our small question. How did they get it?

Imagine that you were with them this afternoon. Imagine you're lying flat on cold ground at the edge of a stand of pines, high above the river, with frost on the needles under your hands, and Ket beside you, so still that he barely seems to breathe. Below you, in a little clearing by a stream, there are five red deer, hinds and young ones, grazing. You can see their breath in the air. You can hear them tearing at the grass. Dun is somewhere off to your left, out of sight, working his way down the slope behind them. Hesk is to the right. And all of you are waiting.

Ket has a spear. It's a long, straight shaft of wood, taller than he is, its point shaped and hardened, or tipped with a sharp flake of stone. He won't throw it from here. It's too far. The way his people hunted, so far as we can tell, was to get close, very close, much closer than any modern hunter with a rifle would ever need to, and then to strike.

We know they had wooden spears, because a few, against all the odds, have survived. The most famous were found in a coal mine at Schöningen, in northern Germany, in the 1990s, and published by the archaeologist Hartmut Thieme in 1997. Their exact age has been argued over and revised more than once, but they are certainly very old. They are long, beautifully shaped hunting spears of spruce and pine, made a few hundred thousand years ago, by people who were probably the ancestors of the Neanderthals. And here, at Abric Romaní, the travertine has preserved something extraordinary. When wooden objects lay on the floor of the shelter and the mineral-rich water washed over them, it coated them in stone. The wood rotted away inside, over thousands of years, but it left a hollow behind, a mould of exactly the shape it had been, sometimes so finely made that you can see the cells of the wood. In October 2026, a team led by Palmira Saladié published a study in the journal Science Advances of five hundred and ninety-seven wooden remains from sixteen levels of this shelter, from about sixty-two thousand to forty thousand years ago. Among them, they identified thirteen worked or used wooden artefacts, including two spears.

So Ket's spear is a real kind of spear, made in a real way, in this very place.

The deer at the stream lifts her head. Something has changed. She's caught a scent, or a sound, from behind her, from Dun's side of the slope. She steps forward, uneasy, towards the trees. Towards you. Towards Ket. Closer. Closer. And then Ket is up and moving, faster than you would ever have imagined such a heavy man could move, and it's over in a moment.

I'll leave the rest of the hunt there, in the trees, in the cold. It's enough to know that it happened, and that everyone came home.

And here we come to a point where scientists have disagreed, and it's about what that kind of hunting cost the hunters.

In 1995, two anthropologists, Thomas Berger and Erik Trinkaus, published a famous study in the Journal of Archaeological Science. They had gathered together the injuries seen on Neanderthal skeletons, the healed fractures and old wounds, and they noticed that a great many were in the head and neck, and in the upper body. They compared that pattern with the injuries suffered by people in modern jobs, and found that the closest match was a surprising one: professional rodeo riders. People who are thrown, and kicked, and crushed, by large, angry animals. Berger and Trinkaus suggested that this was what close-range hunting of big game did to a Neanderthal body. Life, in this view, was violent, dangerous, and hard on everyone who hunted.

But in 2018, a team led by Judith Beier and Katerina Harvati, at the University of Tübingen, published a much larger study in the journal Nature. They compared the skulls of Neanderthals with the skulls of early modern humans who lived in Europe at around the same time, and looked at the rate of injuries in each. And they found that the rates were broadly similar. Neanderthals were not, it seemed, more battered than our own ancestors. Young Neanderthal men did seem to suffer more injuries than young women, and there were hints that Neanderthals were injured younger, on average. But the picture of the Neanderthal as uniquely broken by a uniquely brutal life didn't hold up. The debate has gone on since, and other researchers have pointed out that it's very hard to tell, from a healed fracture, exactly how it happened. But the rodeo rider, like the stooped brute of La Chapelle, may be less the whole truth than we once thought.

Back in the shelter, Hesk is sitting near the fire now, and Orrin has shuffled over beside him. Orrin doesn't say much. He can't hear much, either, as we'll find out later tonight. But he takes the boy's face in his one good hand, and turns it to the firelight, and looks at it. There's a long scratch on Hesk's cheek, from a branch. Nothing else. Orrin nods, and lets go, and pats him once on the shoulder, heavily, the way a man might pat a horse. Hesk ducks his head. He's trying very hard not to smile.

Let's set this moment beside modern life for a moment. When you want meat for your supper, you probably go to a supermarket, or a butcher, and you choose a piece wrapped in plastic, already cut and cleaned and weighed, with a price on a label. You may never in your life have seen the animal it came from. Most of us, today, are many steps removed from that clearing by the stream. For the people in this shelter, there are no steps at all. The deer that's lying on the hide by the fire was alive this afternoon. Hesk carried her head on his own shoulders up from the river. Every part of her will be used, and nothing will be wasted, because tomorrow there may be nothing at all.

And that's worth remembering, as we watch them. The red deer is not just food. Her hide will become a cloak, or a cover for the night, or a bag. Her sinews, dried and split, will become thread and cord. Her bones will be cracked for their marrow, which is one of the richest foods there is, and the broken pieces may become tools. Her fat is perhaps the most precious thing of all, because in a cold winter, fat is fuel, for the body and sometimes for the fire. And even her teeth and antlers might find a use. A single red deer hind, a large animal, might give enough meat to feed a group of nine for some days, if they're careful, and if the cold keeps the meat from spoiling.

Ket is crouching by the hindquarters now, looking at them in the firelight, turning them a little. Dun has gone to the back of the shelter, where a pile of stone flakes and pebbles lies on a hide, and he's choosing. He picks up one, and weighs it in his hand, and puts it down. Picks up another. Holds it to the light. Runs his thumb, very gently, along its edge.

Mora has drawn the fire a little wider, laying on more pine, so that its light will reach the place where the butchering will be done. Sela has given the baby to Tavi to hold, and has come to kneel by the meat. Pim is already as close as he's allowed to be, and a little closer.

Outside, the dark is complete. There's no moon yet. The stars are out over the valley, sharp and many, and somewhere on the far slope, very faint, a wolf is calling, and another answers it, and then they're quiet.

Nobody in the shelter even turns their head. Wolves are a sound of the night, like the river. And the fire is between them and the dark.

Dun has found the stone he wanted. He comes back to the fire, and hands it to Ket, and Ket takes it, and feels its edge, and nods.

It's time to begin.

## Part Four — Around Seven O'Clock: Stone, Sap and Glue

Here is this hour's small question, and it's a practical one. How do you take apart a whole red deer, skin, meat, sinew and bone, without a single piece of metal?

The answer is in Ket's hand. It's the stone that Dun chose for him from the pile at the back of the shelter: a flake of grey stone about the length of your finger, thin at one edge and thicker at the other, with no handle at all. It doesn't look like much. If you found it lying on a path, you might kick it aside. But its thin edge is sharper than almost any steel knife you've ever held. Freshly struck stone of the right kind can have an edge finer than a surgeon's scalpel, at least for the first few cuts.

Imagine that you're kneeling beside Ket now, on the hide, in the warm orange light of the fire, and that he passes you the flake. Hold it the way he holds it, between your thumb and two fingers, with the thick back of it against the pad of your forefinger. It's cool and smooth on its flat side, and faintly rippled on the other, like water. Now draw it, very lightly, along the inside of the deer's leg, where the hide is thinnest. And feel how easily it parts. There's no sawing. No tearing. The edge just slips through, as if the skin had been waiting to open.

Ket takes it back from you and goes on. He works quickly, but he doesn't hurry. He's done this hundreds of times. He opens the hide along the legs and the belly, and then begins to work it back from the meat, cutting with short strokes where it clings, pulling where it doesn't. Sela works alongside him on the other side. Between them, they lift the hide away in one great piece, heavy and steaming in the cold air, and lay it, fur side down, on the floor of the shelter. Later, it will be scraped and dried and worked. Tonight, it's set aside.

Then the meat. The flake goes dull after a while, as all stone edges do, and Ket simply puts it down and reaches for another. Dun is sitting nearby with a rounded river cobble in one hand and a larger lump of stone in the other, and every so often he turns the lump, looks at it carefully, and strikes it with the cobble, a sharp, ringing knock that echoes off the back of the shelter. And each time, a new flake springs away, ready to use.

That's the answer to our small question. You take apart a deer with stone. Not with one tool, but with many, made as you need them, used until they're dull, and then dropped, and replaced. That's why archaeologists find so many stone flakes on the floors of shelters like this one, tens of thousands of them, many with tiny chips and polishes along their edges that show what they were used for. And it's also why they find the bones of the animals covered in fine, straight cut marks, exactly where the meat was taken off the bone, exactly where the tendons were cut, exactly where the hide was opened. The marks of Ket's flake, in a sense, are the signature of everyone who ever butchered a deer at this hearth.

Neanderthals were extraordinarily skilled at this. Many of them used a method that archaeologists call the Levallois technique, named after a suburb of Paris where such tools were first found in the nineteenth century, in which a lump of stone is carefully shaped in advance, like a sculptor roughing out a block of marble, so that a single final blow will strike off a flake of a planned size and shape. Other groups, including many of the people who lived here at Abric Romaní, used different methods, working their way around a core of stone in a circle, striking off flake after flake. Either way, it takes years to learn, and a kind of patience, and a good eye. It's a skill that most modern people, even archaeologists who've spent decades practising, find genuinely difficult.

Ket has set aside the best pieces of meat now, and Sela has cut some of them into strips, and laid them near the fire on the edge of the hearth, on flat stones, to begin cooking. The smell of it rises with the smoke. Pim sits up very straight. Even the baby, on Tavi's lap, turns her head.

But Dun isn't eating yet. He's doing something else.

He has taken a new flake, a good one, thin and evenly shaped, with a straight edge and a slightly pointed tip. And from a fold of hide at his belt, he's taken out something wrapped in a leaf. He unwraps it. It's a lump of something hard and golden-brown and translucent, the size of a walnut, glinting in the firelight like amber. And he holds it, together with the flake, near the fire. Not in it. Near it. At the edge of the hearth, where the heat is steady but not fierce. Every so often he draws his hand back, and touches the lump with his thumb, and holds it out again. And then, after a while, he holds the back of his own hand towards the coals, at the same distance, as if he's testing the heat against his own skin.

So here, at last, is the first question I asked you at the very beginning of the night. Why would a hunter sit by the fire, holding a lump of hard, golden tree-sap and a sharp flake of stone, and warm them both, patiently, at exactly the distance from the coals that he can bear on the back of his hand?

The answer is that he is making glue.

The lump in his hand is pine resin, the thick, sticky sap that seeps out of a pine tree wherever its bark is wounded, and which hardens, over days and weeks, into a golden crust. Cold, it's as hard as stone. Warmed gently, it softens, and becomes sticky, and can be moulded with the fingers. And then, as it cools again, it sets hard, and grips whatever it's touching. Dun is going to use it to fix this flake to the end of a wooden shaft, to make a new spear point, or perhaps to fix it into a short wooden handle, to make a knife that he can grip with his whole fist, and use with real force.

And the reason he's being so careful, testing the heat against his own hand, is that resin is fussy. Too cold, and it won't soften. Too hot, and it bubbles and burns and turns brittle, and the joint will fail the first time it's struck. It has to be warmed slowly, at just the right distance from the fire, and then worked quickly, while it's soft, and pressed firmly around the base of the stone and into a notch in the wood. Then perhaps bound around with a strip of wet sinew, which will shrink as it dries, and tighten. That's why the back of the hand. He's using his own skin as a thermometer. He knows, from long experience, the warmth that makes resin soft and the warmth that ruins it.

So the first mystery is solved. He warms the sap and the stone together because together, with the fire's help, they become something more than either: a hafted tool. A stone blade with a handle, or a stone point on a spear. And that, in the long story of technology, is a very big deal indeed. It's among the oldest known examples, anywhere in the world, of anyone taking two or three entirely different materials, wood and stone and a sticky substance, and joining them, permanently, into a single tool.

Now, how do we know that Neanderthals did this?

There are a number of clues, scattered across Europe and beyond. In 2019, a team led by Ilaria Degano published a study in the journal PLOS ONE of stone tools from two caves in the Lazio region of central Italy, Grotta del Fossellone and Grotta di Sant'Agostino, left by Neanderthals somewhere between about fifty-five thousand and forty thousand years ago. On some of those tools, they found chemical traces of exactly what Dun has in his hand: pine resin, used as an adhesive, and in some cases mixed with beeswax. In the 1990s, at a site called Umm el Tlel in Syria, archaeologists led by Éric Boëda found traces of bitumen, a natural tar that seeps out of the ground, on Neanderthal stone tools, apparently used to haft them. And in 2024, a team including Patrick Schmidt and Radu Iovita restudied old museum collections of stone tools from Le Moustier, in south-western France, the very site that gave its name to the Mousterian, the tool-making tradition of the Neanderthals. They found traces of a carefully mixed paste of ochre and bitumen, which, they argued, had been used to make grips, so that a stone tool could be held comfortably in the hand.

But the most famous Neanderthal glue of all is not resin. It's tar, made from the bark of birch trees.

Birch bark tar is a thick, black, sticky substance that you can't simply collect from a tree. It has to be made. You heat birch bark without letting it burn, and a dark tar oozes out of it. And Neanderthal birch tar has been found in several places. Stone flakes partly coated in it were found in a quarry at Campitello, in Tuscany, and a team led by Paul Mazza published them in 2006. They are around two hundred thousand years old. Lumps of it, one still bearing the imprint of the stone tool it once held, were found at Königsaue, in Germany, and published by Johann Koller and his colleagues in 2001. And in 2019, a team led by Marcel Niekus published a stone flake with a lump of birch tar still on it, found washed up on a man-made beach called the Zandmotor, on the Dutch coast near The Hague, where sand dredged from the floor of the North Sea had been pumped ashore. That flake is around fifty thousand years old. The same age, more or less, as our night.

Whether birch trees grew close to this valley, on this particular night, I can't tell you for certain. The forests here were dominated by pine. So I've given Dun the resin of the pine, the tree that was everywhere around him. But somewhere to the north, beyond the mountains, other Neanderthals were very likely sitting at other fires, tonight or on nights very like it, making their glue in the other way, with the bark of the birch.

And here is where the scientists disagree. How difficult was it, really, to make birch tar? And what does it tell us about the minds of the people who made it?

In 2017, a team led by Paul Kozowyk, at Leiden University, published a study in the journal Scientific Reports in which they tried to make birch tar themselves, using only materials and methods that Neanderthals could have had. They tested several methods, from burying rolls of bark under hot embers in a pit, to heating them under a stone. Some methods worked better than others, and all of them took skill, care and, in some cases, quite a lot of bark for very little tar. Many researchers took this as a sign that Neanderthal tar-making involved planning, experience, and a real understanding of how materials behave, the kind of thinking that some had once believed only our own species was capable of.

But in 2019, a team led by Patrick Schmidt, at the University of Tübingen, published a paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, with a title that went straight to the point. They argued that birch tar extraction does not prove Neanderthal behavioural complexity. They had found that, if you simply burn birch bark close to a smooth, cold stone surface, a little tar condenses onto the stone, and you can scrape it off. No pit, no buried roll, no careful control of the air. It's slow, and it gives only small amounts. But it works. And they suggested that it might have been discovered by accident, by anyone who happened to burn birch bark near a stone.

Other researchers have pushed back, in their turn, arguing that even the simplest method takes knowledge, and that the amounts of tar found on some Neanderthal tools would need a great deal of bark, and patience. The debate goes on. But I think that what both sides agree on is the thing we can see in front of us now, in the firelight. Dun knows his material. He knows exactly how warm the resin should be, how long to hold it, how fast to work. Whether that knowledge came from a flash of insight, or from a long line of fathers and mothers patiently showing their children, it's real knowledge, held in his hands.

Let's set this beside your own home for a moment. Somewhere in a kitchen drawer, you probably have a tube of glue. Superglue, perhaps, that sets in seconds, or a glue gun, which works on exactly the same principle as Dun's resin: heat it until it softens, apply it, and let it cool and set. You might use it to mend a broken mug, or to fix a loose sole on a shoe. You probably don't think about it at all. But the glue in that tube is the far, far descendant of the lump in Dun's hand. And he made his himself, from a tree, with nothing but a fire and the back of his hand to tell him when it was right.

Dun has worked the warm resin around the base of the flake now, and pressed it into a notch he cut earlier, with another flake, in the end of a short, thick stick of wood. He winds a strip of damp sinew around the joint, round and round, and ties it off with his teeth. Then he holds the new knife up to the firelight, and turns it, and looks at it closely, the way a carpenter might look along the edge of a newly planed board.

Then he lays it down on the edge of the hearth, not too close, to let the resin harden slowly as it cools. Tomorrow, it'll be ready.

On the flat stones at the edge of the fire, the strips of deer meat are hissing. The smell has filled the whole shelter. Pim can bear it no longer, and reaches out a hand, and Mora, without looking, catches his wrist and moves it gently back. Not yet. Soon.

## Part Five — Around a Quarter to Eight: Hides Against the Cold

The small question for this part of the evening is about the things on everyone's backs. On a winter night in the Ice Age, what exactly are these people wearing? And how much of their warmth comes from what they wear, and how much from the bodies they were born with?

Let's start with the wearing.

Mora is kneeling by the fire, feeding it, and over her shoulders and back she has a great, soft, heavy hide, with the fur turned inwards against her skin. It's the hide of a red deer, from an earlier hunt, scraped clean on its inner side and worked until it's supple. It falls from her shoulders almost to her knees, and she holds it closed in front with one hand when she sits back, and lets it fall open when she leans forward to the fire. Around her lower legs and feet, she has smaller pieces of hide, wrapped and tied with cord. That's all. No buttons. No seams to speak of. No sleeves.

Sela is wearing something similar, though hers is horse hide, a little stiffer, and she's tucked the baby inside it, against her own body, so that Lu is wrapped in her mother's warmth and the hide's warmth together, with only the top of her head showing. Tavi and Pim each have a smaller hide, a child's hide, which Mora made for them last winter. And the men, now that they've stopped working and the sweat of the hunt has cooled on them, have pulled their hides back round their shoulders too, and are sitting closer to the fire than they were.

Imagine that Mora turns to you now and holds out a hide for you, a spare one, rolled up at the back of the shelter. You take it. It's heavier than you expect, and it smells strongly, of smoke and animal and fat. The fur on the inside is thick and coarse and wonderfully warm. You wrap it around your shoulders, the way she does, and pull it close in front. And at once, the cold on your back, which has been creeping into you since you arrived, is gone. The firelight warms your front. The hide holds the warmth against your back. Between the two, you are, for the first time tonight, actually comfortable.

That's the first half of our answer. They wear the skins of the animals they hunt, draped and wrapped and tied, fur side in.

Now, how do we know? Clothing almost never survives from this far back. Hide and fur and sinew rot. But the archaeologists have found clever ways to look for it.

One way is to look at the tools. Under a powerful microscope, a stone tool that has been used to scrape a hide develops a particular kind of polish and wear along its edge, quite different from the wear left by cutting meat or working wood. Researchers have found that kind of wear on stone tools from many Neanderthal sites. And at two sites in south-western France, Pech-de-l'Azé I and Abri Peyrony, a team led by Marie Soressi, the same archaeologist we met earlier with the fire-making hand-axes, found something more. In a paper published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in 2013, they described small, smoothed tools made of bone, from the ribs of deer and other animals, around fifty thousand years old. Their shape and wear closely match a tool still used by leatherworkers today, called a lissoir, a smoother, which is rubbed against a hide to make it softer, glossier, and more waterproof. It was one of the earliest specialised bone tools ever found in Europe, and it was made by Neanderthals.

So these people didn't just wear raw hides. They worked them. They scraped them and softened them and smoothed them, and they very probably used fat and smoke, as many hunting peoples have, to keep them supple.

The smoke matters more than you might think, and it brings us back to the hearth. Look up, now, at the underside of the overhang, just beyond the reach of the flames. There, on a frame of sticks propped against the rock, Mora has hung a hide from an earlier hunt, and the smoke of the fire is drifting up around it and through it. Among traditional hide-workers in many parts of the world, a hide that has been softened and then smoked keeps its softness far better when it gets wet. An unsmoked hide that's soaked in rain or snow can dry stiff and hard again. A smoked one dries soft. Whether Neanderthals smoked their hides deliberately, we can't prove. But they spent their winters in smoke, and their hides would have hung in it. And the fire, which cooks the food and warms the bodies, would also, quietly, have been curing the clothes.

Here's a gentle, true thing about hides that you may not know. When a deer's hide is fresh, it's full of water and fat, and as it dries it goes stiff as a board. To make it soft and flexible, it has to be worked, stretched and pulled and rubbed and kneaded, for hours, as it dries. In many traditional societies, the brains of the animal itself were used to soften its hide, rubbed into the skin. There's an old saying among some North American hide-tanners that every animal has just enough brain to tan its own hide. Whether Neanderthals knew that trick, we can't say. But they had the deer, and the time, and the need.

And here's where the scientists have disagreed, about how much these people wore, and how it was made.

In 2016, a team led by Mark Collard, of Simon Fraser University in Canada, published a study in the Journal of Anthropological Archaeology. They looked at the bones of animals found at Neanderthal sites, and at sites left by the early modern humans who came into Europe later, and they asked a simple question. Which small, fur-bearing animals were people catching? Because if you want to make close-fitting, tailored clothing, the kind that wraps the arms and legs and seals out the cold, you need certain kinds of fur. The fur of the wolverine, for example, which doesn't frost up with the moisture of your breath, and was used by Arctic peoples for the edges of hoods. Or the soft, warm fur of hares, foxes and other small animals, sewn into trimmings and linings. Collard's team found that the early modern human sites had far more of these small fur-bearing animals than the Neanderthal sites did. They argued that Neanderthals probably wore simpler clothing, capes and wraps, much like the ones we've been imagining, while our own ancestors made more tailored, close-fitting clothes.

But other researchers have pointed out that this argument rests on what's missing, rather than what's there, and that absence of evidence is always harder to read. Neanderthals may have used other materials we can't easily see. And the eyed bone needle, the tool you'd expect for fine sewing, doesn't appear in Europe until after modern humans arrived, but people can sew with an awl, a simple pointed tool that punches holes, and thread the sinew through by hand. Some Neanderthal sites have pointed bone tools that might have served that way. So the honest answer is that we don't know quite how the Neanderthals dressed, only that they did, and that it was enough to see them through hundreds of thousands of years of ice-age winters.

Now the second half of our small question. How much of their warmth comes from their own bodies?

Look at Ket, sitting by the fire. Compared with you, he is short, but immensely heavy for his height. His chest is broad and deep. His forearms and his shins are short compared with his upper arms and thighs. His hands are wide and strong. He's built, in a word, like a barrel. And that shape may be no accident.

Biologists have long noticed that among warm-blooded animals, those that live in cold places tend to have stockier bodies and shorter limbs than their relatives in warm places. The idea is simple. A compact, round body has less surface area, for its size, through which to lose heat, like a ball compared with a long, thin stick. Many anthropologists have argued that the Neanderthal body, with its broad chest and short limbs, was at least partly an adaptation to the cold of ice-age Europe. It's the same principle that makes an Arctic fox smaller-eared and rounder than a desert fox.

But here, too, there's an argument. For a long time, people believed that the famously large Neanderthal nose was another cold adaptation, a kind of radiator that warmed and moistened the freezing air before it reached the lungs. Then, in 2011, a team including Todd Rae, Thomas Koppe and Chris Stringer published a study in the Journal of Human Evolution with a pointed title: "The Neanderthal face is not cold adapted." They looked at the sinuses and the shape of the face, and argued that they didn't fit the pattern you'd expect for a cold-adapted face at all. The big nose and the broad middle of the face, in their view, may have more to do with the Neanderthals' ancestry, or with the needs of a big, active body breathing hard. Other researchers continue to argue that the nose did help with breathing cold, dry air. It's still discussed.

What isn't really in doubt is that these bodies needed a great deal of fuel. A Neanderthal was a large, heavy, very muscular person, living an active life in a cold climate, and keeping all of that warm and moving takes energy. In 2001, the anthropologists Mark Sorensen and William Leonard estimated the daily energy needs of Neanderthals, and others, like Steven Churchill in his 2014 book Thin on the Ground, have explored the same question since. Their estimates vary, but they suggest that a Neanderthal adult in a cold climate may have needed something like three thousand to five thousand calories a day, perhaps more for a hunter in the hardest weeks of winter. For comparison, a modern adult is often advised to eat something like two thousand to two and a half thousand. Ket, by that reckoning, needs to eat perhaps twice what you do, just to stay alive and warm.

That's the second half of our answer, then. Their warmth comes from three places at once: from the hides they wear, from the fire in front of them, and from bodies built strong and broad and fuelled by a great deal of meat and fat.

Let's put that beside modern life for a moment. If you open your wardrobe, you'll find more clothing in it than this whole group owns between them, probably many times more. Much of it is made from fibres that would astonish them. Fleece made from plastic. Down jackets stuffed with the feathers of birds and held in by woven nylon. Thermal layers that pull moisture away from your skin. A good winter coat, today, might keep you warm at temperatures far below freezing for hours. And yet you couldn't live through a night in this shelter in it alone. Because what keeps these people alive isn't one garment, or one fire, or one meal. It's all of them, together, every day.

The meat on the flat stones by the hearth is ready now. Sela has been turning the strips with a stick, and they've browned and blackened at the edges, and the fat is crackling. Mora takes one first, and blows on it, and tears it in two, and gives half to Pim, who has been waiting, it seems to him, his whole life for this moment. Then the rest of the group reaches in, one by one.

They eat sitting close around the hearth, hides pulled round their shoulders, faces lit orange, backs in shadow. Nobody uses a plate. Nobody uses a fork. They hold the meat in their fingers and cut it, sometimes, with a stone flake, holding one end in their teeth and slicing close to their lips. That's not a guess, by the way. Many Neanderthal front teeth have fine scratches on them, made by stone tools, and the angle of those scratches, mostly slanting the same way, has been used by researchers, such as David Frayer and his colleagues, to suggest that most Neanderthals were right-handed, just as most of us are.

Hesk eats as though he hasn't eaten for a week, which, for a fifteen-year-old who hunted all day in the cold, is close to the truth. Ket eats slowly, steadily, watching the fire. Dun has his new knife lying beside him, cooling, and every so often he glances down at it.

And Orrin is eating too. He's sitting in the warmest place of all, right at the edge of the hearth, with his back against a stone, and Sela has cut his meat for him into small pieces and laid them on a flat stone beside him, within reach of his good left hand. He eats them one by one. Nobody comments. Nobody needs to.

We'll come back to that, in the deep of the night. Why Orrin has the warmest place. For now, just notice it.

Outside, the frost is forming on the stones at the mouth of the shelter, white and fine as salt. The river is louder now, in the stillness. The stars have wheeled a little further across the sky. And the fire, fed again and again by Mora's patient hands, burns low and orange and steady, at the heart of the circle.

## Part Six — Around Half Past Eight: Marrow, Mushrooms and the Taste of Smoke

Here is this hour's small question. We've watched these people eat meat, and a great deal of it. But did they eat anything else? Or were the Neanderthals, as people once liked to say, simply hunters of big game and nothing more?

The meat is almost gone now, the first strips of it at least. The rest of the deer is hanging, or wrapped, or set aside at the cold back of the shelter, where the frost will keep it until tomorrow. And around the hearth, something else is happening. Ket has taken one of the long leg bones of the deer, stripped now of its meat, and he's laid it across a flat stone at the edge of the fire, to warm it a little. Then he takes a heavy, rounded river cobble in his good right hand and, with one sharp, well-aimed blow, cracks the bone open along its length.

Inside, there's marrow. Pale, soft, pinkish-yellow, the texture of firm butter, running in a long rope down the hollow middle of the bone. Ket scoops some out with his fingers and passes the bone to Mora, and she passes it on, and it goes around the hearth from hand to hand.

Imagine that it reaches you. Imagine that Tavi turns and holds it out to you, with a little nod, as if to say, go on. You take it. It's warm from the fire, and heavier than it looks, and slippery. You scoop a little of the marrow out with your finger, the way they did, and taste it. It's rich. Astonishingly rich. Mild and slightly sweet, almost nutty, and so full of fat that one mouthful is like eating a spoonful of cream. In a world without sugar, without bread, without oil in bottles, this is about the most delicious and the most valuable thing a person can eat. Fat is warmth. Fat is strength. Fat is the difference, in a hard winter, between a body that keeps going and a body that doesn't.

That's part of why archaeologists find, in shelters like this one, so many animal bones broken open lengthways in exactly this way, with the marks of the cobble's blow still on them. The people here weren't just eating the meat. They were getting every last scrap of the fat.

Now, here's where we come to the question of whether that was all they ate.

For a long time, the answer seemed to be yes, more or less. In 2009, the archaeologist Michael Richards and the anthropologist Erik Trinkaus published a study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, drawing together the results of a technique called stable isotope analysis. The idea is that the chemistry of the food you eat leaves a kind of signature in your bones, in the proportions of slightly different forms of carbon and nitrogen. And when they measured the bones of Neanderthals from across Europe, the signatures looked like those of top predators, wolves and lions and hyenas, animals that ate mostly the meat of other large animals. Neanderthals, in this view, were among the most carnivorous people who have ever lived.

But then the scientists began to look somewhere else. Not at the bones, but at the teeth. Or more precisely, at the hard, yellowish crust that builds up on teeth when they aren't brushed, the stuff your dentist scrapes off and calls tartar, and which scientists call dental calculus. Calculus forms in layers, and as it forms, it traps tiny pieces of whatever passes through the mouth: grains of starch, specks of plant tissue, fragments of smoke, even the DNA of the microbes that lived there. And it can survive for tens of thousands of years.

In 2011, a team including Amanda Henry, Alison Brooks and Dolores Piperno published a study, also in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, of the calculus on the teeth of two Neanderthals: one from Shanidar Cave, in what is now northern Iraq, and one from the cave of Spy, in Belgium. Trapped in it, they found grains of starch from plants. Some came from wild relatives of barley and other grasses. Others came from legumes, plants of the pea and bean family, and some from what looked like date palms. And many of the starch grains were swollen and damaged in a particular way, the way starch is damaged when it's cooked. These Neanderthals had been eating cooked plants.

Then, in 2012, a team led by Karen Hardy published a study of the calculus of Neanderthals from El Sidrón, a cave in Asturias, in northern Spain. They found traces of cooked starch there too, and of wood smoke, from sitting by fires. And they found chemical traces of two bitter-tasting plants, yarrow and chamomile. These have very little food value. Hardy and her colleagues raised the possibility that the Neanderthals of El Sidrón might have eaten them for another reason, perhaps as medicine.

And in 2017, a team led by Laura Weyrich, working with Alan Cooper and others, published a study in the journal Nature of the DNA preserved in Neanderthal calculus. In the calculus of the Neanderthals from Spy, in Belgium, they found the DNA of woolly rhinoceros and wild sheep, the great beasts of the northern steppe. But in the calculus from El Sidrón, in the forests of northern Spain, they found something quite different: the DNA of mushrooms, pine nuts and moss, and very little sign of meat at all. And in the mouth of one young man from El Sidrón, who had a painful abscess in his jaw, they found traces of poplar, a tree whose bark contains salicylic acid, the natural painkiller from which aspirin was eventually made, and of a mould of the kind that gives us penicillin. Whether he was deliberately treating himself, or had simply eaten something mouldy, nobody can say for sure. But it's a striking thing to find.

So that's the answer to our small question. No, the Neanderthals were not simply meat-eaters. In some places, at some times, they ate a great deal of meat, perhaps almost nothing else. In others, they ate plants, nuts, mushrooms and seeds, some of them cooked. They ate what their land gave them.

And here's the disagreement. Not about whether they ate plants, now, but about what the evidence really shows. The isotopes from the bones suggest lots of meat. The calculus suggests plants as well. Can both be right? Some researchers point out that isotope analysis mainly picks up protein, so a diet with plenty of plant starch but most of its protein from meat would still look carnivorous. Others urge caution about the calculus. In 2016, a team including Anita Radini and Karen Hardy, the same Hardy who'd found the yarrow and chamomile, published a study in the journal Antiquity pointing out that not everything trapped in calculus was necessarily eaten. Some particles might have been breathed in, from smoke or dust. Some might have come from chewing wood or plant fibres as tools, holding them in the teeth while working them. A grain in the tartar, in other words, isn't always a meal. The debate is a lively one, and it's slowly bringing the two kinds of evidence together into a single, more varied picture.

Here in the valley of the Anoia, on this winter night, I'd like to imagine that the meal has had a little of both. The deer, of course. And something else. Earlier today, while the hunters were away, Mora and Sela and the children went down along the river with baskets, perhaps of bark or woven grass, and gathered what the season offered. Late in the year, that wouldn't be much. Perhaps some roots dug from the soft ground by the water. Perhaps the last of the autumn's nuts, or seeds, or dried berries saved from weeks ago. Remember that in October 2026, the Abric Romaní team reported four trays made of bark among the wooden objects preserved in the travertine of this shelter. A bark tray is exactly the kind of thing you'd use to carry roots or nuts, or to hold food by the fire. So when Mora sets a bark tray of roasted roots down beside the hearth tonight, she's using a kind of object that really was used here, under this very rock.

The same study described something else among the moulds of wood preserved in the travertine: two wooden poles, crossed, which the team suggested might have supported some kind of platform, perhaps for preparing food. We can't be sure what it was. Wood that survives only as a hollow in stone keeps its shape, but not its story. But I like to imagine a simple frame of crossed poles a little way back from Mora's hearth tonight, with strips of deer meat laid across it, drying slowly in the warm smoke, the way meat has been dried by hunting peoples all over the world. Meat that's been dried and smoked keeps for days or weeks longer than fresh meat. It's lighter to carry. And it would be a sensible thing to do with a whole deer, on a night when nine people can't possibly eat it all.

Let's set this beside modern life for a moment. In your kitchen, there's probably a refrigerator, humming quietly, keeping food fresh for days or weeks. There may be a freezer, keeping it for months. There's a cupboard full of things in packets and tins that would last for years. And there's a shop, not far away, open almost all the time, where you can buy almost any food in the world in any season. For this group, there's none of that. There's what was hunted today, what was gathered today, and what was saved from the days before. The cold of winter is their refrigerator. The back of the shelter is their larder. That's another thing the teeth have told us. In 2018, a team led by Tanya Smith published a study in the journal Science Advances of the teeth of two Neanderthal children from a site called Payre, in south-eastern France. Teeth grow in tiny daily layers, a little like the rings of a tree, and as they grow, they lock in the chemistry of the child's body at that moment. By measuring an element called barium, which rises when a baby is nursing, the team could trace when these children began to eat other foods, and when they stopped nursing altogether. One of them seems to have been fully weaned at about two and a half years old, which is within the range of many modern hunter-gatherer children. The same teeth also showed, in their layers, signs of stress that came again and again in the winter months, when food was scarcer and illness more common. Winters were hard on children. That's one more reason why Sela keeps Lu so close tonight, inside the hide, against her own warmth.

Smell it, if you can. That's the smell of this shelter, and of every shelter like it, in every winter for hundreds of thousands of years. Pine smoke, and roasting meat, and hot fat, and damp stone, and the warm, heavy smell of hides and people. The smoke of the hearth was so much part of their lives that it got into their teeth.

## Part Seven — Around Half Past Nine: The Man Who Could Not Hunt

The small question for this hour is a quiet one. What happened to Orrin? How did a strong man of his people, a hunter, come to have an arm that hangs thin and useless at his side, a leg that drags a little when he walks, and ears that hear the world only faintly, as if through deep water?

Let's go and sit with him, and find out.

Imagine that you get up from where you've been sitting, and step carefully around the edge of the hearth, past Pim, who is asleep now on his hide with his mouth open, and lower yourself down beside Orrin, on his left side, his good side, where he can see you. He turns his head, slowly, and looks at you. His face is broad and lined, and there's an old scar running from his left temple down across his cheekbone, pale against his weathered skin, where the bone beneath it seems somehow flatter than on the other side. His left eye looks at you clearly. His right arm lies across his lap, and when you look at it properly, in the firelight, you can see how thin it is, thinner than a child's, the muscle long gone, the hand curled loosely and still.

He doesn't speak to you. But he's not unfriendly. He reaches out with his good hand and pushes a piece of pine a little further into the fire, the way Mora does, without looking, and then settles back against his stone.

Here is what happened to him, or at least, the story I've imagined for him. Many winters ago, when Ket was a small boy, Orrin was hunting horses on the high ground above the valley, with two other men who are dead now. Something went wrong. A horse turned, or a slope gave way, or both. He was thrown down among rocks. His right arm was broken in more than one place, badly. His face struck stone. His leg was hurt. And the men with him carried him, somehow, all the long way back to the shelter, and the women of the group kept him alive.

The arm healed, after a fashion, but it healed wrong, and the nerves were damaged, and over the years it withered. The leg healed with a limp. And in the years since, slowly, his hearing has faded, until now he hears loud sounds dimly and soft sounds not at all. He can still work. He can still make tools, with his one good hand and his knees. He can still mind the fire. But he hasn't hunted since that day, and he never will again.

That's the answer to our small question, as I've imagined it for him. But I didn't invent the injuries. Every one of them is real. They're taken from a real man.

In 1957, in Shanidar Cave, high in the Zagros Mountains of what is now Iraqi Kurdistan, the American archaeologist Ralph Solecki and his team uncovered the skeleton of a Neanderthal man. Solecki had been excavating at Shanidar since 1951, and over the course of his digs there, until 1960, he would find the remains of ten Neanderthals, men, women and children. This one, the first adult he found, became known as Shanidar 1. And when his bones were studied, above all by the anthropologist Erik Trinkaus, who published a great monograph called The Shanidar Neandertals in 1983, they told a story that has moved people ever since.

Shanidar 1 had suffered a heavy blow to the side of his face, which damaged the bone around his left eye, and which some researchers think may have affected his sight on that side. His right arm had been broken in several places, and had healed, but it was withered, and he had lost his lower arm and hand, possibly from the injury itself, or possibly, some have suggested, from a kind of amputation. His right leg had been injured too, and he would have walked with a limp. He had degenerative disease in his joints. And he had lived with all of this for years. He was somewhere in his forties when he died, which, for a Neanderthal, was old.

Then, in 2017, Erik Trinkaus and his colleague Sébastien Villotte published a new study of Shanidar 1 in the journal PLOS ONE. They'd looked closely at his ear canals, and found that they were narrowed by bony growths, of a kind that can develop in people who spend a lot of time in cold water or cold wind. And they concluded that he would have been effectively deaf in his right ear, and at least partly deaf in his left. A man who could barely hear, in a world of hunters, where hearing the snap of a twig or the growl of a predator could mean the difference between life and death. As Trinkaus put it in the press coverage of the study, the only way such a man could have lived to such an age was with considerable help from others.

So, like Shanidar 1, Orrin has a damaged face, a withered right arm, a limping leg, and ears that barely hear. And like Shanidar 1, he's alive, years after the injuries that could easily have killed him. Which brings us to the question of why.

Let's set this beside modern life for a moment. If you broke your arm badly today, you'd be taken to a hospital, perhaps in an ambulance. You'd be given painkillers, an X-ray, a cast or an operation, and physiotherapy afterwards. If you lost your hearing, there'd be hearing aids, or surgery, or sign language, or subtitles on your television. There'd be laws protecting your right to work, and benefits if you couldn't. Most of us live in societies that have built, over generations, whole systems for caring for people who are hurt, or ill, or disabled. For Orrin, there's none of that. There's no hospital. There's no system. There's only the people around this fire.

And here's another case, closer to home for our shelter. At a site called Cova Negra, near Xàtiva in the region of Valencia, a little way down the Mediterranean coast from here, archaeologists had found a small fragment of a child's skull, the part of the temple bone that contains the inner ear. In 2024, a team led by Mercedes Conde-Valverde published a study of that fragment in the journal Science Advances. Using detailed scans, they found that the inner ear had developed in an unusual way, a pattern associated today with Down syndrome, which would have caused severe hearing loss and disabling dizziness. The child, whom the team nicknamed Tina, though nobody knows whether the child was a girl or a boy, had lived to at least six years old. Without a great deal of care, from more than one person, the researchers argued, such a child could not have survived that long. The fragment is very old, somewhere between about one hundred and forty-six thousand and two hundred and seventy-three thousand years. And it suggests something remarkable. That long before our night, Neanderthals were caring not only for injured adults, who had once been useful and might be again, but for a child who could never repay them.

And here is the point where scientists have disagreed, and it is about the meaning of all of this.

When the Shanidar skeletons were first studied, many people saw in them clear evidence of compassion, of Neanderthals who loved and looked after each other. But some researchers have long been more cautious. A healed bone, they've argued, tells you that someone survived, not why. Perhaps the injured man could feed himself more than we think. Perhaps people helped him only because he was useful, because he knew things, or because he was a relative they were obliged to help, the way you might help a neighbour for the sake of what he could do for you later. Perhaps it was calculation, not kindness. And some anthropologists have pointed out that injuries like these show up among ancient modern humans about as often as among Neanderthals, so perhaps there's nothing special to explain.

In 2018, the archaeologist Penny Spikins of the University of York, with her colleagues Andy Needham, Lorna Tilley and Gail Hitchens, published a paper in the journal World Archaeology with the title "Calculated or caring? Neanderthal healthcare in social context." They gathered together the cases of injury and illness in Neanderthal skeletons, and looked at them alongside what we know about how care works in living hunting and gathering peoples. And they argued that Neanderthal healthcare was widespread, knowledgeable and well organised, and part of a society with strong social bonds. It wasn't, in their view, a cold calculation of who might be useful later. They suggested that it was time to accept Neanderthal healthcare as a compassionate and knowledgeable response to injury and illness. Other researchers still argue about how much we can read from bones into minds. The question of motive is a hard one. It's hard enough to answer about the people we know.

So here, at last, is the second of our three mysteries. I asked you at the start of the night: why would a man who cannot hunt, who can barely hear, and who has not been able to use one of his arms for years, be given the warmest place at the hearth tonight, and some of the best of the food?

There are three answers, and I think they're all true at once.

The first answer is the simplest. Because he is family. Orrin is Mora's brother, Ket's uncle, Tavi's and Pim's great-uncle. He carried Ket on his shoulders when Ket was small. He taught Dun to strike a flake from a core. These people have known him all their lives. Whatever else was true of the Neanderthals, the evidence of Shanidar and Cova Negra and many other sites suggests that they did not abandon their own.

The second answer is that he still matters to the group, in ways that a skeleton can't show. Orrin knows this valley better than anyone alive. He knows where the deer cross the river in which season, and where the best stone can be found, and which slopes the horses favour after snow. He knows how to make every kind of tool his people make. And, with his one good hand and a lifetime of practice, he knows fire. Tonight, as we'll see, he will be the one who sits up with the hearth in the dark, when the others are sleeping, because he sleeps lightly, and because the fire is safest with him.

And the third answer is the one that Spikins and her colleagues argued for. Because they care about him. Because, in a group of nine people in a cold shelter in an ice age, care is not a luxury. It's how everyone survives. Each of them, at some point in their lives, will be the one who's hurt, or sick, or very young, or very old. Each of them will need the warmest place by the fire. And the group that keeps its injured, and its old, and its small, by the fire, is the group that's still there in the spring.

So that's our second mystery solved. Orrin has the warmest place, and some of the best of the food, because he's family, because he's still precious to the group in what he knows and what he does, and, I think, because the people around this fire love him.

Hesk has come over now, and sat down on Orrin's other side, the side of the withered arm, and Orrin turns to him with an effort. The boy holds out his hand. In it is a lump of grey stone, a core, and a cobble to strike it with. He wants to be shown. He's been trying all evening to strike a flake as clean as Dun's, and the flakes keep coming out thick and stubby and wrong.

Orrin takes the core in his good hand and turns it over, slowly, looking at it with his good eye. Then he props it against his knee, wedged so it won't move, and takes the cobble, and shows the boy, without a sound, where to strike. Not here. Here. At this angle. Like this. He makes the motion, slowly, without striking. Then he gives the stone and cobble back.

Hesk tries. A thick, ugly flake. Orrin shakes his head, just slightly, and points again. Hesk tries again. Better. Orrin points again, and lifts his chin, a little, as if to say, once more. And the boy strikes, and a long, thin, beautiful flake springs away from the core and lands on the hide in front of them, and Hesk picks it up and holds it to the firelight, and his face is pure astonishment.

Orrin, beside him, makes a low sound in his chest that might be a laugh.

This, too, is a kind of care. And a kind of survival. Every skill these people have is held in someone's hands, and every skill, sooner or later, has to be handed on. Orrin can't hunt any more. But he can make sure that the boy beside him will hunt well. That knowledge, passed from one pair of hands to another, by firelight, on a winter night, is as much a part of how the Neanderthals survived the ice age as any spear.

## Part Eight — Around Half Past Ten: Voices in the Dark

Here is the small question for this hour of the night. All evening, we've watched these people work together, hunt together, eat together, care for one another. But we haven't heard them say a single word. So how do they talk to each other? Can they talk at all?

Listen.

The shelter, at this hour, is full of sound, but it's soft sound. The fire ticks and whispers. The river murmurs below. Pim, asleep on his hide, breathes in small, quick puffs. And around the hearth, the adults are making sounds too. Not loudly. Sela is saying something to Mora, low, with a little lift at the end, and Mora answers with a short sound and a movement of her hand towards the back of the shelter, and Sela nods and gets up and goes there. Ket, by the fire, says something to Dun, three or four sounds, with a pause in the middle, and Dun laughs, a deep, real laugh, and answers. Tavi asks something of her mother, and her mother answers without looking up.

What are they saying? I don't know. Nobody does. And I want to be honest with you about that, because it's one of the deepest mysteries about these people. That's why, all night, I haven't put a single word into their mouths. Not because I'm sure they had no words. But because, if they did, those words are gone, completely, leaving no trace, and anything I made up would be a lie.

Imagine that you're sitting by the fire now, with your hide around your shoulders, and you close your eyes and simply listen to them. The sounds are human sounds. You'd know that at once. There's rise and fall in them, a rhythm, pauses, the soft sounds of agreement, the sharper sound of a question, a burst of laughter. It's a little like sitting in a café in a foreign country, listening to the people at the next table. You can't understand a word. But you know, from the tone and the timing and the faces, that you're listening to people talking. Whether that's what you'd really hear, in this shelter, fifty-five thousand years ago, is exactly what scientists have argued about for more than half a century.

The argument began, in a sense, with the old man of La Chapelle-aux-Saints, the same skeleton that Marcellin Boule misread in 1911. In 1971, the linguist Philip Lieberman and the anatomist Edmund Crelin published a reconstruction of the old man's vocal tract, the throat and mouth and tongue, based on the shape of his skull. They concluded that a Neanderthal would have been unable to make the full range of vowel sounds that we make, particularly the vowels in words like "see", "too" and "father". Neanderthal speech, if there was any, would have been slower, and more limited, and harder to tell apart. It was an influential idea, and for a while it was widely believed.

Then, in 1983, in Kebara Cave, on Mount Carmel in what is now Israel, archaeologists found the skeleton of a Neanderthal man known as Kebara 2. And with him, preserved by great good fortune, was a tiny bone that almost never survives, a bone called the hyoid. It's a small, horseshoe-shaped bone that sits in the throat, at the root of the tongue, and it anchors the muscles of the tongue and the larynx. In 1989, a team led by Baruch Arensburg published it in the journal Nature. And the Kebara hyoid was almost indistinguishable from a modern human hyoid. The anchor for the tongue was the same shape as ours.

That doesn't prove that the Kebara man could speak. A bone is not a voice. But it suggested that the basic equipment for speech may have been there.

Then the geneticists joined in. There's a gene called FOXP2, which, when it goes wrong in modern people, causes serious difficulties with speech and language. Our species carries a particular version of it. And in 2007, a team including Johannes Krause and Svante Pääbo, at the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology in Leipzig, published a study in the journal Current Biology showing that the Neanderthals carried the same version of FOXP2 as we do. Again, it isn't proof of language. A gene is not a sentence. But it was another piece pointing in the same direction.

And in 2021, a team led by Mercedes Conde-Valverde, the same scientist who would later study the child from Cova Negra, published a study in the journal Nature Ecology and Evolution of the ear. Not the throat, this time, but the hearing. Using detailed scans of fossil skulls, they reconstructed the tiny structures of the Neanderthal outer and middle ear, and calculated what range of sounds those ears would have been best tuned to hear. And they found that Neanderthal ears were tuned to a range very close to our own, the range in which much of human speech is carried, and quite different from the ears of the older fossils from Atapuerca, in northern Spain, that were their ancestors. They argued that Neanderthals had the hearing to support a communication system as complex and efficient as human speech.

So here is where the disagreement stands now. Some scientists are fairly confident that Neanderthals had language much like ours, with words and grammar, and that all these pieces, the hyoid, the gene, the ear, the complex skills that had to be taught, point that way. Others are more cautious. They point out that none of these clues is direct evidence, that the shape of the throat may still have limited the range of sounds, and that language leaves no fossils. And some have proposed something in between. In 2005, the archaeologist Steven Mithen published a book called The Singing Neanderthals, in which he suggested that the Neanderthals may have communicated with a rich system of sound that was something like music and something like language at once, full of rhythm and pitch and emotion and gesture, but not broken into words quite as ours is.

That's the answer to our small question, then, as honestly as I can give it. They talk to each other, of course they do. They couldn't hunt as they do, or teach as Orrin was teaching Hesk, or plan the next day's journey, without some way of sharing what's in their minds. But whether they did it with words like ours, or with something richer or simpler or different, we do not know. And we may never know.

Let's set this beside modern life for a moment. Right now, there may be a phone within reach of you. With it, you could speak to someone on the other side of the world, or send a message that arrives in a second, or read the words of someone who died hundreds of years ago. You could look up a word in any one of thousands of languages. Our world is made of words, written and spoken and broadcast, everywhere, all the time. The people in this shelter have never seen a written mark that meant a sound. Whatever they know, they carry in their heads and their hands, and they share it face to face, by firelight, in a small circle. Everything they know about the world, about fire and stone and hides and deer, has come to them that way, from the mouth and hands of someone they could see.

Sela has come back from the back of the shelter with an armful of dry grass and bracken, and she's spreading it on the ground along the rear wall, under the overhang, in a long, rough line. Tavi gets up to help her without being asked. Bedding. We'll come back to that very soon.

And there's another thing that the scientists have learned, which bears on Sela herself. In 2011, a team led by the Spanish geneticist Carles Lalueza-Fox published a study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the remains of twelve Neanderthals from the cave of El Sidrón, in Asturias, the same cave where the mushroom and pine nut eaters were found. They had all died at around the same time, and they seem to have been members of a single group: adults, teenagers, children, and an infant. When the team studied their mitochondrial DNA, which is passed down from mother to child, they found that the adult men all shared the same maternal lineage, while the adult women each had a different one. The simplest explanation is that the men of the group had stayed with the group they were born into, while the women had come from other groups, as Sela came to this one. It's only one group, at one site, and it may not have been true everywhere. But it's a remarkable glimpse of family life, from DNA alone.

So when Sela first came to this valley, as a girl, from the band over the northern hills, did she speak in exactly the same way as the people here? Or did she have to learn a slightly different way of talking, as people who marry into another family, or another town, sometimes still do today? We can't know. But I like to imagine that, even after all these years, there's something a little different in the way she says some things, a lilt from over the hills, that makes Ket smile.

Mora is humming now. It's very soft, so soft you might not notice it under the sound of the fire. A low, wordless sound, rising a little and falling back, rising and falling, over and over. She's not humming to anyone in particular. She's humming the way people hum when their hands are busy and their minds are quiet. Sela, spreading the bracken, picks it up after a moment, and hums too, a little higher. And Lu, in Sela's hide, makes a small sound of her own, and goes quiet again, listening.

Orrin can't hear it. Or almost can't. He's sitting with his eyes half closed, his back against the stone. But he can feel it, perhaps, a faint vibration in the air, or in the stone behind him. Or he can see Mora's face, and know what she's doing. He's known that humming all his life. His mother hummed it. His grandmother. He doesn't need to hear it to know it.

Whatever the Neanderthals did or didn't have in the way of words, I find it very hard to imagine that they didn't have this. A low voice by a fire, at the end of a long day, in the dark, humming. It's one of the oldest sounds there is.

There's something else here that you'd recognise, if you've ever been near a small baby. Watch Sela now, when Lu stirs and fusses inside the hide. Sela bends her head down close, and the sounds she makes change. They go higher. They go slower. They rise and fall in long, exaggerated swoops, and she repeats them, over and over, with her face very close to her daughter's, her eyes wide. Psychologists who study the way adults talk to babies have found this same pattern in culture after culture around the world, in languages that have nothing else in common. Higher pitch, slower pace, bigger swoops of melody, lots of repetition. Some researchers call it infant-directed speech, and some, more simply, call it motherese, though fathers and grandparents and older children do it too. Whether Neanderthal mothers did exactly this, nobody can say. But Mithen, in his book, suggested that this kind of musical, emotional sound, shared between a parent and a baby, may be one of the very oldest roots of human communication. ## Part Nine — Around Half Past Eleven: Talons, Lines and Flowers

The small question for this part of the night is a gentle one, but it goes very deep. Did these people ever make anything that wasn't useful? Anything just for beauty, or for meaning? A mark, an ornament, a sign?

Tavi is still awake. She's sitting at the edge of the hearth, on the side away from the heat, where the old ashes from earlier fires have spread out in a soft, grey, powdery fan across the floor. And she has a stick in her hand, a thin, charred twig, and she's drawing with it in the ash. Lines. One, and then another beside it, and then another crossing them. She looks at them for a while. Then she smooths them away with the flat of her hand, and begins again.

Imagine that you're sitting beside her, watching. The ash is warm on the side nearest the fire and cool on the far side. When she draws, the stick leaves a dark furrow in the grey, and a little puff of fine ash rises and settles. She's not drawing anything you could name. Not a deer, not a person, not the sun. Just lines. Crossing lines. And yet she does it with such care, such attention, that you can't help feeling it means something to her. Or perhaps she simply likes how it looks.

I've invented Tavi, of course, and her lines in the ash. Lines drawn in ash don't survive a single night, let alone fifty-five thousand years. But lines drawn in stone can.

In 2014, a team led by Joaquín Rodríguez-Vidal, working with the Gibraltar Museum's Clive Finlayson and others, published a paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences about a discovery at Gorham's Cave, a great sea cave at the foot of the Rock of Gibraltar, at the very southern tip of the Iberian peninsula, more than eight hundred kilometres from here. At the back of the cave, on a ledge of bedrock, they had found a set of deeply incised lines, cut into the rock with a stone tool: a crosshatched pattern, eight lines in all, not unlike the symbol we'd call a hashtag. The engraving was covered by an undisturbed layer of sediment that contained Neanderthal stone tools and was older than about thirty-nine thousand years. So the lines beneath it were older still. The team made experimental engravings of their own, on the same kind of rock, and found that cutting lines that deep took many repeated strokes, a deliberate, sustained effort. Not an accident. Not the by-product of cutting meat. Someone had meant to make those lines.

That's one clue. Here's another, and it's one of my favourites.

At the end of the nineteenth century, between 1899 and 1905, the Croatian palaeontologist Dragutin Gorjanović-Kramberger excavated a rock shelter at Krapina, north of Zagreb, and found the remains of dozens of Neanderthals, along with thousands of animal bones. Among the bones, almost unnoticed for more than a century, were eight talons from white-tailed eagles, the great sea eagles of Europe, with wingspans of more than two metres. In 2015, a team including Davorka Radovčić and David Frayer published a study of them in the journal PLOS ONE. They found cut marks on the talons, and smooth, polished facets, and notches, which they interpreted as the marks of the talons having been strung together, rubbing against one another and against a cord, as part of a necklace or bracelet. The Krapina talons are about one hundred and thirty thousand years old. That's long before modern humans arrived in Europe. If the team is right, then Neanderthals were wearing jewellery, made from the claws of one of the most magnificent birds in their world, tens of thousands of years before anyone else in Europe.

And there's colour, too. At Cueva de los Aviones, a sea cave near Cartagena on the south-eastern coast of Spain, archaeologists found seashells with holes in them, some stained with red and yellow pigments, alongside lumps of the pigments themselves. In 2018, in a second paper that same year, a team led by Dirk Hoffmann and João Zilhão reported in the journal Science Advances that the layers containing those shells were about one hundred and fifteen thousand years old. Some of the shells may have been strung, or used as little containers for pigment. Whatever they were for, someone had gathered them, and coloured them, and kept them.

Neanderthals also gathered black. At Pech-de-l'Azé I, in the Dordogne, the same site where the bone smoothers were found, there are hundreds of small blocks of black manganese oxide, many of them rubbed and scraped, as if to make powder. For a long time, people assumed it was for decorating bodies or hides, a kind of black paint. Then, in 2016, a team led by Peter Heyes published a study in the journal Scientific Reports with a different suggestion. They'd found that the particular manganese mineral the Neanderthals chose, ground to a powder and mixed with wood shavings, makes tinder catch fire at a much lower temperature. They proposed that the black powder may have been, at least in part, a fire-lighting aid. So even the colours of the Neanderthals bring us back, in the end, to the hearth. Black for burning, perhaps. Red for something else. We still don't fully know.

Then there are the caves of Spain itself. In 2018, a team led by Dirk Hoffmann, with Chris Standish, Alistair Pike and João Zilhão among others, published a study in the journal Science about paintings in three Spanish caves: La Pasiega in Cantabria, Maltravieso in Extremadura, and Ardales in Andalusia. The paintings were simple, a red ladder-like shape in one cave, a stencilled hand in another, red-painted stone draperies in the third. The team dated thin crusts of calcite that had formed over the paint, using the slow decay of uranium into thorium, and they reported that some of the crusts were more than sixty-four thousand years old. That would mean the paint beneath them was older still, and that it was made at a time when, as far as we know, the only people in Europe were Neanderthals.

And here is where the scientists disagree, sharply.

Some researchers welcomed the dates as proof of what they'd long suspected, that Neanderthals made art. But others were deeply sceptical. In the same year, 2018, a team including Maxime Aubert, Adam Brumm and Jillian Huntley published a response in the Journal of Human Evolution, raising questions about the dating method and about whether all the marks were really paintings made by people. In 2020, another team, led by Randall White, published a longer critique, arguing that the uranium-thorium method can give dates that are too old if water has washed uranium out of the calcite over time, and questioning some of the samples. Hoffmann and his colleagues replied, defending their results. The argument has not been settled. It's one of the most contested questions in the study of the Neanderthals: not whether they could do what Tavi is doing, scratching lines in ash, but whether, somewhere in the dark of a Spanish cave, they ever reached up and laid their hand against the rock, and blew red pigment around it, and left their mark.

So that's the answer to our small question, as honestly as I can give it. Yes, it seems that Neanderthals made things that weren't only useful. Lines deliberately cut into rock. Eagle talons, cut and polished, perhaps worn on a cord. Possibly, though not certainly, paintings on the walls of caves. Each piece of evidence, taken alone, has its critics. Taken together, they've changed the way most scientists think about these people.

Let's set this beside your own home for a moment. Look around the room you're lying in. On the walls, perhaps, there are pictures. On a shelf, maybe, there are photographs of the people you love, or small things you've kept because they remind you of someone, or some place, or some time. Perhaps you're wearing a ring, or a necklace, that means something to you beyond its metal. None of these things will keep you warm, or feed you, or help you hunt. And yet you'd be very sad to lose them. If the scientists are right about the talons of Krapina, then the Neanderthals felt something like that too.

There's one more mystery I want to tell you about, very gently, because it's about the people they lost.

In 1960, in Shanidar Cave, the same cave where Shanidar 1 was found, Ralph Solecki uncovered the skeleton of another Neanderthal, a man known as Shanidar 4. Solecki collected soil samples from around the skeleton, and sent them to the French palynologist Arlette Leroi-Gourhan, who studied ancient pollen. And in the soil, she found pollen, in clusters, from several kinds of flowering plants. She and Solecki suggested that flowers might have been laid in the grave, perhaps as a kind of offering. In 1971, Solecki published a book about his excavations with the title Shanidar: The First Flower People. The idea caught the world's imagination. The Neanderthals, it seemed, had buried their dead with flowers.

But other scientists were not so sure. In 1999, the anthropologist Jeffrey Sommer published a short paper in the Cambridge Archaeological Journal suggesting that the pollen might have been brought there by a small, gerbil-like rodent called the Persian jird, which lives in the region, digs burrows, and stores seeds and flower heads underground. And in 2023, a team led by the palaeoecologist Chris Hunt published a study in the Journal of Archaeological Science arguing that the clumps of pollen fitted much better with the work of ground-nesting bees, which carry pollen into their burrows. Hunt found the burrows of such bees in the sediments at Shanidar, ancient and modern. The plants in the pollen, he pointed out, didn't even all flower at the same time of year.

Meanwhile, in 2018, a new team at Shanidar, led by Graeme Barker of the University of Cambridge, found the upper body of another Neanderthal, very close to where Shanidar 4 had lain. They called this one Shanidar Z. In 2020, Emma Pomeroy and her colleagues described the find in the journal Antiquity. The body seemed to have been laid deliberately in a hollow, part of a cluster of individuals buried close together. Pomeroy said that she had come to the project sceptical about the idea of deliberate burial at Shanidar, and had moved towards thinking it much more plausible. Whether there were ever flowers, though, remains unknown. As the archaeologist and writer Rebecca Wragg Sykes has noted, the plant material near Shanidar Z still leaves open the possibility of some kind of intentional inclusion of plants with the dead.

So the flowers may have been bees. But the care, the hollow in the ground, the bodies laid together, may have been real. And that, perhaps, is the most moving part of the story, whether or not anyone laid a single flower.

Tavi has stopped drawing. She's looking at her lines in the ash, and her eyelids are heavy. Her mother, behind her, reaches out and touches her shoulder, and Tavi turns, and smooths away the lines one last time with the flat of her hand, and gets up, and goes to her mother.

The bedding is ready now. Along the back of the shelter, under the deepest part of the overhang, where the rock leans out furthest above, Sela and Tavi have spread a long, rough strip of dry grass and bracken, and laid hides over it. And Mora is doing something new. She's taken a burning stick from the main hearth, a long piece of pine with a good glowing end, and she's carrying it, carefully, shielding it with her body, to the back of the shelter. ## Part Ten — Around Half Past Twelve: The Row of Little Fires

Here is the small question for this hour of the night. Now that it's time to lie down, what exactly are these people going to sleep on? And how do you make a bed on the floor of a rock shelter, in the middle of an ice age, that's warm enough to sleep in?

It's past midnight now. The main hearth, Mora's hearth, is burning low in the middle of the shelter. And along the back wall, under the deepest part of the overhang, the row of little fires that Mora lit from it is flickering, five of them now, small and bright, each one about a long stride from the next. Between them and the rock, there's the long, rough strip of bedding that Sela and Tavi laid out earlier.

Imagine you're kneeling down at the end of that strip, and you press your hand into it. Underneath, there's a thick layer of dry grass and bracken, gathered from the slopes and the riverbank, springy and crackling, a hand's width deep or more. It smells of summer, faintly, and of dust. On top of it, there are hides, laid fur side up, overlapping a little, so that the grass doesn't poke through. And at the very back, against the rock, the bedding is a little thicker, piled up like a long, low pillow.

That's the answer to the first half of our small question. They sleep on plants and hides. Grass and bracken below, to lift them off the cold ground and trap a layer of air, like the stuffing of a mattress. Hides on top, for softness and warmth. And more hides over them, as blankets.

We have some evidence for this kind of thing from Neanderthal sites, though it's hard to find, because grass and bracken rot away. At Esquilleu Cave, in the mountains of Cantabria in northern Spain, a team including Dan Cabanes and Carolina Mallol studied the microscopic remains of plants in the floor deposits, tiny glassy particles called phytoliths, which form inside the cells of grasses and survive long after the plants themselves have gone. In 2010, they reported layers rich in grass phytoliths, which they interpreted as the remains of plant material brought into the cave, possibly as bedding. Here at Abric Romaní, the travertine has preserved the shapes of many pieces of wood. A bed of grass is a much harder thing to catch. But it would be very strange if these people, who knew so much about keeping warm, slept on the bare ground.

Now the second half of our question. How do you make that bed warm enough?

Lie down on it, now, with me, and you'll feel the answer straight away. Lie on your side, facing out, towards the open front of the shelter and the valley. Behind you, close enough to touch, is the rock wall, still faintly warm from the day's sun and from hours of firelight. In front of you, about an arm's length beyond your knees, is one of the little fires, burning low and steady, and you can feel its warmth on your face and chest and hands. Above you, the overhang leans out far enough that no rain or snow, or even the frost of the open sky, can reach you. And the hide pulled over you holds the warmth against your body. You're lying, in other words, in a narrow, sheltered, warm channel, between a fire and a wall.

And so we come, at last, to the doorstep of our third mystery. But before we cross it, I'd like to tell you what happened to the people who slept in places like this. Because it's part of the story, and because it's not as sad as you might think.

Mora and her family, on this night, are living about fifty-five thousand years ago. Neanderthals, by then, had been living in Europe for hundreds of thousands of years. And they would go on living here for another fifteen thousand years or so, which is roughly as long as the time between us and the end of the last ice age. In 2014, a team led by Tom Higham, then at the University of Oxford, published a large study in the journal Nature. They had re-dated material from about forty sites across Europe, from Spain to Russia, using improved methods of radiocarbon dating. And they concluded that the Neanderthals had disappeared, across Europe as a whole, by around forty thousand years ago, after a long period, perhaps several thousand years, when they and modern humans lived in the same continent, in a kind of patchwork, side by side.

Why did they disappear? Here, the scientists disagree, and they've been disagreeing for a very long time. Some point to the climate, which, in the last part of the Neanderthals' time, swung wildly between colder and warmer phases, sometimes over just a few decades, making it very hard for small groups to keep up. Some point to competition with the incoming modern humans, who may have had larger social networks, or more varied tools, or simply more people. Some point to disease. And some, like the team of Krist Vaesen, who published a study in PLOS ONE in 2019, have argued that the Neanderthals may have been so few, and so thinly spread, in small, scattered groups like the one in this shelter, that ordinary bad luck, a few failed births here, a few hard winters there, could have been enough, over thousands of years, to tip them into decline, without any single great catastrophe. Most scientists now suspect that it was some combination of all of these things, and that it may have happened differently in different places.

But here's the part that isn't sad.

In 2010, a team led by Richard Green, working in the laboratory of the Swedish geneticist Svante Pääbo at the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology in Leipzig, published, in the journal Science, the first draft sequence of the Neanderthal genome, pieced together from tiny fragments of DNA extracted from bones found in a cave in Croatia. And when they compared it with the DNA of living people, they found something that surprised almost everyone. People today whose ancestors came from outside Africa carry a small but real share of Neanderthal DNA, in every cell of their bodies. Later estimates have put it at roughly one to two per cent of the genome. The two kinds of people, Neanderthals and modern humans, had met, and had children together, and those children had children, down through all the generations to now. In 2022, Svante Pääbo was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for his work on the genomes of extinct humans.

The meetings between the two kinds of people have since been traced in remarkable detail. In 2015, a team led by Qiaomei Fu, working with Pääbo and others, published a study in the journal Nature of a jawbone found in a cave in Romania called Peștera cu Oase, the Cave with Bones. It belonged to a modern human man who lived around forty thousand years ago. Between six and nine per cent of his genome came from Neanderthals, far more than in anyone alive today, and the pattern of it suggested that he'd had a Neanderthal ancestor only four to six generations back. A great-great-grandparent, or thereabouts. And in 2018, a team led by Viviane Slon published, also in Nature, the genome of a small fragment of bone from Denisova Cave, in the Altai Mountains of Siberia. It came from a girl, about thirteen years old, who lived some ninety thousand years ago, and whose mother was a Neanderthal and whose father belonged to another ancient group of people, called the Denisovans. A daughter of two worlds. People, it seems, have always found one another.

And here, too, there's a disagreement, and it's one that touches the land we're sitting in tonight. For many years, the Portuguese archaeologist João Zilhão and others argued that the Neanderthals of southern Iberia, south of the great river Ebro, which reaches the sea about a hundred miles to the south-west of this shelter, survived for several thousand years longer than those in the rest of Europe, perhaps until around thirty-seven thousand years ago, in a last refuge, while modern humans spread through the north. Then, in 2013, a team led by Rachel Wood published a study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences re-dating bones from a number of southern Spanish sites with more rigorous methods of cleaning the samples, and found that many of them were older than had been thought, closer to fifty thousand years. That made the late survival harder to support. Zilhão and his colleagues have continued to argue that some late dates do stand. It's still discussed. This shelter lies north of the Ebro, so its people would have been on the near side of that frontier, if it ever existed. But whichever way it falls, the Iberian peninsula was one of the last homes of the Neanderthals, long after our night, and long before ours.

So if your family's roots lie in Europe, or Asia, or the Americas, or many other parts of the world, then somewhere far back along the long, branching line of your ancestors, there's someone very like Mora. Or like Sela, or Ket, or little Pim. Not this group, exactly. I made them up. But people like them. The Neanderthals didn't simply vanish. In a small but real way, they're still here. Some of them are lying in bed, right now, listening to this.

Let's set this beside your own home for a moment. In your house, perhaps, there's a radiator in each bedroom, or a vent from the furnace, or an electric heater in the corner, or an electric blanket under your sheet, set to just the right warmth, with a dial. Each sleeper, in each room, has their own little source of heat, placed close to where they sleep. That's not so very different, in its way, from the row of little fires along the back of this shelter, though what those fires are really for is a mystery we'll come to in a little while.

Here is a small, gentle question for this hour of the night. Now that almost everyone is lying down, who will be the first to fall asleep, and who will be the last? And who will keep the fire?

The shelter is very quiet now. The river murmurs below in the dark, the same low sound it has made all night. Because now, at last, we come to the third of our three mysteries. I asked you at the very start of the night: when it is time to sleep, why will this whole group lie down in a single line along the back of the shelter, each with a tiny fire of their own, each fire about one long stride from the next?

Here's how we know that they did.

In 2010, a team of twelve researchers, led by Josep Vallverdú and including Eudald Carbonell and many of the Abric Romaní team, published a paper in the journal Current Anthropology with the title "Sleeping Activity Area within the Site Structure of Archaic Human Groups: Evidence from Abric Romaní Level N Combustion Activity Areas." Level N. Our level. Our night. In the inner part of the living surface of level N, the part nearest the back of the shelter, they had uncovered a remarkably well preserved set of five small combustion areas, five little hearths, lying in a line, about one metre apart from one another. And they compared that pattern with what is known about the camps of modern hunting and gathering peoples, where people often sleep in a row near small fires, each fire kept going through the night by the people sleeping beside it. And they wrote, in the summary of their paper, that this set of combustion areas "suggests analogy with sleeping-and-resting activity areas of modern foragers." A bedroom, in other words, fifty-five thousand years old. It's one of very few such sleeping areas ever identified for archaic humans anywhere. And the team, and others after them, have used it to estimate the size of the group that slept here: something like eight to twelve people. Which is why, tonight, I gave our group nine.

So why sleep in a line, along the back wall, each with a small fire, about a stride apart? There are four answers, I think, and they all work together.

The first is shelter. The back of the shelter, under the deepest part of the overhang, is the driest place, the place furthest from wind and frost and the open sky. The rock behind the sleepers blocks the cold air coming from behind, and holds a little warmth.

The second is warmth, used wisely. A big fire throws a lot of heat, but most of it goes up into the air, or out into the night. A small fire, close to a sleeper, puts its warmth exactly where it's needed, on the front of the body, while the rock and the hides take care of the back. Several small fires, each tended a little through the night, use far less wood than one great blaze, and heat the sleepers far better.

The third is spacing. About a metre apart is close enough that each little fire's warmth overlaps a little with the next, so that there are no cold gaps along the line, and far enough that nobody's sleeping hide is too close to the flames.

And the fourth, I think, is the hearth itself. Every one of those little fires was lit from the big one. Each sleeper, or each pair of sleepers, has their own small piece of Mora's fire to keep them warm through the night, a little ember of the hearth they all share.

So that's our third mystery solved.

There's a place I'd like to tell you about now, very gently, because it's one of the loveliest mysteries in the whole story of the Neanderthals, and it's the kind of mystery that can wait. Far to the north of here, in the south of France, in the valley of the Aveyron, there's a cave called Bruniquel. In 1990, a young caver named Bruno Kowalsczewski dug his way through a blocked passage into it, and found, deep inside, more than three hundred metres from the entrance, in total darkness, something nobody had expected. Broken stalagmites, hundreds of them, about four hundred pieces in all, deliberately snapped off and laid together into two great rings and several smaller piles, on the floor of a chamber.

In 2016, a team led by Jacques Jaubert published a study of the rings in the journal Nature. Using the slow growth of new crystal over the broken stalagmites, they dated the structures to about one hundred and seventy-six thousand years ago. Long, long before modern humans came to Europe. Which means that the only people who could have built them were early Neanderthals. And on many of the stalagmite pieces, the team found traces of fire: reddened, blackened patches, and fragments of burned bone. Someone, a very long time ago, carried fire deep into the dark under the hills, and built rings of stone there, and lit fires among them.

What were the rings for? Nobody knows. That's the gentle disagreement I'll leave with you for this part of the night. Some have imagined a kind of shelter, or a place of refuge. Some have wondered whether it was a place of ritual. Others have simply said, honestly, that we can't tell. The team who studied it were careful not to claim too much. They said only that it showed planning, and cooperation, and a group of people who were at home in the deep dark, with fire for company. I like to think of it that way. Somewhere under a hill in France, there's a ring of stone that Neanderthals built, by firelight, for reasons of their own, and it's still there, in the dark, exactly where they left it.

Hesk is next. He's lying on his back by the fourth little fire, one hand still under the edge of his hide, resting on his new flake, the one Orrin helped him strike. He's thinking about the deer, and about the long, thin flake springing away from the core, and about the look on Orrin's face. He's thinking that tomorrow he'll strike another one, and another. He's thinking that he's not tired at all. And then, between one thought and the next, he isn't thinking anything. His mouth falls a little open. His hand goes slack on the stone.

Hesk is asleep.

Dun goes almost at the same moment, as quietly as he does everything else, with one arm over his face. And Sela, who has been lying very still with her arm around the baby, listening to Lu's breathing, lets her own breathing slow to match it, in and out, in and out, until there's no telling which of them is which.

Dun is asleep. And Sela is asleep.

Here's one small, warm, true thing to carry with you into the last part of the night. In 2007, a team led by Carles Lalueza-Fox, the same geneticist who studied the family of El Sidrón, published a study in the journal Science of a gene called MC1R, recovered from the bones of two Neanderthals, one from El Sidrón, in Spain, and one from Monti Lessini, in Italy. In modern people, some versions of this gene give red hair and pale, freckly skin. The Neanderthals carried a version not known in any living person, and when the team tested its effect in the laboratory, it seemed to work in a similar way to the red-hair variants of today. They suggested that at least some Neanderthals may have had red hair, and pale skin. Not all of them, perhaps. And we can't know about these nine. But I like to imagine that somewhere in this line of sleepers, by the light of a little fire, there's a head of hair that glints a little red.

Ket is lying behind Sela, between her and the rock, with one heavy arm over both of them. He's been awake longer than he meant to, thinking about the hunt, and about the horses he saw on the far slope this afternoon, and whether they'll still be there in two days' time. His thoughts grow slower, and slower. He breathes out, long and deep, the sigh of a man whose work, for today, is done.

Ket is asleep.

And Mora. She's lying at the first little fire, with Pim tucked against her and Tavi beyond him, and her face is turned towards the middle of the shelter, towards her hearth, where Orrin sits. She's watched that hearth all day. She's watched hearths like it all her life. And she watches it now, for a little while longer, from across the shelter, the way some people can't fall asleep until they've checked, one last time, that the door is locked. The hearth is burning. Orrin is beside it. All is well. Her eyes close.

Mora is asleep.

Now only Orrin is left.

He sits by the main hearth for a long time, longer than you'd think, with his hide around his shoulders, looking into the embers, listening, as well as he can, to the silence. He can't hear the breathing of the sleepers. But he can see them, in their line along the back wall, the little fires flickering in front of them. Every so often he gets up, slowly, with his one good hand on his knee, and walks the line, the way Mora did, stooping to push a stick into each small fire, so that none of them burns out. Then he comes back, and sits down again, by the hearth at the centre of it all.

At last, when the night is at its deepest, he does what fire-keepers have done for as long as there have been fires. He draws the embers of the main hearth together into a tight, glowing heap, and lays two thick, heavy pieces of pine across them, and rakes a layer of warm grey ash over the top, so that the fire will burn very slowly, smouldering under its blanket, until the morning. The bright flames sink down. The hearth glows softly through the ash, a deep, dark, breathing red.

Then he goes to the back of the shelter, to the end of the line, to the fifth little fire, and lowers himself down, very carefully, one stiff joint at a time, beside you. He pulls his hide over his shoulder. He looks once more across the shelter, at the soft red glow of the hearth. It's there. It's always there.

Orrin is asleep.

## Part Twelve — Around Seven in the Morning: The Hearth Wakes

The last small question of our night is the simplest of all. On a frozen winter morning, in a shelter like this, what is the very first thing anybody does?

Let's wait and see.

The long dark is ending. It's a little before seven in the morning, and the sun won't lift over the ridges on the far side of the valley until after seven, by my reckoning, perhaps around twenty past. But already, something has changed. The sky beyond the lip of the overhang, which was black and thick with stars all night, has turned a deep, soft blue, and then a paler grey, and the stars are going out, one by one, the faintest first. The shapes of the pines on the far slope are coming back out of the dark. Down below, the river is still talking to itself, as it has all night, but now you can see a pale mist lying along it, hanging just above the water, white and still.

Along the back of the shelter, everyone is still asleep. The five little fires have burned down in the night to small, grey, quiet heaps of ash, with perhaps a single red spark still hiding in the heart of one or two. Pim has kicked his hide half off and is sprawled across his grandmother's arm. Tavi is curled up tight. Lu is a warm, small bundle inside her mother's hide. Ket is snoring, very gently. Dun has his arm over his face. Hesk is lying on his side, with one hand still curled around his flake. And Orrin, at the end of the line, beside you, is lying exactly as he lay down, on his side, facing the middle of the shelter, as if even in sleep he were watching the hearth.

And Mora opens her eyes.

She doesn't need the light to wake her. She's woken at this grey hour almost every morning of her life. She lies still for a moment, listening, feeling. Everyone's breathing. Everyone's here. Then she eases her arm out from under Pim, very slowly, so as not to wake him, and pulls her hide around her shoulders, and gets up, and walks, barefoot and quiet across the cold, ashy floor, to the hearth in the middle of the shelter.

That's the answer to our last small question. The very first thing anybody does, on a winter morning in a place like this, is wake the fire.

Imagine you're kneeling beside her now, at the edge of the hearth, in the grey half-light. The ground under your knees is still faintly warm. In front of you is the low, grey mound of ash that Orrin raked over the embers in the deep of the night, with the charred ends of two thick pine branches poking out of it. It looks dead. It looks like a heap of dust. Mora takes a stick, and very gently, the way you might brush snow off something precious, she draws the ash back from the top of the mound.

And there they are.

At the heart of the ash, still glowing, are the embers. A small heap of them, not much bigger than two cupped hands. Dull red at the edges, and in the centre, where the cold morning air reaches them now for the first time in hours, slowly brightening, orange, and then gold. They've been alive all night. Through the hunters' homecoming and the marrow and the glue, through Orrin's lesson and Tavi's lines in the ash, through the lighting of the little fires and the long, slow falling asleep, they've been here, breathing very slowly under their blanket of ash, waiting for this.

Mora reaches for a handful of dry pine needles and a few thin twigs from the pile beside the hearth. She lays the needles gently on the embers. Then she leans down close, so close that you can feel the warmth on her face and on yours, and she blows. A long, slow, steady breath.

The embers brighten. The needles begin to smoke, a thin white curl of it. She blows again. The smoke thickens and curls up towards the rock overhead. She blows a third time. And then, with a soft sound, almost like a sigh, the needles burst into flame.

It's a small flame at first, yellow and flickering. Mora feeds it the thin twigs, one by one, and they catch, and crackle. Then a few thicker sticks. Then the charred ends of last night's branches, dry and black and ready, which catch almost at once. And within a minute or two, the hearth is burning again, in the middle of the floor of the shelter, just as it was at dusk last night, low and orange and steady, and its light is moving on the underside of the rock, and on the faces of the sleepers along the back wall.

The hearth is awake. And, one by one, so is everyone else.

Pim first, who sits bolt upright on the bedding and looks around as if he's never seen the world before. Then Tavi, who pulls her hide back over herself, and then gives up and gets up. Then Hesk, who reaches at once for his flake, and finds it, and holds it up to the new light. Then Dun, stretching. Then Ket, groaning, and rubbing his face, and looking over at the fire, and at his mother beside it, and nodding, as if to say, there. We've done it again. Another night. Sela sits up with the baby, and Lu blinks at the flames with round, astonished eyes, as if she had never seen a fire before. And last of all, Orrin, slowly, one stiff joint at a time, pushing himself up on his one good arm, and looking across the shelter at the hearth, and at his sister kneeling beside it, and smiling.

Let's set this beside your own home, one last time. Perhaps your heating is set on a timer, so that the house begins to warm itself before you wake. Perhaps your coffee machine starts brewing on its own. You may wake to an alarm, or to the light of a phone, and walk into a kitchen that's already warm. Mora's morning has none of that. But she has something that most of us have lost. She has the knowledge, in her hands and her breath, of how to keep a small fire alive through the longest nights of the winter, and how to wake it, in the morning, with nothing but a handful of needles and her own breath.

Now, before we leave them, I'd like to tell you one more thing, about this hearth, and the little fires, and what happens to them next.

In a day, or a few days, or a few weeks, this group will move on. The deer will move, and the people will follow, up the valley or over the hills, to wherever the next season's food is to be found. They'll leave behind them, on the floor of the shelter, what every group leaves behind. The big hearth in the middle of the floor. The line of five small patches of ash along the back wall. The broken bones of the deer, cracked for their marrow. The flakes of stone that Ket and Dun and Hesk struck and used and dropped. Perhaps a bark tray, or a broken stick.

And the water will keep on doing what it's always done. Seeping out of the cliff. Dripping from the lip of the overhang. Trickling across the floor of the shelter, and laying down, film by film, its thin, patient crust of stone. Over the ashes. Over the bones. Over the hollow in the bedding where nine people lay. In time, other people will come and make their fires a little higher up, on top of the new floor, and leave their ashes too, and the water will cover those as well. Layer upon layer. Twenty-seven levels, the excavators have counted, almost all of them left by Neanderthals.

Here's a gentle disagreement to end with, the last of the night. When archaeologists find a floor like level N, with its hearths and its bones and its stones, how much time does it really represent? Is it one visit, by one group, for a few nights? Or many visits, by many groups, over many years, all pressed together into a single layer, like the pages of a book stuck together? Archaeologists call such a layer a palimpsest, after the old parchments that were scraped and written on again and again. At Abric Romaní, researchers such as Manuel Vaquero and Ignasi Pastó, in their 2001 study of the hearths and the scatters of objects around them, and the many authors of the 2012 volume on level J edited by Eudald Carbonell, called High Resolution Archaeology and Neanderthal Behavior, have worked very hard to tell the difference, by fitting broken stones and bones back together, piece to piece, across the floor, to see which belonged to the same moment. In some places, they've found overlapping hearths that show the same spots being used again and again. In others, they've argued, the travertine sealed the floors so quickly that we're looking at something very close to a single stay. It's a slow, careful, unfinished argument, the kind that can wait. And it's the reason I can tell you, tonight, that a line of five small hearths along the back wall of level N might really be the traces of a few nights, and a few people, and one row of little fires.

Fifty-five thousand years go by.

The ice comes and goes, and comes and goes again. The Neanderthals are gone, and then, in some small part, they're in us. Modern people come into this valley. The forests change. A town grows up beside the river, and in time it builds mills along the water, and makes paper from the springs that once built the travertine. And one day in August, in 1909, a man from that town named Amador Romaní, who makes his living from paper, finds, in the sediment under the old cliff, stone tools and the bones of animals, left there by people far older than any town.

And then, much later, imagine some warm summer afternoon in the decades after 1983, when a member of the excavation team kneels on a wooden board on the floor of the shelter, in the shade of the great overhang, with a trowel and a soft brush in her hand. Very gently, the way you might brush snow off something precious, she draws the pale, fine sediment back from a patch of ground. And there, beneath it, she sees a smudge of black. Then a reddened rim around it. Then a thin, flat lens of grey ash and dark charcoal, lying in the earth exactly where it was left.

A hearth.

She doesn't know who made it. Nobody ever will. She doesn't know their names, or their language, or what they said to each other by its light. But she knows what it is. Someone knelt here. Someone fed this fire with pine, one branch at a time, through a long winter night, and kept the people around it alive until morning.

But that's a long way off. For now, it's morning in the valley, and the hearth is burning.

The sun is just lifting over the far ridges now, and a long, low beam of pale gold light comes slanting across the valley, and under the lip of the overhang, and touches the floor of the shelter, and the faces of the people gathered around the fire. Pim is holding out his hands to the flames. Tavi is leaning against her mother. Hesk is showing his flake to Dun. Ket is crouching by the leftover deer meat, choosing. Orrin has shuffled over to his warm place, with his back against his stone. And Mora is kneeling at the hearth, feeding it, one branch at a time, the way she did at dusk last night, the way she will tonight, and tomorrow night, and through all the nights of this long winter. The fire that was kept, and covered, and waited through the dark.

And the flames rise up, low and orange and steady, and the pine smoke drifts up against the rock and out into the cold, bright air of the valley, and the light of the hearth moves softly on every face in the shelter.

They have survived the night. They knew how. And now, so do you.

Thank you for spending this long ice-age night with me, and with Mora and Orrin, Ket and Sela, Dun and Hesk, Tavi and Pim and little Lu, under the travertine cliff of the Abric Romaní. If this story helped you rest, or helped you drift off somewhere along the way, I'm truly glad. And if you'd like to come back for more nights like this one, you might consider liking this video and subscribing to the channel, gently, whenever you're ready. There's no hurry at all.

Next time, we'll travel a long way forward in time, and a long way north. We'll spend a winter night in Skara Brae, the stone village of Neolithic Orkney, about five thousand years ago, where families slept in stone box-beds around a central hearth, with the wind of the North Atlantic blowing over the roofs, and we'll find out how they kept warm, and fed, and safe, at the very edge of the world. I hope you'll join me there.

Until then, sleep well. Keep warm. And may your own fire, whatever it is, still be glowing in the morning.

## How the sources were checked

- **The group is fictional.** Mora, Orrin, Ket, Sela, Dun, Hesk, Tavi, Pim and Lu are invented. Part One says so, and says that Neanderthal names and language cannot be known. No invented person is presented as appearing in any record. Orrin's injuries are modelled on Shanidar 1, and the narration says so in Part Seven. The group has nine members because the published estimate for level N is about 8 to 12 people.
- **The setting is real.** The Abric Romaní rock shelter is in Capellades (Anoia, Barcelona province, Catalonia), under the travertine cliffs of the Cingles del Capelló. The night is set in archaeological level N, which Vallverdú et al. (2010) date to about 55,000 years ago. The night is a midwinter night. The exact season of the level N occupation is not established, and the script never claims it is.
- **How the facts were checked.** As with the first script, page fetches were blocked, so facts were checked through WebSearch result summaries. **The session's shared web-search budget (200 calls per turn, shared across agents) ran out partway through research.** Items marked **V** below were confirmed by search in this session. Items marked **K** are well-established published findings, cited from the author's existing knowledge of the literature, and were **not** re-checked by search in this session. Every K item is stated cautiously in the narration, but each should be checked against the named paper before publishing.
- **Quotations.** The script quotes no historical speech, and no words are put in the mouths of the Neanderthals or of any real scholar. The only verbatim strings are published titles and one short phrase from an abstract, all listed below. Everything else is paraphrase.
- **Left out or softened for lack of verification.** These were left out: Katherine Dettwyler's 1991 critique of the "compassion" reading (her name could not be confirmed by search, so the sceptical side is described without naming her); the claim that Shanidar 1 was blind in one eye (the script says only that "some researchers think" his sight may have been affected); Kupka's 1909 illustration (not confirmed); a Neanderthal tooth from Abric Romaní (not confirmed); a claim that water was heated at Abric Romaní (not confirmed); a precise hearth count for level J. The level N count of "around nineteen hearths" comes from a secondary blog summary, so the narration hedges it as "researchers have reported".
- **Computed, not quoted.** Sunset (about 4:34 pm local solar time), sunrise (about 7:23 am) and night length (about 14 h 50 min) at Capellades (41.53° N, 1.69° E) were computed with PyEphem for the modern winter solstice (21 December 2025) and converted to local solar time. The narration says these are modern calculations, and that the slow change in the Earth's tilt over 55,000 years alters them only slightly. No moon phase is claimed for the night.
