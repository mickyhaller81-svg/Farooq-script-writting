# What It Was Really Like to Be a Caveman in the Ice Age | History for Sleep

## Part One — Around Half Past Three in the Afternoon: The Last Light on the Steppe

The sun is low already, and it is sinking fast.

Across a wide, pale, rolling land, the winter sky has gone the colour of old ice, with a thin band of copper light along its south-western edge. There are almost no trees here, only a few bare clumps of willow and birch down by the river. The land runs on and on, grass and sedge, stiff with frost, under a dusting of thin snow. The air is so still that every sound carries. Somewhere out on the plain, something large is moving through the snow.

And on a low terrace above the river, where the ground is a little raised and a little sheltered, there are a few strange, rounded shapes. Domed. Humped. Dark against the snow. Come closer, and you see that their walls are not wood, or stone, or earth. They are bones. Enormous jawbones and skulls and curved tusks, stacked and interlocked, the bones of woolly mammoths. Hides are stretched over the top. And from a gap in one of them, a thin grey thread of smoke is rising straight up into the frozen air.

This is a camp on the bank of a small river in what is now central Ukraine, a few hours' drive south of the modern city of Kyiv, near the village that today is called Mezhyrich. The time is roughly eighteen thousand years ago. It is the depth of winter, close to the shortest day of the year. And the night that is beginning now will be one of the longest nights of the year.

When most of us picture people of the Ice Age, we picture a hairy, stooped figure in a scrap of fur, grunting, dragging a club, living in a dark cave.

But that is not who we are going to meet tonight. Not the caveman of the cartoons. The people in this camp are not a different species, half-way between us and the apes. They are people like you. Homo sapiens. Anatomically modern human beings, with the same bodies, the same brains, the same capacity for love and worry and jokes and grief that you have. And they do not live in a cave. They live in houses they built themselves, out of bones, and they wear tailored clothes, sewn with needles.

But I should tell you something honestly, before we go in. If you were taken from your own warm bed tonight and set down on this frozen plain in your nightclothes, you would not know how to live through it. Not because these people were tougher than you, but because they knew a thousand small things that we have forgotten. Without those things, on a night like this one, you would not have survived until morning.

So tonight, we are going to learn those things. Slowly. One at a time. And we have the whole long night to do it.

While we do, I'd like you to carry three small mysteries with you, like three smooth pebbles in a pocket.

The first is this. In the coldest season of the year, when the ground is frozen as hard as iron, why would the people of this camp have gone to the enormous trouble of digging deep holes into the earth, all around their homes?

The second. Why might a tiny sliver of bone, smaller than your little finger, with a hole at one end, have mattered more to this family's survival than any spear?

And the third. On the coldest night of the year, when the fire is all that stands between this family and the frost, why would the grandmother of the house feed it not with wood, but with bones?

I'll answer each of them in its own time. The first not long after supper. The second in the middle of the evening. And the third in the very deep of the night, when the house is nearly asleep. There's no hurry. We have until morning.

Now, before we step inside, I want to be clear with you about something, because it matters on this channel. The family you are about to meet is invented. We do not know, and we can never know, a single name that anyone spoke during the Ice Age. There was no writing. No one anywhere would write anything down for nearly another thirteen thousand years. So the names I am going to give these people, their ages, their habits and their little quarrels and kindnesses, I have made up. But almost everything around them is real. The camp is real. Its bone houses have been dug out of the ground, measured, drawn and photographed. And the evidence I'll tell you about as we go, the excavations, the scientific papers, the radiocarbon dates, the needles and beads and lamps and figurines, all of that is real, and I'll give you the names and the dates, so that you can look them up yourself, if you ever want to, on some quiet afternoon.

Let me say a little more about who these people are, because it matters for everything that follows. If one of their babies were somehow carried forward through time and raised in your own town, that child would grow up to go to school, and learn to read, and argue about films, and nobody would ever know. They live in houses they have built themselves, on open ground, out of the bones of the largest animals in their world. They wear tailored clothes. They trade beautiful things with people who live far away. They make music. They make art. And they know how to do a thousand things that would defeat us completely. How to keep a fire alive with almost no wood. How to sew a coat that the wind can't get through. How to store food in a land where nothing grows for months. The old cartoon caveman, with his club and his cave and the dinosaur that, in truth, had been gone for more than sixty million years, is a joke. These people are not.

Out on the plain, the land runs on and on, grass and sedge and wormwood, yellow-grey under the snow, until it meets the sky. The wind has dropped, and the air is so dry and so still that every sound seems to carry for miles. The bone houses on the terrace are each about the size of a small room, and over each dome, heavy hides are stretched and weighted down against the wind.

So. Let's meet them.

The woman walking slowly up from the river, with a bundle of thin willow sticks under one arm, is the grandmother of the household. I'm going to call her Ennu. She's about fifty years old, which in this world makes her one of the oldest people anyone knows. Her face is lined and brown from wind and smoke, and she moves carefully over the frozen ground, but she is not frail. She has lived through fifty winters like this one. She is the keeper of the fire.

Inside the bone house, sitting cross-legged by the hearth with a hide across her knees, is Siva. She's about twenty-seven, and she is the finest sewer in the camp. Her partner, Ennu's son, is a hunter called Haro. He's about thirty, and at this moment, he is somewhere out on the plain with Siva's younger brother, Mael, who is nineteen, tall and restless and eager to prove himself. They should be home before the dark comes down.

Then there are the children. Tula is eleven, sharp-eyed and quick-fingered, already learning to scrape hides and stitch them. Pim is six, and he is the one who asks why about everything. And wrapped in a soft bundle of fur in a hollow near the hearth is Nia, who is about eight months old, and who has just discovered that she can grab her own toes.

Seven people. One house of bone. One fire.

Here is a small question to begin with, one that we'll answer before this part is done. How do we know when this camp was here? There are no calendars, no coins, no written records. So how can anyone say "eighteen thousand years ago" and mean it?

Imagine, now, that you are standing beside Ennu on the frozen terrace as the sun touches the horizon. Your breath is pouring out of you in white clouds. The cold is not wet and raw like a British winter; it's dry and sharp, and it gets inside your nose and makes your eyes water and your cheeks sting. Ennu stops, and turns, and looks out over the river valley to the west, where the sun is going down. You look with her. The light on the snow is turning pink, then violet. The willows by the river are black. Out on the far slope, a line of small dark shapes is moving slowly along the skyline. Horses, perhaps. Or reindeer. Ennu watches them for a long moment, the way you might glance at the weather forecast on your phone, reading something in them that you can't. Then she nods to herself, and turns, and walks on towards the bone house.

The place we're standing was first found, in the modern world, by accident. In 1965, a farmer in the village of Mezhyrich was digging to enlarge the cellar under his house, and his spade struck something hard and enormous. It was the lower jawbone of a mammoth. Archaeologists came, and over the years that followed, beginning with the Ukrainian scientist Ivan Pidoplichko, they uncovered not just one bone but thousands, and among them the remains of four great circular structures built of mammoth bone. Their walls were made, in large part, of mammoth lower jaws, turned upside down and stacked one inside another, in a pattern that the excavators compared to the herringbone weave of a cloth. Mammoth skulls had been built into the walls too. And all around them there were hearths, and heaps of ash, and workshops where stone tools were made, and deep pits dug into the earth. Pidoplichko described these mammoth-bone dwellings in detailed books, and one of them was translated into English and published by the United States Geological Survey in the 1970s. Mezhyrich became one of the most famous Ice Age sites in the world.

And now, here is the answer to our small question, and with it, our first disagreement among scientists.

For years, Mezhyrich was usually described as being about fifteen thousand years old. That figure came largely from radiocarbon dating. In 2012, a team of French researchers led by Laurent Marquer published a careful study of the tiny scraps of charcoal in the camp's hearths, in the Journal of Archaeological Science, and the charcoal gave dates of around fourteen thousand six hundred radiocarbon years. But radiocarbon years are not quite the same as calendar years. Over thousands of years, the amount of radiocarbon in the atmosphere has wobbled, and so raw radiocarbon dates have to be calibrated, converted, using records from tree rings, cave formations and ocean sediments, into real years. And when you do that, a date of fourteen and a half thousand radiocarbon years turns into something rather older.

Then, in 2025, a team from Leiden University in the Netherlands, led by Wei Chu, together with the Ukrainian archaeologist Pavlo Shydlovskyi and the German archaeologist Andreas Maier, published a new study in the journal Open Research Europe. They noticed a problem. Many of the earlier dates had been taken from mammoth bones. But the people who built these houses may well have gathered mammoth bones that were already old, lying on the steppe from animals that had died years, or even centuries, before. A house built of old bones would look older than it really was. So instead, Chu and his colleagues dated the small bones of animals that the people had eaten or trapped: foxes, hares and wolves, found inside one of the bone houses, in a nearby pit, and in the trampled ground outside. Their result placed the bone house they studied, known as Mammoth Bone Structure Four, at between about 18,250 and 17,750 years ago. And they found that the camp may have been occupied for a fairly short time, from a single season up to, at most, a few centuries.

So, depending on which study you read, Mezhyrich is about fifteen thousand years old, or about eighteen thousand. The truth is that the new date and the old charcoal date are not as far apart as they sound, because one is in calendar years and the other in radiocarbon years. But scientists are still debating the details, and the Leiden team themselves have called for more sampling. Tonight, I'll follow the newest evidence. Our winter night is around eighteen thousand years ago.

Let me put that in a way that's easier to hold. The great pyramids of Egypt were built about four and a half thousand years ago. Stonehenge, a little before that. If you laid those four and a half thousand years end to end four times over, you would still not quite reach back to this camp. The first farmers, the first villages, the first wheat fields, the first written words, all of it lies far, far in this family's future. And yet the people here are no further from you, in body and in mind, than your own great-grandparents.

Think of it in terms of your own home. Your house was probably built within the last century or two. It has foundations, perhaps of concrete, and walls of brick or timber, and a roof of tile or slate, and it was designed by someone, approved by someone, and built by people who used machines. Ennu's house was built by her own family and their neighbours, by hand, from the remains of animals that weighed several tonnes each. And it was built to stand through a winter colder than almost anything that a modern European city ever has to face.

The sun is gone now. The copper line on the horizon has faded to a dull red smudge, and the snow has turned blue. Ennu reaches the bone house, and bends low, because the entrance is low, and pushes aside a heavy hide that hangs across the doorway. A breath of warm, smoky air comes out to meet her.

She looks back once over her shoulder, towards the plain, where the hunters should be coming from. Nothing yet.

Then she ducks inside, and the hide falls back into place behind her.

And we follow.

## Part Two — Around Four O'Clock: The House of Bones

Here is the small question for this part of the evening. How many mammoths does it take to build a house?

It sounds like the beginning of a joke. But it's a real question, and archaeologists have spent a long time counting, and we'll have an answer of sorts before this part is through.

First, though, let's look around.

Imagine you are stepping in through the low doorway behind Ennu now, ducking your head, pushing past the heavy hide that hangs across the entrance. The hide is stiff with cold on its outside and soft on its inside, and it smells of smoke and animal. You straighten up, carefully, because the roof is not high. And you stand still for a moment, letting your eyes adjust.

The first thing you notice is the warmth. Not the warmth of a modern living room, nothing like it. But after the knife of the air outside, it is like stepping into a coat that someone has just taken off. The second thing you notice is the smell: smoke, mostly, and fat, and hide, and people, and something faintly sweet and scorched that you can't name yet. And the third thing you notice is the fire. It's low, and small, a heap of glowing embers in a shallow scoop in the floor near the middle of the house, with a few small flames licking at something laid across it. It gives off a soft orange light that reaches the faces of the people sitting nearest to it and then fades into shadow.

Now look up, and look around, at the walls.

They curve up and over you, like the inside of a great upturned bowl. And as your eyes adjust, you begin to see what they are made of. Down at the base, all the way around, there are huge, heavy, curved shapes, pale and rough, packed tightly together. Those are mammoth jawbones. Each one is a lower jaw, the kind of bone that, in your own body, holds your bottom teeth. But a mammoth's lower jaw is massive, and shaped a little like a great broad scoop, and the people who built this house have turned them upside down and fitted them one into another, nested together, like a row of bowls stacked on their sides, so that they make a thick, solid wall. Above them, there are long bones, leg bones, and shoulder blades, broad and flat like paddles, and here and there the great domed curve of a skull. And arching overhead, perhaps, there are tusks, long and curved and heavy, which may have helped hold up the roof.

Over all of that, outside, there are hides. We can't see them from in here, and of course they haven't survived eighteen thousand years in the ground. Archaeologists can't dig up a roof that rotted away so long ago. But most of those who've studied these houses think the bone frames would have been covered with animal skins, and perhaps with turf or earth, and weighed down against the wind. On a night like this, there might be a layer of snow on top too, which, strange as it sounds, would help hold in the warmth.

The house you're standing in is not large. It's roughly circular, and perhaps five metres across, a little less than the width of a modern double garage. Some of the structures at Mezhyrich were larger, the largest perhaps eight metres or so. In a space like this, seven people can sit, and work, and sleep around the fire, but nobody is far from anybody else.

So now, our small question. How many mammoths does it take?

We have a very good recent count from a site not far away. Several hundred kilometres to the east, at a place in western Russia called Kostenki, a team led by the archaeologist Alexander Pryor, of the University of Exeter, excavated a great circular structure of mammoth bone and published their results in the journal Antiquity in 2020. It was a big one, about nine metres by nine. And in its walls, they counted fifty-one mammoth lower jaws and sixty-four mammoth skulls. Sixty-four skulls. That means the bones of at least sixty-four mammoths went into that single structure. Alongside the mammoth bones, they found small numbers of bones from reindeer, horses, bears, wolves, red foxes and Arctic foxes.

The houses at Mezhyrich are smaller, but they are still built from the remains of dozens of animals, each of which weighed several tonnes when it was alive. So the answer to our small question is something like this: a few dozen, for a small house, and sixty or more for a very large one. Building a house like this was not a family job. It needed many people, working together, over days.

And this kind of house was not unique to Mezhyrich. Archaeologists know of around seventy of these mammoth-bone structures, scattered across what is now Ukraine and the western part of the Russian Plain. For thousands of years, through the coldest part of the last Ice Age, people on this great open land built their winter homes from the bones of the animals that shared it with them.

Archaeologists have a name for the people who built the houses at Mezhyrich, or rather, for their way of making things. They call it the Epigravettian. It's an awkward word, but it has a simple story behind it. Long before this night, across much of Europe, people made stone tools and ornaments in a style that archaeologists named the Gravettian, after a rock shelter in south-western France called La Gravette, where that style was first described. The prefix "epi" means something like "after" or "following on". So the Epigravettian is the tradition that grew out of the Gravettian, in the east and the south of Europe, through and after the coldest part of the last Ice Age. Ennu, of course, has never heard of France, and would not recognise the word. To her, the way her people knap flint is simply the right way to do it, the way her mother did it, and her mother's mother.

The tools are all around you, now that you look. A little heap of flint flakes and blades by Tula's knee. Scrapers. Awls, slender points of stone and bone for piercing hide. A bone with a sharp, polished tip. A few pieces of mammoth ivory, carefully set aside. And near the wall, in a hollow scraped out of the earth floor, a pile of something soft and dark that turns out to be dried grass and moss, kept for tinder and for stuffing into boots.

There is one more thing about those bones in the wall that I find quietly moving. Many of them were almost certainly not fresh when they were used. The steppe was scattered with the remains of mammoths that had died, of old age, or sickness, or accident, years or even generations before. The people here walked out across the plain, and found those great bones, bleached and half-buried in the grass, and dragged them home, and gave them a second life, as walls. That's exactly why the Leiden team didn't trust dates from the mammoth bone itself. A jawbone in Ennu's wall might be from an animal that died long before Ennu's grandmother was born.

Let's step back from the walls now, and sit down by the fire, next to Siva.

She looks up at you and gives you a small nod, and moves over a little on the hide she's sitting on, to make room. The floor under you is earth, beaten hard and covered with hides and furs. It's warmer than you'd expect. Siva goes back to what she was doing, which is working a piece of reindeer hide in her lap, folding it, feeling it, checking it for thin places. Across the fire, Tula is sitting with a scraper, a little tool of flint with a rounded edge, working at the inside of another hide, scraping away the last of the fat and flesh. Pim is lying on his front, chin in his hands, staring at the fire. Nia is asleep in her nest of fur.

And here, I'm afraid, we come to a point where the archaeologists disagree. Not quietly, either.

The first disagreement is about the shape of these houses. The neat, domed huts you can see in museum reconstructions, and in drawings in books, are just that: reconstructions. When the bones were excavated, they were lying collapsed in the ground, flattened, tumbled, sometimes scattered. Turning that tumble back into a standing building takes a great deal of careful thought, and some guesswork. And not everyone has agreed with the first versions. The scholars Jelinek and Hanzalek, for example, argued that one of the reconstructions, with rows of skulls set high up in the walls, simply couldn't have stood up in the way it was drawn, and they offered a different design of their own. So when I describe the walls of Ennu's house, I'm describing one reasonable version, not a photograph.

The second disagreement goes deeper. Were these structures really houses at all?

For a long time, it was simply taken for granted that they were dwellings, homes where families lived through the winter. But in their study of the great bone circle at Kostenki, Alexander Pryor and his colleagues found something surprising. The intensity of activity there seemed less than you'd expect from a long-term base camp. They found burned wood, and more than fifty tiny charred seeds, the remains of plants, perhaps food, perhaps fuel. They concluded that the people there had learned where to find edible plants, even in the Ice Age. But they also raised the question of whether the structure might have been used for something else, perhaps for storing food, or as a monument, or for some purpose we haven't yet guessed. Other archaeologists, over the years, have suggested that some of these bone heaps might have been food stores, or places of ceremony, or even something to do with the dead.

But at Mezhyrich, the Leiden team, in their study of 2025, came down firmly on the other side. Wei Chu and his colleagues described the structure they dated as a probable dwelling, a shelter built to protect people from the harsh climate of the last Ice Age, and their dating fitted well with the idea that the camp was lived in during a single period, rather than being used and re-used over thousands of years. So the honest answer is that some of these bone circles were very likely homes, and others may have been something else, and the archaeologists are still working it out, one site at a time. Tonight, I'm siding with Chu. Ennu's house is a home.

Let's look at it through modern eyes for a moment. In your own home, the walls are probably filled with insulation: fibreglass, or foam, or mineral wool, and your windows are double-glazed, and the warm air from your heating is held in by a sealed shell that builders have spent a century learning to perfect. Ennu's house has no glass, no insulation, no central heating. Its walls are bone and hide. And yet it has some of the same qualities. It's small, which means there's less air to heat. It's round, which means there are fewer corners for cold to gather in. It's low, and partly sunk into the earth, which protects it from the wind. And it is full of people. A resting human body gives off something like a hundred watts of heat, roughly as much as an old-fashioned light bulb. Seven people, sitting close together in a small room, are a little furnace all by themselves.

And there's one more thing the house has. It has the fire.

Let's look at the hearth properly now, because it's going to be with us all night, from now until morning, and it's the heart of everything that happens in this house.

It's a shallow, roughly round hollow scooped into the floor, lined with the ash of many earlier fires, grey and soft and fine. In it, there is a low heap of embers, glowing a deep orange-red. Laid across the embers there are a few thin sticks of willow, crackling quietly. But there is something else on the fire too, something you might not expect. A piece of pale, broken bone, lying right in the embers, with a small, steady, yellowish flame running along its edge. It hisses faintly. It's from there that the sweet, scorched smell is coming.

Hold on to that. That's the third of our three mysteries, and its time is not yet.

At Mezhyrich, the archaeologists found hearths both inside the bone houses and outside them, and around the hearths, the evidence of everyday life: burned bone, ash, stone tools, the waste from making tools, and the bones of the animals that were eaten. The fire is where everything happens. It's where food is cooked, and where tools are mended, and where hides are softened, and where stories are told. It's the centre of this house in a way that nothing in a modern home quite is.

Ennu lays her bundle of willow sticks down beside the hearth, carefully, in a neat little pile. She sits on her heels and looks into the fire for a while. Then she takes one of the willow sticks, snaps it in half across her knee, and lays the two pieces in the embers, crosswise, where the flames can find them. They catch. The light in the house brightens a little, and the shadows of the great curved bones on the walls lean and sway.

Pim asks his grandmother when his father is coming home. Ennu says, soon. Pim asks how soon. Ennu says, when the stars come out. Pim crawls to the doorway and lifts the edge of the hide and peers out at the sky, which is going from violet to deep blue, and reports that there is one star. Ennu says that one star isn't enough. Pim lies down by the doorway to wait for more.

Outside, the last of the light is draining out of the world. The snow on the plain has gone from blue to grey. The willows by the river have vanished into shadow. And somewhere out there, two men are walking home across the frozen land, carrying something heavy between them.

## Part Three — Around Five O'Clock: The Hunters Come Home

The small question for this hour is a hunter's question. On a frozen plain where great herds of giant animals roam, what do two hunters actually bring home on an ordinary winter evening?

You might expect the answer to be a mammoth. Let's see.

Imagine you are out on the plain now, a mile or so from the camp, walking beside Haro and Mael in the gathering dark. The snow is thin here, blown into ridges by the wind of the last few days, with the stiff grey stems of grass and wormwood poking through. Your feet crunch. Your breath freezes on the fur around your hood, and you can feel tiny beads of ice forming on your eyelashes. Above you, the sky is a deep, clear, endless blue going to black, and the first stars are coming out, sharp and many, and to the south-west there is still the faintest glow where the sun went down an hour ago. There is no moon yet. There are no lights anywhere in the whole world, not one, except, far ahead of you, a tiny orange spark low on the terrace above the river, where the smoke-hole of a bone house is glowing.

Haro walks steadily, not fast, not slow, in the long, easy, tireless stride of a man who has walked across this land every day of his life. Over his shoulder, slung on a cord, are three white hares, their fur thick and soft and pure as the snow. Mael is carrying a fox. It's an Arctic fox, small and fluffy, its winter coat a creamy white, and he's very proud of it, because it's the first fox he's taken this winter, with a snare he set himself in the willows down by the river, two days ago.

So there's the answer to our small question. On an ordinary winter evening, what the hunters bring home is not usually a mammoth. It's hares. It's a fox. On a good day, perhaps a reindeer, or a horse, which are much larger and much harder to take. And we know that these small animals mattered at Mezhyrich because their bones are all through the camp. When the Leiden team chose what to date, they picked exactly these animals: the bones of foxes, hares and wolves, found inside the bone house, in a pit nearby, and in the trampled ground outside. Each of those little bones belonged to an animal that someone brought home, perhaps on an evening just like this one.

Foxes and hares were not only food. Their furs were precious, perhaps more precious than their meat. An Arctic fox's winter coat is one of the warmest furs of any animal on earth. And the long, soft fur of a winter hare, though it tears easily, is wonderfully light and warm, perfect for lining a hood, or a mitten, or a baby's bedding. Siva will want both of these.

Now, as you walk, look out across the plain to the north, in the dim light. There's a low ridge there, and along it, very faintly, a line of great dark humps, moving slowly. They're a long way off, and it's nearly dark, and you can barely make them out. But Haro has seen them. He stops for a moment and looks, the way Ennu looked at the horses on the skyline earlier. Mael looks too. Neither of them says anything.

Mammoths.

Let me tell you a little about them, because they are the reason this camp exists. The woolly mammoth was roughly the size of a modern African elephant, though some were smaller, with a high, domed head, a sloping back, small ears, a short tail, and a thick, shaggy coat of long dark hair over a dense woolly undercoat. Its tusks were long and spiralling, curving out and up and in. It ate grass, and sedges, and low shrubs, enormous amounts of it every day, and it roamed this great cold grassland that scientists call the mammoth steppe, which once stretched from the edge of the Atlantic across Europe and Asia and into North America. It was a dry, cold, open land, very different from the frozen tundra of the far north today. There were mammoths here, and woolly rhinoceros, and herds of horses, and reindeer, and bison, and the animals that hunted them: wolves, and cave lions, and bears. And there were people.

And here we come to one of the most interesting questions of the whole night, and one of the most argued about.

Did the people of this camp hunt mammoths? Or did they mostly gather the bones of mammoths that had already died?

It sounds as though it ought to be easy to answer. It isn't. In 2008, the Belgian palaeontologist Mietje Germonpré and her colleagues, writing about another mammoth-bone site on the Russian Plain, set out the possible explanations for how all those bones came to be in the camps. They ranged from people gathering bones at places where mammoths had died naturally, all the way to people using the bones of animals they had actively hunted and killed themselves. And Germonpré and her colleagues noted plainly that the idea of active mammoth hunting remained controversial.

On one side, the American archaeologist Olga Soffer, who worked for many years on the sites of the Russian Plain, including Mezhyrich, argued that most of the mammoth remains in these camps had probably been collected rather than hunted. A computer simulation study of these settlements later found results that agreed with her. The American archaeologist Gary Haynes, who spent years studying the bones of modern elephants that had died naturally in Africa, showed that natural processes, such as heavy animals trampling the bones, can break them in ways that look very like the work of human hands. That made people more cautious about reading every broken mammoth bone as a sign of a hunt.

But on the other side, there's the evidence from the pits around the Mezhyrich houses. A study presented in 2010 reported that the mammoth meat that people were eating at Mezhyrich seemed to come from just a few young animals, juveniles and young adults, and that these were probably hunted. Young animals are easier to take than great adult bulls. And in 2026, a large new study in the journal Current Biology looked at the ancient DNA of more than five hundred mammoths, and compared which sex they were. The researchers found that in natural deposits, where mammoths had died without any human involvement, most of the animals were male. But at sites shaped by human activity, most of them were female. That's a striking difference. Female mammoths, like female elephants today, lived in family herds, with their young. If people were taking mostly females, it suggests something more than simply picking up whatever bones happened to be lying about. It suggests choice.

So the honest answer, for now, is probably both. The people of the mammoth steppe may sometimes have hunted mammoths, perhaps mostly young ones and females from family herds, and much more often they gathered the bones and tusks of animals that had died naturally, which were scattered across the land for anyone who knew where to look. The debate is still going on, and each new technique, from radiocarbon to ancient DNA, adds another piece.

Haro and Mael walk on. Haro is thinking, as hunters always do, about the herd on the ridge. Which way they're moving. How many young there might be. Whether, when the weather turns, it might be worth gathering the other men of the camp and going out together. Mael is thinking about his fox.

While they walk, look up. Because the sky above this plain is one of the great sights of the Ice Age, and almost nobody alive today has seen anything like it. With no light anywhere on the ground, and the air dry and still and clear, the stars come out not in ones and twos, but in thousands, and then in tens of thousands. The Milky Way rises out of the snow in the east and arches right over your head, a long, pale, glowing river with dark lanes of dust running through it. It's so bright that, after a while, you can almost see your own shadow by it.

And here's a small, true, strange thing about those stars. They're the same stars you can see from your own garden, on a dark night far from a city. The same patterns, almost exactly, because eighteen thousand years is not long enough for the stars to move much against each other. But the sky is turning around a different point. Today, the whole northern sky wheels slowly around Polaris, the Pole Star. But the Earth wobbles very slowly on its axis, like a spinning top, in a cycle that takes about twenty-six thousand years, and so the point in the sky that the north pole points to slowly drifts. Polaris has only been close to the pole for the last couple of thousand years or so. On this night, eighteen thousand years ago, Polaris is not the pole star at all. The northern sky is turning around a different, emptier patch of stars. If you could ask Haro which star stays still while all the others turn, he would not point to the one you would.

He would, though, know the sky. People who live entirely outdoors, who walk home in the dark every winter evening, know the night sky the way you know the streets of your own town. Which stars rise first at dusk in the cold season. Which ones climb high by the middle of the night. Which ones are setting as the dawn begins. We can't know what stories Haro tells about them. Every people who have ever lived under an open sky seem to have told stories about the stars. His have all been lost. But the stars themselves are still there, waiting, every clear night.

Mael, walking beside him, has his head tipped back, looking up. He says something to Haro about a bright star low in the south, and Haro answers, and they both laugh, quietly, and walk on.

Let's set this beside your own life for a moment. If you need food tonight, you can probably walk to a shop, or open a fridge, or tap a screen and have a meal arrive at your door within the hour. Your food comes from farms you'll never see, through supply chains so long and complex that no single person understands all of it. Haro's food comes from the land he can see with his own eyes, by the work of his own hands, and the knowledge in his own head. He knows every fold of ground for a day's walk in every direction. He knows where the hares run, and where the foxes den, and where the river is shallow enough to cross on the ice, and where the horses go to graze in the deep of winter. He carries, in his memory, a map more detailed than any you've ever used. And if he makes a mistake, there is no shop to go to.

There's one more thing about this land that you'd notice, walking across it in the dark. It's quiet. Completely quiet. No traffic, no hum of machines, no aircraft overhead, no distant roads. Just the crunch of your feet, and the sigh of your own breathing, and once, far away, the long, rising call of a wolf, answered by another. Haro lifts his head and listens to the wolves. He isn't afraid of them. Wolves and people have been sharing this land for a very long time, and they mostly keep out of each other's way. But he notes where the sound is coming from. He notes everything.

The orange spark of the camp is getting closer now. You can see the shapes of the bone houses against the snow, low and rounded, and the glow of the smoke-holes, and you can smell the smoke on the still air, a faint, sweet, scorched smell, the same one you noticed in the house. And out of the dark ahead, a small figure comes running, stumbling over the frozen ground, shouting.

It's Pim. He's been waiting by the doorway, counting stars, and when there were enough of them, he decided that was the signal, and ran out to meet his father.

Haro scoops him up with one arm, hares and all, and Pim grabs at the white fur and asks if he can have one, and Haro says they're for his mother. Pim asks Mael if he can hold the fox. Mael lets him. Pim holds it very carefully, with both arms, the way you'd hold a baby, and walks the rest of the way home in silence, almost bursting with importance.

At the doorway, Ennu is waiting, holding the hide aside. She looks at the hares, and at the fox, and nods. Not a big catch. But a good one. Enough.

Inside, Siva takes the hares and lays them by the wall where it's cool, to be skinned in the morning. She takes the fox from Pim and runs her hand along its thick creamy fur, feeling it, judging it, already planning, the way a tailor might feel a bolt of cloth. Tula comes to look too. The fur will become the lining of something, or the trim of a hood. Nothing will be wasted. Not the fur, not the meat, not the sinew, not the bones.

Haro sits down by the fire at last, and stretches his frozen hands towards it, and then, slowly, carefully, begins to unlace his boots. His feet are cold. Not dangerously cold. His boots are good. But cold.

Ennu reaches past him and lays another stick of willow on the fire. And beside it, another piece of bone.

It's a little after five. The hunters are home. The hide is down across the door. And now, at last, it's nearly time to eat.

## Part Four — Around Six O'Clock: Meat, Marrow and the Pits in the Frozen Ground

Here is this hour's small question, and it's a simple one. What's for supper?

The hares are lying by the wall, and the fox too, and they won't be eaten tonight. They need skinning, and that's a careful job for daylight, so as not to spoil the furs. So what is this family going to eat, on the coldest night of the year, in a land where nothing has grown for months?

Ennu knows. She's already getting up.

Imagine you are following her out of the bone house now, ducking under the hide, into the dark. It's properly night, now. The sun set a little before four, by our clocks, and by six, the last of the twilight has gone completely. I worked that out, by the way, with a modern astronomical program, for a midwinter evening at this latitude. Nobody here, of course, has a clock, or would care what one said. To them, it's simply dark. The stars are blazing. The snow gives off a faint, ghostly grey light of its own. And the cold, after the warmth of the house, is so sharp that it makes you gasp.

Look around you, while your eyes adjust. Off to one side, a little way along the terrace, there's another low, rounded shape against the snow, with a faint glow at its top. Another bone house. And beyond it, perhaps, another. At Mezhyrich, as I said, the excavators found four of these structures. Whether all four were standing and lived in at the same time, in the same winter, is something archaeologists still can't be sure of. The new dates from the Leiden team fit well with a single period of occupation, but they can't rule out that people came back to the place again and again, over a few centuries at most. Tonight, I'll imagine that there are neighbours. A few other families, related to Ennu's by blood and marriage, each in their own house of bone, each with their own fire, perhaps thirty or forty people in all, sharing this terrace above the river through the winter.

Ennu walks a few steps from the doorway, to a place where the ground is a little raised, and covered with a heap of something dark. She kneels, and begins to pull it aside. It's a pile of heavy bones, and under them, a flat mammoth shoulder blade, broad as a tray, and under that, a layer of hide, stiff and frozen. And under the hide, there's a hole in the ground.

It's not large. Perhaps an arm's length across, and deeper than that. Ennu reaches down into it, right up to her shoulder, feeling around in the dark. And she brings out a great, stiff, frozen lump of something dark red and white. Meat. Horse meat, cut from a horse that Haro and the other men killed several weeks ago, when the first hard frosts came. It's frozen solid, as hard as wood. She brings out another piece, smaller, with a lot of white fat on it. Then she lays the hide back over the hole, and the shoulder blade over the hide, and the bones over that, heavy and careful, so that nothing can get in.

And so we come, a little earlier than you might have expected, to the first of our three mysteries.

I asked you, at the very beginning of the night: in the coldest season of the year, when the ground is frozen as hard as iron, why would the people of this camp have gone to the enormous trouble of digging deep holes into the earth, all around their homes?

You've just seen the answer. The holes are larders. And in winter, they're something even better. They're freezers.

At Mezhyrich, the archaeologists found the bone houses surrounded by pits. Lots of them. Dug into the ground around the dwellings, some of them large, and filled, by the time they were excavated, with animal bones, and ash, and stone tools, and other leftovers of life. The Encyclopedia of Ukraine, describing the site, calls Mezhyrich a winter settlement of Palaeolithic hunters, and lists, among the things the excavators found there, storage pits and fire pits. One of the pits, known as Pit Six, has had a whole study of its own, published in a Ukrainian archaeological journal, devoted entirely to the animal bones that were found inside it. And as we heard in the last part, studies of the pits suggest that some of the meat people were eating came from young mammoths.

Here is how most archaeologists have understood those pits. Through the deep of the Ice Age winter, this land was bitterly cold, and the ground near the surface would freeze hard. So in the autumn, when the hunting was good, people dug pits, before the ground froze too hard to dig, and filled them with meat, and fat, and bones full of marrow, and covered them over with hides and heavy bones, to keep out the wolves and the foxes. Then, as winter came down, the pits froze, and everything in them froze too, and stayed frozen, safe and fresh, for months. A pit in the frozen earth is a natural deep-freeze. Whenever the family needed food, someone went out, uncovered the pit, reached down, and took out what they needed. Just as Ennu has done.

So that's our first mystery solved. The people of this camp dug holes in the ground around their houses because those holes were where they kept their food. In a land where nothing grows for half the year, a pit full of frozen meat was the difference between a winter you survive and a winter you don't.

I should be honest, though, about what we can and can't know. When the pits were excavated, thousands of years after they were dug, many of them were full of things that had been thrown away: broken bones, ash, worn-out tools. So some of them may have ended their lives as rubbish pits, even if they began as stores. And archaeologists still argue about exactly what each one was used for. But the idea that at least some of them were stores for food through the cold season has been one of the most widely held explanations ever since they were first found.

Let's put that beside your own kitchen. You probably have a fridge, and a freezer, humming quietly in the corner, kept cold by electricity and a little pump circulating a refrigerant. Inside, there's food from many countries, frozen in factories, packed in plastic, labelled with dates. Ennu's freezer is the earth itself. It's powered by the winter. It has no door, only a hide and a shoulder blade. And instead of a label, it has Ennu's memory. She knows exactly what's in every pit around this house, and how much, and how long it must last. When the spring comes, and the ground begins to thaw, everything in those pits will have to be eaten quickly, or it will spoil. So she's counting, even now. Always counting.

There's something else about these pits that's easy to miss. They are a sign of planning. Of thinking ahead, months ahead, to a time that hasn't come yet. Digging a deep hole in the ground with tools of bone, antler and stone is hard, slow work. Filling it with meat means not eating that meat now, when you could. It means trusting that you'll be here, in this place, when the snow is deep, and that the food will still be there waiting for you. Every pit around Ennu's house is a small act of faith in the future. And when the archaeologists at Mezhyrich lifted the earth out of those pits, thousands of years later, what they were uncovering, in a way, was the forethought of people who had looked at an autumn day and imagined the bitter heart of the winter to come.

Back inside, Ennu lays the frozen meat on a flat piece of bone near the fire, to begin to thaw. It will take a while. In the meantime, there's something else to eat.

Haro has a mammoth leg bone, a long, heavy, thick-walled bone, which has been lying at the edge of the hearth all afternoon, warming slowly. Now he takes a heavy, rounded stone, and with a sharp, practised blow, cracks the bone open along its length. Inside, there's marrow: a long, soft, pale yellow core of fat, rich and warm from the fire. He scoops out a piece with a sliver of bone and gives it to Nia, who's awake now and watching everything, and she sucks it with enormous satisfaction. Then Pim, then Tula, then the rest of them.

Marrow, to these people, is one of the best foods in the world. It's nearly pure fat, packed with energy, and fat is what a human body most needs to stay warm through a winter like this one. Lean meat on its own, without enough fat, is not enough to live on for long. People who live on meat in the cold have always known this, and have always prized the fattiest parts of every animal: the marrow, the fat around the organs, the fat under the skin, the tongue, the brain. On the mammoth steppe, every bone that came into camp was likely to be cracked for its marrow, sooner or later. Many of the broken bones archaeologists find at sites like this were probably broken for exactly that reason.

Then, when the horse meat has thawed enough, Ennu cuts it into strips with a flint blade, quick and sure, and lays the strips on the hot embers and the edges of the hearth stones, where they sizzle and spit and blacken. The smell fills the house. It's the best smell in the world. Pim asks four times if it's ready yet.

And now here is a point where the archaeologists have changed their minds, or at least, are still arguing. Did these people eat only meat?

For a long time, the picture of Ice Age people on the cold steppes was that they lived almost entirely on animals. And it's true that through the depths of winter, there would have been very little else. But in their study of the bone circle at Kostenki, published in 2020, Alexander Pryor and his colleagues found something that changed the picture a little. Inside the circle, among the burnt bones, there were charred remains of plants. Wood, yes, but also non-woody plant tissue, and more than fifty tiny charred seeds. Some of these may simply have come from plants that grew nearby. But others may be the remains of cooking, and of meals. Pryor and his colleagues suggested that the people there had learned where to forage for edible plants, even in the Ice Age. Roots, perhaps. Berries, in the short summer. Seeds and greens. And plant material might also have been used as fuel, or for tinder, or for wicks.

So the people of the mammoth steppe were mostly meat-eaters, especially in winter. But probably not only meat-eaters. In the summer and autumn, the women and children especially may have spent many days gathering plants along the rivers, and some of what they gathered might have been dried and stored for the winter. It's very hard to prove, because plants, unlike bones, rarely survive eighteen thousand years. But every new site that's excavated carefully, with fine sieves and modern methods, seems to add a little more evidence.

In Ennu's house tonight, along with the meat, there's a small bundle of dried roots, gathered in the autumn by the river and kept in a hide bag hanging from a tusk in the roof. She hands one to each child. They're hard and stringy and faintly sweet, and the children chew them slowly, like sweets.

Supper doesn't take long. Nobody here eats slowly. The meat is torn from the strips with the teeth, and the grease runs down the children's chins, and Tula wipes Pim's face with a handful of moss, and he squirms. The bones are set aside, every one, because bones are useful. Some will be cracked for more marrow. Some will become tools. Some will be burned. Nothing here is wasted. Not one scrap.

When everybody has eaten, Haro leans back against the curving bone wall and closes his eyes for a moment. Mael licks his fingers. Siva, who has been feeding Nia, settles her back in her fur nest. And Ennu sits by the fire, as she always does, closest to it, and looks into the embers.

There's one more small detail about tonight's supper that I like. The people of this camp ate their meat cooked. Fire has been used by humans and their ancestors for hundreds of thousands of years, and cooking makes food softer, safer, and much easier to digest, so that a body gets more energy from every mouthful. Every scrap of horse meat that Ennu laid on those embers tonight was a little piece of very old wisdom, passed down from people who lived long, long before her, by way of hands and fires, generation after generation, back into the deep past.

Outside, the frost is hardening. The stars are turning. Inside, the meat is eaten, the marrow is gone, and the children are full and drowsy and warm.

But the night has hardly begun. There is work still to do. And much of it has to do with the clothes everybody here is wearing.

## Part Five — Around Seven O'Clock: Fur, Hide and the Warmth of Clothes

The small question for this part of the evening is about what everyone in this house is wearing. On a night like this, how many layers is each person dressed in, and what are they made of?

Let's start by looking at Haro, because he's just come in from the cold and he hasn't taken everything off yet.

Imagine you're sitting beside him by the fire now, close enough to touch the sleeve of his coat. Go on. Reach out. It's fur, on the outside, thick and dense and slightly stiff, the hairs pointing downwards like the coat of the animal it came from, so that snow and frost slide off it. Reindeer, probably. Reindeer hair is hollow, each hair a tiny tube full of air, which makes it one of the warmest furs there is. The coat comes down past his hips. It has a hood, which he's pushed back now, and the hood is trimmed around the face with longer, softer fur, perhaps wolf or fox, which catches his breath and keeps the frost from forming on his skin. The sleeves are long, and at the wrists, they're tied snug with thin cords, so the wind can't get up inside.

Now, look closer. Haro is leaning forward, and the coat has fallen open at the neck, and underneath it there is another layer. This one is fur too, but turned the other way, with the hair against his skin, soft and warm. Two layers, then, one with the fur outside and one with the fur inside, with a thin layer of still, warm air trapped between them. That's the secret of it. Not one thick coat, but two lighter ones, worn together.

On his legs, trousers of hide, also fur-lined. On his feet, boots: soft, high boots of hide, stuffed inside with dried grass and moss, which he's now pulling out, damp and flattened, and laying by the fire to dry. On his hands, a moment ago, mittens, fur inside, hanging now on a cord around his neck, so he can't lose them in the snow.

That's our answer, more or less. Four or five layers, all of them made from the skins and furs of the animals of the steppe, all of them shaped and fitted to the body. And all of them sewn.

I have to be honest with you about how we know this, because it's one of the hardest things in archaeology to be sure of. Clothes almost never survive. Fur and hide rot away in the ground in a few years, or a few decades at most, and these clothes have been gone for eighteen thousand years. Not a single stitch of Ice Age clothing from the mammoth steppe has ever been found. So everything I've just described is reconstructed, from clues. And the clues come from some remarkable places.

One of them is a set of tiny carvings found far to the east, in Siberia, near Lake Baikal, at a place called Mal'ta. In the late 1920s and 1930s, the Russian archaeologist Mikhail Gerasimov excavated an Ice Age settlement there, and among the things he found were small figurines carved from mammoth ivory, about twenty-three thousand years old. Many of them seem to show human figures wearing clothes, carved with patterns that some archaeologists have read as fur. And at a nearby site called Buret', one ivory figure seems to wear a hood, and something like a one-piece suit, covering the whole body from head to foot. To some scholars, these little figures look a lot like people dressed in the kind of tailored fur clothing worn by Arctic peoples in recent centuries. Other scholars are more cautious, and say the patterns might mean something else entirely. But they're one of the closest glimpses we have.

There's a lovely recent twist to the Mal'ta story. In 2021, a study published by Cambridge University Press looked again at those little ivory figures, and suggested that they might not all show adults. Some of them, the authors argued, might show children, at different ages and stages, from babies to adolescents, each with clothing and ornaments of their own. If that's right, then some of the oldest pictures of clothing anywhere in the world are pictures of children, dressed up warm against the Siberian winter, by people who loved them enough to carve them.

And there's another clue, and it's one of the most extraordinary pieces of detective work in all of science. It comes from lice.

Human beings carry two kinds of lice, which are very closely related. Head lice live in the hair of the head. But body lice, or clothing lice, are different. They live in clothes. They lay their eggs in the seams and folds of fabric, and they only come out to feed. They cannot survive for long without clothing. So, scientists reasoned, body lice must have evolved from head lice at some point after human beings began to wear clothes regularly. And if you could work out when the body louse split from the head louse, you'd have a clue to when people began to dress.

In 2003, three geneticists, Ralf Kittler, Manfred Kayser and Mark Stoneking, at the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology in Leipzig, published a study in the journal Current Biology. They collected head lice and body lice from people around the world, and compared their DNA, using a kind of molecular clock, which estimates how long ago two lineages split from the rate at which their DNA has changed. Their conclusion, in their own words, was that body lice originated "not more than about 72,000 ± 42,000 years ago."

That's a wide range, as you can hear. Anything from about thirty thousand years ago to more than a hundred thousand.

And here's our disagreement. A few years later, a team that included Melissa Toups and David Reed, of the University of Florida, ran a new analysis, using more of the louse's DNA and a different method. And their answer was older. Much older. They found that clothing lice had split from head lice at least eighty-three thousand years ago, and possibly as much as a hundred and seventy thousand years ago. Which would mean that our ancestors had been wearing clothes, of some kind, long before they left Africa, long before they ever came near a land as cold as this one.

So the two studies disagree, and the range between them is large. But both point to the same deeper truth. By the time of our night, eighteen thousand years ago, human beings had been wearing clothes for tens of thousands of years at the very least. Clothing was not a new invention to Ennu's family. It was as old and ordinary to them as language.

Now, let's set all this beside your own wardrobe. If you live somewhere cold, you might own a down jacket, filled with the fine feathers of ducks or geese, held in by a shell of woven nylon. You might have a fleece made of polyester, which is plastic spun into fibre. You might have thermal base layers, and a waterproof shell with taped seams, and boots with soles of rubber and linings of synthetic fur. All of it was designed, tested and made in factories, using materials invented in the last hundred years. And yet, if you look closely at how a modern cold-weather outfit works, it's remarkably like Haro's. Layers. Air trapped between them. A shell that keeps out the wind. A hood. Ruffs of fur, or fake fur, around the face. Mittens rather than gloves, because mittens keep the fingers together and warmer. People in the Arctic were dressing like this long before the scientists of the twentieth century worked out why it worked.

There's one more clever thing about Haro's clothes that's easy to miss. They're loose in some places, and tight in others, on purpose. Tight at the wrists, the ankles and around the face, where the cold would otherwise rush in. But loose around the body, so that there's room for warm air to gather, and so that when Haro is walking hard, or running, he can loosen the neck of his coat a little and let some of the heat and the damp of his body escape. Sweat is dangerous in the deep cold. If your inner layers get wet with sweat, and then you stop moving, the damp turns cold against your skin, and the cold goes into you fast. Experienced people in cold places learn to stay warm, but never too warm. Haro learned that before he was Pim's age, from his father, who learned it from his.

Siva has been watching you look at Haro's coat, and she smiles. She made that coat. She made both of them, the inner and the outer, last autumn, from the skins of four reindeer. And she made Tula's, and Pim's, and the little fur bag that Nia sleeps in, and most of the boots in the house. She will be making clothes, or mending them, for most of her waking life.

Making a fur coat from a reindeer is a very long job, and I want you to understand how long. First, the animal has to be skinned, carefully, so as not to tear or cut the hide. Then the skin has to be scraped, on the inside, to remove every scrap of fat and flesh, which is what Tula was doing earlier with her flint scraper. If any fat is left, the skin will rot. Then it has to be dried, and stretched, and worked, and softened, over many days, by bending and rubbing and chewing. Among many hunting peoples in recent centuries, the skins were often smoked too, over a smouldering fire, which made them more supple and better able to shed water. We can't know exactly which of these methods were used at Mezhyrich. But the hides had to be made soft somehow, or they would have been as stiff as a board in the cold.

And then comes the cutting, and the sewing. Pieces of hide, cut to shape with a flint blade, fitted together, and stitched along every seam with thread made of sinew, the tough, stringy tendons from an animal's legs and back, dried and split into fine fibres and twisted together. Thousands of stitches. Every one of them small and tight, so that the wind can't find its way through.

How do you make thousands of small, tight stitches through a reindeer hide?

That, in a little while, will bring us to the second of our three mysteries. Its time is not quite yet.

For now, Haro has taken off his outer coat, and laid it over a tusk in the wall to air, fur side out. He's sitting by the fire in his inner layer, warm at last. Mael has done the same. Pim has crawled across to sit in his father's lap, and Haro has wrapped him up in the loose front of his coat, so that only Pim's face is showing, round and pink in the firelight.

Inside the house, people don't need all their layers. It's warm enough, by the fire, in the fur of the inner coat. And the outer coats, damp from the breath and the frost of the day, need to dry, or they'll freeze stiff in the night. So they hang, on tusks and bones around the walls, like dark, shaggy figures watching over the room.

And Nia? Nia is in the warmest place in the house. She's lying in a soft bag of fur, a little like a modern baby's sleeping bag, with the fur turned inwards, against her skin, and her head in a tiny hood. Underneath her, there's a thick pad of dried grass and moss, which does two jobs at once: it keeps the cold of the ground away from her, and it soaks up whatever a baby without nappies needs to soak up, and can simply be pulled out and replaced in the morning. Many peoples of the far north, in recent centuries, have used moss in exactly this way. We can't prove that Siva did. But moss grew along the river here, and it's soft, and it's warm, and it's thrown away without a second thought, which is exactly what a tired mother of an eight-month-old would want.

Babies lose heat faster than adults, because they're so small. So Nia is never far from the fire, and never far from her mother, and at night, she will sleep pressed against Siva's body, inside the same furs, where it's warmest of all. That is the oldest and best way there is to keep a baby warm, and every family in this camp would know it without being told.

There's one more thing about clothes in this world that's worth knowing. A good fur coat is life. If a hunter's coat is torn and not mended, the wind gets in. If his boots get wet and freeze, his feet could freeze too. So every evening, in every house in this camp, by every fire, someone is looking over everyone's clothes. Checking the seams. Feeling for thin places in the fur. Looking for tears. And that someone, most of the time, is Siva.

She's doing it now. She has Mael's boots in her lap, and she's turning them over, slowly, feeling along the seams with her thumb. Near the heel of the left one, she finds something. A small split, where the stitches have pulled loose. Not big. But big enough. She sets the boot aside, by her knee, with a little nod, as if to say, I'll see to you later.

Mael pretends not to notice. He's nineteen. He thinks his boots are fine.

Outside, the frost is deepening. The air is so cold now that if you went out, the hairs in your nose would freeze on your first breath. But in here, by the hearth, the fire is glowing, and the clothes are drying, and the family is warm. And on the fire, half hidden among the embers, there is still that piece of bone, with its small, steady, yellow flame.

## Part Six — Around Eight O'Clock: Fat, Flame and the Grey Dog at the Door

Here is the small question for this part of the night. When the nearest trees are a thin line of willows by a frozen river, and every stick of wood is precious, how do you light a house through sixteen hours of winter darkness?

Because that's what this family faces. At this time of year, at this latitude, the sun is gone by about four in the afternoon and doesn't come back until about eight the next morning. Sixteen hours of night. In your own home, you'd hardly notice. You'd reach for a switch, and the room would fill with light, and you wouldn't think about it again. But here, every bit of light has to be made, by hand, from something that could have been used for something else.

The first and most important light is the fire itself. For most of the evening, the whole family lives inside the circle of its glow. Beyond the edges of that warm orange circle, the house fades into shadow, and the great pale shapes of the jawbones in the walls loom and fade, and then there's only dark.

But firelight flickers, and it doesn't reach into corners, and sometimes you need to see properly. Siva, for one, is going to need to see very well indeed, in a little while.

So Ennu reaches behind her, into the shadows by the wall, and brings out a lamp.

Imagine you are sitting right beside her as she sets it down on the floor in front of you. It's a flat, heavy stone, about the size of your hand, with a shallow hollow worn or carved into its upper surface, like a little dish. In the hollow there is a lump of pale, whitish fat, the fat from the horse they ate tonight, and a few scraps from the marrow bone. And lying in the fat is a wick: a little twist of dried moss, or perhaps a few fibres of plant stem. Ennu picks a glowing ember out of the fire with two slivers of bone, like tongs, and holds it to the wick. It smokes. It catches. And a small, clear, steady flame springs up, yellow and quiet, no bigger than the tip of your little finger. As the flame warms the fat, the fat melts into a little pool of clear oil, and the wick drinks it up, and the flame burns on.

That's the answer to our small question. You light the long winter dark with the fire, mostly. And when you need more, you burn fat. You make light out of the animals you've eaten.

Now, I must be honest about this lamp. As far as I know, no stone lamps have been reported from Mezhyrich itself, and I'm imagining this one. But we know that people of the Ice Age made and used lamps like it, because we've found them. The most famous come from far to the west of here, in France, from the painted cave of Lascaux, which was discovered in 1940 by four teenagers. Over a hundred lamps have been found at Lascaux, most of them simple, natural stones with a hollow in them. But one of them is extraordinary. In the early 1960s, the French priest and archaeologist André Glory, who spent years studying the cave, was excavating at the bottom of a deep part of the cave known as the Shaft, and he found a lamp made of red sandstone. It's beautifully shaped and polished, with a handle, like a spoon or a little frying pan, and it's decorated with engraved lines. It burned animal fat. And it was by the light of lamps like these that the painters of Lascaux worked, deep in the dark of the cave, far from any daylight, painting horses and aurochs and deer on the walls.

Let's stop and think about that light, compared to your own. A single modern light bulb gives off somewhere around eight hundred lumens. A small fat lamp like Ennu's gives off perhaps something similar to a candle, only a few lumens, and probably less. If you were sitting here tonight with one lamp and a low fire, you'd be living in a tiny fraction of the light you're used to. And yet you'd be able to see. Give the human eye twenty or thirty minutes in the dark, and it opens itself up like a flower, and a single small flame begins to feel like a lantern. The people of this camp spent so much of their lives in this kind of half-light that a modern room at night would probably have seemed to them almost painfully bright.

The fire, of course, is more precious than any lamp. And here's another small, true thing about it. In Ennu's world, a fire is not something you start lightly. Making fire from nothing, in the cold and the dark, is slow and difficult. One method used by people in the Upper Palaeolithic was to strike a piece of flint against a lump of a mineral containing iron, such as pyrite, so that a spark flew off and landed in a nest of dry tinder, which then had to be coaxed, very gently, with breath, into a flame. Another method, used by many peoples all over the world, is to spin one stick very fast against another, until the friction makes a glowing ember. Either way, it takes time, and skill, and dry tinder, and patience. In a world like this, you don't let your fire go out if you can possibly help it. And so the hearth in Ennu's house has been burning, or smouldering, or glowing, ever since the family came here at the beginning of the winter. It has hardly gone out once.

Think about how easy fire is for you. Somewhere in your home, there's probably a box of matches, or a lighter, or a gas hob that clicks and lights itself at the turn of a knob. You could make a flame in two seconds, in the dark, without thinking. The friction match was only invented about two hundred years ago. For every generation of human beings before that, for hundreds of thousands of years, fire was something you kept, and carried, and tended, and shared. When a family moved from one camp to another, they might carry glowing embers with them, wrapped in moss or fungus, or packed into a hollow of bone or horn, to start the new hearth. When a neighbour's fire went out, you gave them a coal from yours. A fire, in this world, was a little like a living thing that the family looked after together, and that looked after them.

And where does all the smoke go? Up. Through the gap at the top of the roof, where the hides are folded back a little, the smoke rises in a slow grey column and finds its way out into the night. Not all of it. Some hangs under the roof, in a soft, blue, drifting layer just above everyone's heads, and stings the eyes for a while, until you stop noticing it. The people here have lived with smoke all their lives. It's in their hair, their clothes, their furs, the hides of the walls. To them, the smell of smoke is simply the smell of home. And a little smoke is useful. It keeps away insects in summer, it helps to cure the hides, and it dries the meat that hangs from the tusks in the roof.

There's a danger in it too, of course. A house of hides and dry grass and fur, with an open fire in the middle of the floor. A spark that flies too high. A lamp knocked over in the dark. Ennu has watched sparks rise towards that roof every evening of her life, and she has never once stopped watching. When a log or a bone shifts, and a little fountain of sparks flies up, her eyes follow every one until it winks out. She does it without thinking, the way you might glance at a pan on the stove while you talk.

Pim, meanwhile, has crawled back to the doorway, and lifted the edge of the hide, and is looking out into the dark. And something outside is looking back at him.

Imagine you're lying beside him now, on your stomach, with your chin on your hands, looking out through the gap at the bottom of the hide. Outside, the snow is grey under the starlight. And just beyond the doorway, curled up in a hollow in the snow, there's an animal. It's grey, and thick-furred, about the size of a large dog, with a broad head and a long muzzle. Its eyes catch the firelight from the doorway and glow green for a moment. It lifts its head and looks at Pim. Pim looks at it. Then it lays its head down again on its paws, and sighs, and closes its eyes.

Is it a wolf? Or is it a dog?

That, believe it or not, is one of the most hotly argued questions in the whole study of the Ice Age.

Everyone agrees that dogs are descended from wolves. And everyone agrees that, at some point, somewhere, some wolves began to live close to human beings, and over many generations became tamer, and changed in body and behaviour, until they were dogs. But when did that happen? And where?

In 2009, the Belgian palaeontologist Mietje Germonpré and her colleagues published a study of ancient canid skulls, comparing their shapes. They argued that some very old skulls from Europe, including one from a cave in Belgium called Goyet, which is more than thirty thousand years old, came from what they called Palaeolithic dogs, animals that were already different from wolves in the shape of their skulls. And they linked these to two skulls found at a site called Eliseevichi, on the Russian Plain, not so very far from here, which they also identified as dogs. Later, others proposed that some canid remains from Mezhyrich itself might belong to early dogs too.

But in 2015, a team led by Abby Grace Drake published a study in the journal Scientific Reports that came to the opposite conclusion. Using a detailed three-dimensional method of measuring and comparing skull shapes, they examined the Goyet skull and one of the Eliseevichi skulls, and concluded that they were, in their words, "definitively wolves and not dogs." Germonpré and her colleagues have continued to publish evidence for early dogs, and the argument has gone back and forth ever since. Ancient DNA studies have added more evidence, and more questions. The identification of any Palaeolithic dogs at Mezhyrich has not been confirmed.

So, the honest answer is that we don't know whether the people of Mezhyrich had dogs. They might have had something in between: wolves that had learned to hang about the camp, eating scraps, tolerated by the people and tolerating them in turn, not yet dogs, but no longer quite wild. Tonight, I'm going to imagine exactly that. A grey animal that sleeps outside the door, that follows the hunters at a distance, that takes the bones that are thrown to it, and that barks, or howls, when something strange comes near in the dark. Pim calls it Smoke, because it's grey. The adults don't call it anything at all.

Smoke has already closed his eyes again. Pim lets the hide fall, and crawls back to the fire.

Now, while the lamp burns and the fire glows, Ennu leans forward and does something that she will do many times tonight. She takes a piece of bone from a small pile beside the hearth. It's broken, from a large animal, greasy, the kind of bone that's already been cracked for its marrow. And she lays it carefully across the embers, next to the piece that's already burning there. The new bone begins to smoke. Then a small yellow flame runs along its edge, and it hisses, very softly, and the sweet, scorched smell fills the house again.

Pim asks her, as children do, why she's burning a bone. Ennu says it's what bones are for. Pim says he thought bones were for walls. Ennu laughs, and tells him bones are for walls, and for fires, and for tools, and for soup, and for little boys who ask too many questions to chew on. Pim isn't sure if that's a joke. It is.

We'll come back to that, deep in the night, when it's the right time. For now, notice it. Notice that, in this house, on this night, the fire is fed with willow sticks and with bone.

If you could rise up now, above the roof of Ennu's house, and look down on the camp from the height of a bird, you'd see almost nothing. A few low, pale humps on a white terrace, and at the top of each, a faint red glow, where a smoke-hole leaks a little firelight into the night. Thin grey threads of smoke rising straight up, in the windless air, and spreading out slowly far overhead. And around them, in every direction, for as far as you could see and much further, nothing but snow and dark. Each of those small red glows is a fire like Ennu's, kept by a family like hers. Together, they're the only lights in the world for many days' walk.

Back by the hearth, Tula has moved closer to the lamp, and is watching her mother. Siva has picked up Mael's boot again. And beside her, on a scrap of soft hide, she has laid out a few small things. A fine, stiff bundle of dried sinew. A little flint awl, with a sharp point. And something very small, and very thin, and very pale, that catches the lamplight.

Siva picks it up between her finger and thumb, and holds it to the flame, and squints.

## Part Seven — Around Nine O'Clock: The Needle

The small question for this hour is about thread. In a world with no cotton, no wool from sheep, no spinning wheel and no shops, what do you sew with?

Siva is about to show us.

Imagine you are sitting right beside her now, so close that your shoulder is almost touching hers, in the little pool of yellow light from the fat lamp. The fire is warm on your face. Behind you, the house is dim and quiet. Across the hearth, Haro is half asleep against the wall, with Pim asleep in the front of his coat. Mael is sitting with his knees drawn up, watching his sister work on his boot, trying not to look guilty. Ennu is by the fire, of course, closest to it. And Tula has come to sit on Siva's other side, because she's eleven, and she's learning, and tonight her mother has promised to let her try.

In Siva's lap there's a small bundle of something pale and stringy. It looks a little like dried, shredded straw. It's sinew. Earlier in the winter, from the legs and back of a reindeer, Siva cut out the long, tough, silvery tendons, and dried them, and pounded them gently with a stone until they began to split apart into fibres. Now she takes a few of those fibres, and wets them in her mouth, and rolls them together against her thigh with the flat of her hand, quick and practised, until they twist into a single fine, strong thread. She does it again. And again. Within a minute she has a thread as long as her arm. As it dries, it will tighten a little, and grip.

That's the answer to our small question. On the mammoth steppe, you sew with the animal itself. Its tendons become your thread. Sinew is extraordinarily strong for its weight, and when it's wet it's soft and easy to work, and as it dries it shrinks a little, pulling the stitches tight. Arctic peoples in recent centuries have sewn with sinew for exactly these reasons, and the stitches can last for years.

But people of the Ice Age didn't only use sinew. We know, now, that they also made string, and cord, and even woven cloth, from plants. And the evidence for that comes from a place called Pavlov, in the Czech Republic, not far from the famous site of Dolní Věstonice, in a part of Moravia that, about twenty-six thousand years ago, was home to another community of mammoth hunters.

At Pavlov, archaeologists found small fragments of fired clay. And pressed into the surface of some of those fragments, preserved as though in a fossil, were impressions. Tiny, regular patterns. When the American archaeologists James Adovasio and Olga Soffer, working with the Czech archaeologist Bohuslav Klíma, examined them closely, they realised that these were the marks of woven fibres, of cords and textiles, pressed into wet clay before it was fired. In 1996, they published their findings in the journal Antiquity, under the title Upper Palaeolithic fibre technology: interlaced woven finds from Pavlov I, Czech Republic, c. 26,000 years ago. In later work, the team found dozens of these impressions, and described them, in press reports, as "the earliest evidence for cordage and textile production in the world." Adovasio suggested that the fibres might have come from plants such as nettle, or from the fibrous inner bark of trees like alder or yew, and that the impressions might come from finely woven baskets, bags or mats. Nobody knows exactly how they came to be pressed into the clay. Perhaps the people there used baskets as moulds. Perhaps they simply walked on mats laid over damp clay floors.

So the people of the Ice Age knew how to twist plant fibres into cord, and cord into nets and bags and woven cloth. And they knew how to make thread from sinew. Siva, tonight, is using sinew, because she's sewing hide, and sinew is best for hide. But in the corner of the house there's a net bag, made of twisted plant cord, hanging from a tusk, with dried roots in it. That's the bag Ennu took the roots from at supper.

Now Siva picks up the small, pale thing she set out on the hide earlier. She holds it up to the lamp, and you can see it properly at last.

It's a needle. It's made of bone, perhaps a little longer than your little finger, and as thin as a fine twig, smooth and polished from use, with a sharp point at one end. And at the other end, so small that you have to look twice to see it, there is a hole. An eye.

Siva takes the end of her new sinew thread, and moistens it again, and pinches it to a point, and threads it through the eye. Then she takes up Mael's boot, and finds the split near the heel, and begins to sew.

Watch her hands. First, she takes the little flint awl, and pierces the hide, just beside the split, making a tiny hole. Then she pushes the needle through that hole, and pulls the thread after it, and pulls it snug. Then another hole with the awl. Then the needle. Pull. Snug. Each stitch is small, and close to the last, and goes only part of the way through the thickness of the hide, so that the hole doesn't go right through and let in the water. It's slow. Painstaking. Beautiful to watch.

She hands it to Tula, and shows her where. Tula pierces the hide, and pushes the needle through, and pulls, and the thread tangles, and she sighs, and Siva untangles it, and shows her again. Tula tries again. This time it works. She looks up, with a small, fierce, pleased face.

Siva watches her, and doesn't take the work back. This is how it has always been learned. Not from a book, or a class, or a video, but by sitting beside someone who knows, in the lamplight, night after night, winter after winter, watching their hands. Siva learned from her own mother, on nights exactly like this one, and from Ennu, after she came to live in Ennu's family. Tula is learning from Siva. One day, if all goes well, Tula will sit by a fire with a daughter of her own, and show her where to put the awl, and untangle her thread for her, and say, again, try again.

Let's think for a moment about how much sewing a family like this one needs. Every person in the house needs an inner coat and an outer coat, trousers, boots, mittens and a hood, and children grow out of theirs every year or two. Boots wear through. Seams split. Mittens get lost. Hides get torn on the hunt. Among Arctic peoples in recent centuries, a single full set of winter clothing took many days of skilled work to make, and every family needed several. Multiply that by every family in the camp, and every winter, and you begin to see that, in a world like this, sewing is not a small household chore. It's one of the great industries of the whole society. It's work on which every single life depends. And the people who were best at it, the Sivas of the Ice Age, must have been among the most valued people in any camp.

Outside, in the other bone houses along the terrace, I imagine the same thing is happening tonight, by the light of other fires and other lamps. Women and girls, and some of the men too, bending over hides, piercing, stitching, pulling the thread tight. If you could walk along the terrace now and listen at the doorways, under the murmur of voices and the crackle of fires, you might hear it: the tiny, soft, regular sound of sinew being drawn through hide. Pull. Snug. Pull. Snug. All across the frozen plain, on this night and on thousands of nights like it, that small sound was keeping people alive.

And so we come to the second of our three mysteries.

I asked you, at the beginning of the night: why might a tiny sliver of bone, smaller than your little finger, with a hole at one end, have mattered more to this family's survival than any spear?

The answer is in Siva's hands. It's because of what the needle makes possible. Clothes that fit.

You can make a kind of clothing without a needle. You can wrap a hide around your body like a cloak, and tie it with a cord. Or you can pierce holes along the edges of two hides with an awl, and push a thong through, and lace them together, a bit like lacing a shoe. People did both of those things, for a very long time, and probably long before anyone ever made an eyed needle. But a cloak lets the wind in at every edge. A laced seam has gaps. And in a land as cold as this one, a gap is where the cold gets in, and the cold is what kills.

With an eyed needle, everything changes. The thread is carried through the hide in one smooth movement, attached to the needle, so that you can make many small, tight, close stitches, quickly. You can sew curved seams. You can shape a sleeve to fit an arm, and a hood to fit a head, and a boot to fit a foot, and join them all so closely that no wind can get through. You can make clothes in layers, inner and outer, each one fitted to the body underneath it, with warm air trapped in between. You can make the kind of clothing we saw on Haro earlier. Tailored clothing. And tailored clothing, in a place like this, in a winter like this, is the difference between life and death.

A spear can bring you food. But if you freeze on the walk home, the food won't help you. The needle keeps you alive long enough to use the spear.

That's our second mystery solved.

And we have a good deal of scientific evidence about how old these needles are. In 2024, a team led by the Australian archaeologist Ian Gilligan, of the University of Sydney, published a study in the journal Science Advances, looking at the evidence for early needles from Europe, the Middle East, Southeast Asia, southern Africa and Australia. They argued that eyed needles appear around forty thousand years ago, during a period when the last Ice Age was growing colder, and that they mark the arrival of fitted, or tailored, clothing. Gilligan was quoted in coverage of the study as saying: "Eyed needles made sewing more efficient and reflected the advent of fitted or tailored clothing."

But here, of course, there's a disagreement, and it has two parts.

The first part is about which needle is the oldest, and who made it. In 2016, archaeologists working in Denisova Cave, in the Altai Mountains of southern Siberia, found a bone needle about seven centimetres long, made from the bone of a bird. It was estimated to be around fifty thousand years old. That's older than any other eyed needle known from that time. But Denisova Cave is famous for having been lived in, at different times, by modern humans, by Neanderthals, and by a third group of humans, called the Denisovans, who were first identified from their DNA. So who made the Denisova needle? Was it modern humans, like Siva? Or Denisovans? Nobody can be sure. And there are other possible needles, from South Africa, which some have suggested might be even older.

The second part of the disagreement is about what the needles were for. Gilligan and his colleagues argued that the eyed needle was not only a practical tool. Some needles, they suggested, were very fine, too fine perhaps for sewing heavy hides, and might have been used for decoration: for sewing beads and ornaments onto clothes, or for fine embroidery. In their view, as clothes became necessary for warmth, they also became something else. A way of showing who you were. A way of looking beautiful. Not everyone agrees with every part of that argument, and it's still being discussed. But I find it a lovely thought: that from the very beginning, the needle was for warmth, and also for beauty.

Let's set this beside your own life. The clothes you're wearing now were almost certainly sewn by a machine, in a factory, perhaps on the other side of the world, by someone you will never meet. A modern industrial sewing machine can make thousands of stitches a minute. There's probably a little sewing kit somewhere in your home, with a few steel needles and spools of thread, and you probably use it once in a blue moon, to put a button back on. For Siva, the needle is one of the most valuable things she owns. If she loses it in the dark, among the furs on the floor, it will be a disaster. Making a new one takes many hours: cutting a sliver from a bone, grinding it down against a stone into a thin rod, polishing it, and then, hardest of all, drilling that tiny eye, slowly, with a sharp flint point, without splitting the bone. Many a needle must have broken in the making. And so she keeps hers very carefully, in a little case, perhaps a hollow bird bone, tied with a cord.

Siva finishes the last stitch on Mael's boot. She ties it off, and bites the thread with her teeth. Then she runs her thumb along the seam, feeling it. Tight. Smooth. Good.

She hands the boot back to Mael without a word. He takes it, and turns it over, and looks at the seam, and then at his sister. He says, quietly, that he thinks it was fine before. Siva gives him a look that every younger brother in history has received from every older sister. Mael grins, and sets the boot down by the fire to warm.

Tula has picked up a scrap of hide, and is practising, by herself, piercing and stitching, piercing and stitching, with her tongue between her teeth, in the lamplight.

And Siva puts the needle back in its little case, very carefully, and ties the cord, and tucks it away.

## Part Eight — Around Ten O'Clock: Amber, Shells and Faraway People

Here is the small question for this part of the night. In the whole of Ennu's life, how many people has she ever met?

It's a strange question, and nobody can answer it exactly. But by the end of this part, I think we'll have a rough idea. And it may surprise you.

The children are drowsy now. Pim is still asleep in the front of his father's coat, and Haro has laid him down gently on the furs beside the fire, and covered him over. Tula has put away her scrap of practice hide, and is lying on her side near her mother, with her eyes half closed, watching the flame of the lamp. Nia is awake again, in her mother's arms, playing with something that hangs on a cord around Siva's neck.

Imagine you're leaning in close, to see what it is.

It's a small string of ornaments. There are a few beads made of mammoth ivory, small and round and creamy white, each one drilled through the middle. There's a fox's tooth, with a hole bored through its root. And there are two things that don't belong to this landscape at all. One is a small seashell, pale and ribbed, pierced at one end. And the other is a little rounded lump of something golden-orange and translucent, that glows in the lamplight like a drop of honey. Nia has it in her fist and is trying to put it in her mouth.

It's amber.

At Mezhyrich, among the many things the archaeologists found, there were ornaments of amber and fossil shells. Neither comes from here. Amber is the fossilised resin of ancient trees, and it's found only in certain places, often far from here, where old forests were buried long ago. Fossil shells come from rocks that formed under ancient seas. Some writers on the site have estimated that these materials were carried to Mezhyrich from sources somewhere between three hundred and fifty and five hundred kilometres away. I'd treat those exact figures carefully, because I haven't been able to trace them back to the original excavation reports. But the basic point is not in doubt. People here wore things that came from very far away.

Amber has a few strange and lovely properties, and I like to think Siva knew all of them. It's light, so light that some kinds will float in salty water. It feels warm to the touch, never cold like stone. When it's held near a flame, it can give off a faint, resinous, piney scent, the smell of a forest that died millions of years ago. And if you rub it briskly against fur, it becomes charged with static electricity, so that it picks up tiny scraps of dry grass or ash, as if by magic. Thousands of years later, the ancient Greeks noticed this too, and their word for amber was elektron, which is where our words electric and electricity come from. So in a sense, the little honey-coloured bead on Siva's cord is the distant ancestor of every light switch in your home. Nia, of course, is only interested in whether it can be eaten.

Seashells, in particular, were used as ornaments all across Ice Age Europe. In Ukraine, archaeologists studying shell ornaments from the Upper Palaeolithic have found that two kinds were especially popular: small, pointed, cone-shaped shells of a kind called Nassa, and rounder, ribbed shells of the cockle family, called Cardium. And at another site, a cave called Mezmaiskaya, in the mountains of the north-western Caucasus, archaeologists have found shells from the Black Sea and other distant sources, a clear sign of people moving, and trading, and giving, over long distances.

So how did a little shell from a far-off sea, or a drop of amber from a distant forest, end up on a cord around Siva's neck, in a bone house on a frozen plain?

Almost certainly, nobody from Mezhyrich walked all that way to fetch it. Instead, it probably passed from hand to hand. From one group to another. A gift, perhaps, at a meeting between families in the summer. A token of friendship, or of marriage. Something given to a new bride by her mother, when she went to live with another band, and then given again to her own daughter. Over years, over generations, small precious things like these could travel hundreds of kilometres, a few at a time, from camp to camp, along chains of people who knew each other. A study of the wider region, published by archaeologists working on what they call the Mezinian landscape system, notes that the movements of human groups across this land can be traced partly through exactly these things: non-local materials, including amber and seashells, found far from where they began.

Think of it a little like a very slow postal service, with no post office, no roads and no addresses, which ran for thousands of years. A parcel sent today can cross a continent in a few days, tracked at every step. The amber on Siva's cord may have taken years, or a lifetime, to travel a few hundred kilometres. But the network that carried it was made of something that no modern postal service can quite match. Every single step of the journey was a meeting between people who knew each other's faces.

And those meetings mattered for more than beads. Small, scattered groups of people, living far apart, need each other. They need partners to marry from outside their own band, so that families stay healthy across generations. They need friends to turn to when the hunting fails, or the winter is too long, or a sickness comes. They need to share news: where the herds have gone, which rivers have flooded, where the flint is good. A gift of amber or shell was never only a gift. It was a promise. It said: we know each other, and if you ever need us, we'll remember.

Siva got this amber from Ennu, when Siva first came to live with this family. And Ennu got it from her own mother, who got it, she always said, from a man who came from the north-west in the summer of a very good year, a long time ago.

And that brings us back to our small question. How many people has Ennu ever met?

To answer it, we need to know how many people there were. And that's one of the hardest questions in the whole study of the Ice Age. It's also one of the places where scientists disagree most.

There is no census. There are no records. All that archaeologists have are the sites themselves, the places where people lived, and left their tools and bones and ash behind. So the scientists who study ancient populations try to work backwards: from the number of sites, and their size, and how long they were used, to the number of people who might have made them. And the results are startlingly small.

In 2019, two archaeologists from the University of Cologne, Isabell Schmidt and Andreas Zimmermann, published a study in the journal PLoS ONE estimating the population of western and central Europe during an earlier part of the Upper Palaeolithic, called the Aurignacian, between about forty-two thousand and thirty-three thousand years ago. Their best estimate, across that whole huge area, was about fifteen hundred people. With a lower limit of about eight hundred, and an upper limit of about three thousand three hundred. The most crowded region they found, south-western France, may have had around four hundred and forty people. Northern Spain, about two hundred and sixty. Belgium, about two hundred and ten.

Fifteen hundred people. In the whole of western and central Europe. Fewer people than live in a large modern village. Fewer than could fit on a single passenger ship.

Later, a team that included Schmidt, Zimmermann and their colleague Birgit Gehlen made a similar estimate for a much later period, from about fourteen thousand to eleven thousand six hundred years ago, just a few thousand years after our night. They came up with around six thousand six hundred people for Europe, with a range from about three thousand to about eleven thousand. And they suggested that even that was probably an overestimate.

But not everyone uses the same methods, or gets the same answers. Earlier, in 2005, the French demographer Jean-Pierre Bocquet-Appel and his colleagues had made their own estimates of Upper Palaeolithic populations, using a different approach, which combined the density of known sites with growth rates drawn from studies of recent hunter-gatherer peoples. Different methods give different answers, and every estimate depends on assumptions about how many people lived at each site, how long they stayed, and how many sites have been lost or never found. Some archaeologists think the true numbers might have been several times higher than the lowest estimates. Others think they really were that small. It's a lively argument, and it matters, because the size of a population affects everything, from how ideas spread to how well a group could survive a run of bad winters.

But whichever estimate you prefer, the picture is the same. The Ice Age world was almost empty of people. A few small bands, scattered across vast distances, each one perhaps a few dozen people, living in a land the size of a continent.

So now, our small question. How many people has Ennu ever met?

Perhaps a few hundred, over her whole life. The people of her own camp, thirty or forty of them, whom she knows as well as her own hands. The people of the other camps nearby, related by marriage and by blood, whom she meets at certain times of year, perhaps in summer, when several bands gather together by a river where the fishing is good, or where the herds pass. A few strangers, now and then, from further away. Perhaps a few hundred faces, in fifty years. And she would know the name, the family, and the story of nearly every one of them.

Let's set that beside your own life. The average person in a modern city might pass thousands of strangers in a single week, on the street, on trains, in shops. You might have hundreds of names in your phone, and hundreds more connections online, people you've never met in person. You might see, on a screen, in a single evening, more human faces than Ennu will see in her whole life. And yet, I wonder whether Ennu, with her few hundred people, each one known and named and remembered, might feel less alone than many of us do.

And here's one more place where we can see the faraway people, and how they cared for each other. It comes from a site called Sungir, near the city of Vladimir, in western Russia, about a thousand kilometres to the north-east of here. Sungir was discovered during clay digging in 1956, and it was excavated, from 1956 until 1977, by the Russian archaeologist Otto Bader. And there, Bader found burials. In one grave, two young people, adolescents, had been buried head to head. With them were more than ten thousand beads, made of mammoth ivory, which had once been sewn onto their clothing, and sixteen spears made of mammoth ivory too.

Ten thousand beads. Each one had to be cut, shaped, and drilled by hand. Estimates of how long that would have taken vary a great deal: some archaeologists have suggested around an hour for each bead, while others have suggested much less. But by any estimate, it was an enormous amount of work, many weeks or months of skilled labour. And it was given to two young people, and buried with them.

Think, for a moment, about what making one of those beads involves. First, a piece of mammoth tusk has to be cut, which is hard, because ivory is dense and tough. Then it's split and ground down into thin rods. Then each rod is cut into short pieces. Then each little piece has to be drilled through the middle, slowly, with a sharp flint point, turning and turning, without splitting it. Then the edges are rounded and smoothed and polished. Then the bead is sewn onto clothing, one at a time, with a needle and a fine sinew thread. Ten thousand times over.

As always, there are disagreements. Sungir is usually described as being around thirty to thirty-four thousand years old, though the different dates published over the years don't all agree. And in 2017, when the anthropologists Erik Trinkaus and Alexandra Buzhilova re-examined the skeletons, they and others discussed whether the two young people were boys, as they've usually been described, or whether, from skeletons that young, it's really possible to be sure. Some specialists doubt it.

What isn't in doubt is the love. Somebody, more than thirty thousand years ago, sat by a fire, night after night, making beads for those children. Perhaps someone like Siva. Perhaps someone like Tula, when she's grown.

Not everything people wore was meant to be useful. That's one of the most human things about the Ice Age. These were people who lived on the very edge of what a human body can survive, in a land where every scrap of fat and every hour of daylight mattered. And still they spent hours, and days, and months, making things that were simply beautiful. Beads. Pendants. Carved figures. Patterns scratched into bone. Red ochre on a skull. They could have spent that time on something else. They chose not to.

Nia has let go of the amber at last. She's yawning. Siva lifts her up and kisses the top of her head and lays her in her nest of fur, and tucks the furs around her. The little string of beads swings back against Siva's chest, and the amber glows, once, in the lamplight, like a small warm coal.

Outside, the stars are wheeling slowly over the frozen plain. And somewhere out there, hundreds of kilometres away in every direction, other families are sitting by other fires, in other houses of bone, or hide, or stone. A few small lights, scattered across an enormous dark.

## Part Nine — Around Eleven O'Clock: Songs, Stories and the Painted Skull

The small question for this hour is a gentle one. On a long winter night, with no books, no screens and no lights to speak of, what do people do for joy?

Let's listen, and find out.

The house has grown quieter. Pim is sound asleep on the furs, his mouth a little open, one arm flung out towards the fire. Tula's eyes have closed. Nia is drowsing in her nest. Mael is lying on his back with his hands behind his head, staring up at the curving bones of the roof, thinking whatever nineteen-year-old young men think about, which is probably the same thing they've always thought about. Haro is sitting against the wall, half awake. Siva has put away her sewing. And Ennu is by the fire, as always, closest to it.

And Ennu, very softly, begins to sing.

Not loudly. Not to anyone in particular. It's a tune with no beginning and no end, low and wandering, rising a little and falling again, the kind of song that has been passed down from mother to daughter for so long that nobody remembers where it came from. Her voice is thin and cracked and perfectly in tune. Siva, after a while, joins in, under her breath, a little lower. And Haro, against the wall, begins to tap the rhythm with two fingers on a bone beside him, very lightly, tap, tap, tap-tap, tap.

We can't know what they sang. Not a single note of Ice Age music has survived. Nobody wrote it down, and there was no way to record it. But we know that they made music, because we've found their instruments.

Imagine you're sitting on the other side of the fire from Ennu now, and listening. The song fills the little round room, and seems to soak into the bone walls. The fire crackles in time. Outside, very faintly, Smoke, the grey animal by the door, lifts his head and makes a small sound in his throat, as if he wants to join in, and then thinks better of it. And you notice, for the first time, something by the doorway, half in shadow. A great, rounded, pale shape. A mammoth skull, set on the ground near the entrance. And on its broad, domed top, there are marks. Red marks. A pattern of dots and lines, painted in something dark red.

At Mezhyrich, at the entrance to one of the bone houses, the archaeologists found exactly that: a mammoth skull, painted with a pattern of red ochre dots and lines. Ochre is a natural earth pigment, rich in iron, which can be ground into a powder and mixed with fat or water to make paint, and people have been using it for many tens of thousands of years. And the top of this skull, some researchers have noted, has depressions on it, worn places, which might have come from being struck, again and again. So some writers have called it a drum. One of the earliest percussion instruments ever found, they suggest. The red design, some have imagined, might even represent flames, or sparks of fire.

But I have to tell you honestly that this is interpretation, not fact. The marks on the skull might have come from use as a drum. Or from something else entirely. The painting might mean fire, or something else we can't begin to guess. We don't know. And nearby, at another site in Ukraine called Mezin, other painted mammoth bones were found, which some archaeologists in the twentieth century also interpreted as musical instruments, a kind of Ice Age orchestra of bone. Others have been doubtful. It's very hard to prove that something was used to make music, when all that's left is the object itself, and the music is gone.

Tonight, I'm going to let Ennu's family use their painted skull as a drum, sometimes, on special nights. Not tonight. Tonight is a quiet night. But on the night of a great feast, after a good hunt, perhaps, someone might sit by that skull and beat a slow rhythm on it with a mammoth bone, and the whole camp might sing.

What we can say for certain is that people of the Ice Age had musical instruments, and very old ones. And the most famous comes from a cave in southern Germany called Hohle Fels. In 2008, a team led by the archaeologist Nicholas Conard, of the University of Tübingen, found there the scattered pieces of a flute. It had been made from the wing bone of a griffon vulture, a very large bird, and it was found broken into twelve pieces, which the team carefully put back together. It had five finger holes, and a V-shaped notch at one end, where the player blew. Conard and his colleagues published their discovery in the journal Nature in 2009. The flute was at least thirty-five thousand years old, and perhaps closer to forty thousand. Conard was quoted in National Geographic calling it "unambiguously the oldest instrument in the world." The scientific paper itself was a little more cautious, describing it as likely the oldest recognisable musical instrument yet found. Pieces of flutes made from mammoth ivory were found in the same region too, which must have been much harder to make: the ivory had to be split, hollowed out, and then fitted back together again so that it was airtight.

And there's a detail about that flute that I love. Less than a metre away from where it was found, in the same layers of the cave, the same team found a small carved figure of a woman, made from mammoth ivory, which is now called the Venus of Hohle Fels. Music, and art, lying side by side in the ground for forty thousand years.

Let's set that beside your own home for a moment. You can probably play almost any piece of music ever recorded, in seconds, through a speaker or a pair of headphones, at any hour of the day or night. Millions of songs, from every country and every century, at the touch of a screen. Ennu's family has only the songs they carry in their own heads, and their own voices, and perhaps a flute, or a drum of bone. But every song they know, they know by heart. Every one of them has been sung to them by someone they love. And when Ennu sings tonight, the song is not coming from somewhere far away. It's coming from her.

And there are stories. When Ennu's song comes to an end, it's Haro who speaks next, quietly, so as not to wake the children. He's telling the story of the day. The hares. Mael's fox. The herd on the ridge. The wolves calling to each other in the dark. Siva asks him something, and he answers, and Mael adds something, and they laugh. And then Ennu tells a story of her own. It's an old one, one she's told many times, about a winter long ago when she was a girl, and the snow was so deep that the men had to dig a path from one bone house to the next. Or perhaps it's a different kind of story, about the beginning of the world, or about the animals, or about the stars. We can't know. But every people that has ever lived has told stories by the fire on winter nights. It's one of the oldest things we do.

People of the Ice Age made pictures too, of course, and some of the most beautiful pictures ever made by human hands. On the eighteenth of December, 1994, three cave explorers, Jean-Marie Chauvet, Éliette Brunel and Christian Hillaire, squeezed into a narrow opening in a limestone cliff in the Ardèche, in southern France, and found themselves in a series of great chambers whose walls were covered in paintings and drawings. Horses, with their heads raised and their mouths open, as if they were calling. Lions, hunting together. Woolly rhinoceroses, with their great curved horns. Mammoths. Bears. Owls. The cave is named after Jean-Marie Chauvet, and the scientists who have studied it believe that the oldest of its paintings were made around thirty-six thousand years ago, though, as they themselves say, the dates are still being refined as more samples are tested. In 2014, the cave was listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Nobody in Ennu's family has ever seen Chauvet. It lies far away, across the whole width of Europe, and it was already very ancient by the time of our night, older to them than the pyramids are to us. But I like to think that the same impulse lived in this house, too. The same wish to look at the animals that filled their world, and to make something of them, with charcoal and ochre and ivory and clay. Somewhere in this camp, perhaps, someone is carving a little figure tonight, by the light of a fat lamp. We'll never see it. But I'd be surprised if no one was.

Now let me tell you about another figure, and about a disagreement over what she was made of.

In Moravia, in what is now the Czech Republic, at the site of Dolní Věstonice, on the thirteenth of July 1925, an archaeological team working under the Czech archaeologist Karel Absolon found a small figure of a woman, broken in two pieces, lying in a layer of ash. She's about eleven centimetres tall, round and full-bodied, with a featureless face and two slanting slits for eyes. She's usually dated to somewhere between about thirty-one thousand and twenty-five thousand years ago. And she's not made of stone, or ivory, or bone. She's made of fired clay. She's one of the oldest known ceramic objects in the world, thousands of years older than the first pottery.

In 2004, scientists examining the figure with a scanner found a fingerprint pressed into her surface, fired into the clay. The researchers who studied it, Králík, Novotný and Oliva, concluded that it was the print of a child, perhaps aged between about seven and fifteen. They thought the child probably didn't make the figure, but had perhaps handled it, before it was fired. A child's fingertip, preserved for nearly thirty thousand years.

And here's the disagreement. Karel Absolon believed the figure was made from a mixture that included mammoth bone, crushed into powder and mixed with clay. That idea was repeated in books for decades. But in 2024, a team of researchers looked inside the figure using micro-computed tomography, a very fine kind of scan, and published their results in the Journal of Archaeological Science. They found that the material was made from loess, a fine, wind-blown sediment, with small stones, microfossils and grains of carbonate in it, and no sign of mammoth bone. So, nearly a century after she was found, the Venus of Dolní Věstonice was quietly given a new recipe.

What did these figures mean? That's a much bigger and older argument. People have suggested they were symbols of fertility, or of motherhood, or goddesses, or good-luck charms, or portraits, or toys, or that they meant different things in different places. I don't think anyone will ever be certain. But I like to think of them as people of the Ice Age showing us, across all that time, something that mattered to them. Human bodies. Human faces. Each other.

Haro's story, tonight, is about mammoths. About a herd he saw once, as a boy, crossing the frozen river in the spring, one great animal after another, testing the ice with their trunks before they stepped onto it, the young ones pressed close against their mothers' legs. He tells it slowly, the way you'd tell a story you've told many times before and love telling. Mael has heard it a dozen times. He listens anyway.

There's one more quiet thing about stories that I'd like you to think about. In a world with no writing, every single thing that anyone knows has to be carried in someone's memory, and passed on by mouth. Where the flint is good. Which plants are safe to eat and which will make you ill. How to read the ice on a river. How to set a snare, and skin a fox, and sew a boot. The names of the dead, and what they did. The stories of how the world began. All of it lives in the heads of people like Ennu, and it lives in the stories she tells. When an old person dies in a world like this, it's a little like a library burning. That's one reason, I think, why the oldest people in a camp like this one were so precious. Not because they were strong, but because they remembered.

Mael has fallen asleep at last. He's lying on his back, with his mouth open, and every so often he snores, quietly, and Siva looks at him and shakes her head and smiles.

Haro's fingers have stopped tapping. His head has tipped back against the bone wall. His eyes are closed.

Siva lies down beside Nia, and pulls the furs over the two of them. Nia nestles in against her mother's body, warm and small. And Siva, too, closes her eyes.

Tula stirs, and mumbles something, and Siva reaches over and smooths her hair, without opening her eyes, and Tula settles again. Pim, in his sleep, has kicked his furs away, and Haro, half awake, leans over and pulls them back over him, and tucks them in around his feet. These are the small, unthinking kindnesses of a family at night, and they haven't changed in eighteen thousand years. If you've ever got up in the night to pull a blanket back over a sleeping child, you've done exactly what Haro is doing now.

Only Ennu is awake now. She sits by the fire, cross-legged, with a fur around her shoulders, humming the last few notes of her song, so softly that nobody could hear them but you. She looks around the house at each of them in turn. Pim, sprawled on the furs. Tula, curled on her side. Nia and Siva, wrapped together. Mael, snoring. Haro, asleep against the wall.

All here. All warm. All safe.

Then she reaches out, and lays another piece of bone on the fire.

## Part Ten — Around Midnight: The Keeper of the Fire

Here is the small question for this part of the night. When everyone in the house is asleep, who keeps the fire alive?

You already know the answer, I think. But let's sit with her for a while, and watch.

It's around midnight, or near enough, though of course nobody here has any idea what midnight is. To Ennu, it's simply the deep middle of the night, the time when the cold is settling in hardest, and the stars have wheeled round to a new place in the sky, and the fire needs her.

Imagine you're sitting beside her now, cross-legged, on the warm furs right next to the hearth. You have a fur over your shoulders too, and the heat of the embers is on your face and your hands and your knees, while your back, towards the room, feels the cool air of the house. The lamp has gone out. Ennu let it burn down when the sewing was done, to save the fat. So the only light now is the fire, low and red, a heap of glowing embers with a few small flames, giving off just enough light to see the faces of the sleepers nearest to it, and the pale curve of the jawbones in the wall behind them, and nothing more. The rest of the house is dark.

And it's quiet. So quiet. You can hear everyone breathing. Pim's quick, light breaths. Tula's slow, soft ones. Mael's snore. Haro's deep, even breathing against the wall. Siva and Nia, wrapped together, breathing almost in time. And outside, very faintly, the creak of the frost on the hides of the roof, and once, the sigh of Smoke turning over in his hollow in the snow.

Ennu doesn't sleep much at night, in winter. She hasn't for years. She'll doze, sitting up, for a while, and then wake, and look at the fire, and feed it, and doze again. Many old people, even today, find that they sleep less deeply and wake more often in the night, and Ennu is the oldest person in the house. But it's more than that. It's her job. Somebody has to keep the fire alive through the night, and in this family, that somebody has been Ennu for a very long time.

That's the answer to our small question. The keeper of the fire is the grandmother. And I don't think that's a coincidence. In many hunting and gathering peoples whom anthropologists have studied in recent times, grandmothers have been some of the most important people in the whole community, gathering food, caring for children, and passing on knowledge. Some anthropologists, above all the American anthropologist Kristen Hawkes, have even argued that grandmothers played a central part in human evolution itself, by helping their daughters raise more children than they could have raised alone. That's an idea that's been much debated, and I won't pretend it's settled. But sitting here, beside Ennu, in the dark, I find it easy to believe.

Across the house, Nia stirs. A small, creaking, snuffling sound, the sound of a baby who is waking up and is not at all sure she approves. Siva is awake at once, the way mothers always are. She doesn't sit up. She just shifts a little, inside the furs, and brings the baby to her breast, and the snuffling stops, and turns into a small, contented, rhythmic sound. And in a few minutes, mother and baby are both asleep again. Ennu watches them, and smiles, and turns back to the fire.

Now let me tell you something about these people that we have only learned in the last few years, and that I find quietly astonishing. We know something about who they were, and who their descendants were, from their DNA.

In 2023, a large team of scientists led by Cosimo Posth, of the University of Tübingen, published a study in the journal Nature. They had analysed the genomes, the complete genetic information, of three hundred and fifty-six ancient hunter-gatherers, a hundred and sixteen of them newly sequenced, from fourteen countries across western and central Eurasia, who lived between about thirty-five thousand and five thousand years ago. And from all that DNA, they were able to trace how groups of people moved, and mixed, and replaced one another, across Europe, over thirty thousand years.

They found that people linked to the Epigravettian, the same tradition as the people of Mezhyrich, had a long and important story. In southern Europe, around the coldest part of the Ice Age, the study found evidence that one population had replaced another. And then, from at least fourteen thousand years ago, after our night, as the ice began to retreat, people carrying Epigravettian-related ancestry spread out from the south across the rest of Europe, largely replacing the people who had lived in the west before. In other words, the broad family of peoples to which our camp belongs became the ancestors of many of the hunter-gatherers who lived in Europe in the thousands of years that followed. Which means that, very probably, some of the people living in Europe today still carry a little of that ancient ancestry.

And here's the gentle part of it. Long before our night, in this same part of the world, there were other people, too. Neanderthals. A different kind of human, with their own long history, who had lived in Europe and western Asia for hundreds of thousands of years. By the time of Ennu's family, they had been gone for more than twenty thousand years. But they didn't vanish completely. Modern humans and Neanderthals met, long ago, and sometimes had children together, and so, today, most people whose ancestors came from outside Africa carry a small share of Neanderthal DNA, usually around one or two per cent. Ennu, almost certainly, carried a little too. So do many of you, listening to this. That's a story for another night.

Let's set all this beside your own life, very gently. If you've ever sent off a little tube of saliva to a company that tells you where your ancestors came from, you've used the same kind of science that Posth and his colleagues used, in a much simpler form. Somewhere in that report there might be a line about ancient hunter-gatherers. Those are, in a sense, Ennu's people. Not her in particular, of course. Nobody can trace their family tree back eighteen thousand years by name. But her people. Her world.

Ennu gets up, slowly, one stiff joint at a time, and goes to the doorway. She lifts the edge of the hide, just a little, and looks out.

Imagine you're crouching beside her, looking out under the hide. The cold comes in at once, like water, around your face and hands. Outside, the terrace is grey and silver under the stars. The other bone houses along the terrace are low, dark humps, with the faintest red glow at the top of one of them, where another fire is being kept, by another grandmother, perhaps, in another family. Beyond them, the land falls away to the river, where the willows are black, and the ice is a pale, still ribbon in the dark. And beyond that, the plain runs away to the horizon, white and empty and silent, under a sky so full of stars that it seems to crackle.

Smoke, in his hollow in the snow, opens one eye and looks at Ennu, and closes it again. His fur is white with frost along his back. He doesn't seem to mind.

Ennu breathes in the cold air. She looks at the sky, at where the bright stars have moved to since dusk, and she knows, in her own way, how much of the night has gone and how much is left. More than half is still to come. Then she lets the hide fall back, and comes back to the fire, and sits down again, and holds her hands out to the embers to warm them.

She is thinking, perhaps, about other winters. About the winter she was Tula's age, when her own grandmother kept the fire, and Ennu lay awake in the furs and watched her, just as Tula sometimes watches her now. About the winter Haro was born. About the winters that were hard, when the pits ran low before the spring came, and everyone was thin, and the children cried with hunger. And about the winters that were good, like this one, so far, with the pits still full and the family well. She has lived through fifty of them. She knows that this one, like all the others, will end. The days will lengthen. The ice on the river will crack and groan and break. The geese will come back. And the family will leave this bone house, and move on, following the herds, as they always do, and perhaps come back to it next winter, and perhaps not.

There's something about the deep middle of the night that makes people think like this. Ennu has sat here, on nights like this, thousands of times. And on every one of them, the fire has been her company.

Let me tell you a little about that hearth, before she feeds it again, because by now you know it almost as well as she does. It's a shallow scoop in the earth floor, about as wide as a large dish, lined with layer upon layer of fine grey ash, the ash of every fire that has burned in this house since the family came here in the autumn. Around its edges, there are a few flat stones and pieces of bone, blackened and cracked by the heat, which hold the warmth for a long time after the flames have died down. If you lay your hand flat on one of them now, it would be warm, like a cat that has been lying in the sun. And underneath the ash, there are embers, glowing very slowly, keeping the heat of the evening folded up small and safe.

The fire has burned down a little. The embers are dimmer, and the small flames have gone. Ennu reaches to her side, to the little pile of fuel she gathered in the evening. There are a few sticks of willow left there, thin and dry, and a few pieces of birch twig. And beside them, there is a heap of bones. Broken bones, cracked open for their marrow at supper, still greasy inside. A piece of a leg bone. A few vertebrae. The end of a rib.

She takes a stick of willow, and snaps it, and lays it on the embers. Then, carefully, she lays a piece of the greasy bone across it.

The willow catches first, with a small bright flame. Then the bone begins to smoke, and to hiss, and then a flame runs along its edge, yellowish and steady, and the sweet, scorched smell rises into the house again. The light comes back. The faces of the sleepers glow. Ennu sits back on her heels, and watches it burn.

And so we come, at last, to the third of our three mysteries.

I asked you, at the very beginning of the night: on the coldest night of the year, when the fire is all that stands between this family and the frost, why would the grandmother of the house feed it not with wood, but with bones?

The first part of the answer is simple. Wood is scarce. On the open steppe, around this camp, there are almost no trees. In the hearths at Mezhyrich, when Laurent Marquer and his colleagues studied the charcoal in 2012, they identified wood from birch and willow, trees that grew in sheltered patches along the riverbanks, in a landscape that was mostly open steppe. So there was some wood. But not much. And what there was would have been gathered, stick by stick, in the short days of winter, by people like Ennu, walking down to the river and back. Every armful mattered.

The second part of the answer is that bone, in a land full of mammoths and horses and reindeer, is everywhere. And greasy bone burns. Not like wood, exactly. Bone on its own is hard to light, and it doesn't burn well by itself. But a bone that still holds grease and marrow inside, laid on a bed of hot embers with a little wood to get it going, will burn with a slow, steady flame for a long time, as the fat inside it melts and feeds the fire. It's a little like a fat lamp, turned inside out. The bone is the lamp, and the marrow is the oil.

And here is the third part, which is where the scientists come in, and where they have disagreed. For a long time, it was said that the hearths at the mammoth-bone sites had almost no charcoal in them at all, as if the people there had burned nothing but bone. But when Marquer and his colleagues looked very closely at the hearths of Mezhyrich, with fine sieves and microscopes, they found that there was wood charcoal there after all. Much of it had simply crumbled away, or been lost, over the thousands of years, or with the usual ways of digging. And they suggested that adding bones to the hearths might itself have helped break down the charcoal, so that less of it survived. In their reading, the people of Mezhyrich were managing their fuel carefully, burning wood and bone together, with the bone helping to make up for how little wood they had. And at Kostenki, Alexander Pryor's team found burned wood too, inside the great bone circle, showing that people there burned wood as well as bone.

So the answer to our third mystery is this. Ennu feeds the fire with bone because, in a land with very few trees, a greasy bone is one of the best and most plentiful fuels there is. She uses a little wood to start the flame, and bone to keep it going through the long night. Nothing is wasted. The mammoth that gave its bones for the walls, and the horse that gave its meat for supper, give their bones once more, for warmth.

Ennu watches the bone burn, and nods, and pulls her fur a little closer around her shoulders. The fire is safe. It will last a while now.

## Part Eleven — Around Two in the Morning: The Deep Sleep of the Bone House

Here is a small, gentle question for this hour of the night. Now that the fire is fed and the house is quiet, who will be the last to fall asleep?

Let's find out together, slowly.

It's somewhere around two in the morning. The bone that Ennu laid on the fire is burning low and steady, a small yellow flame running along its edge, giving off a soft, warm light and a faint, sweet smell. The cold outside is at its deepest now, or nearly. But in here, among the furs, it's warm enough.

Imagine that you're lying down now, on the furs near the hearth, between Tula and the fire. Pull a heavy reindeer skin up over yourself, fur side down, so that the soft hair is against you. It's warm from the fire, and it smells of smoke, and of animal, and of the people who have slept under it before you. Beneath you, there are more skins, and under those, a thick layer of dried grass, which crackles faintly whenever you move and then is still. Your feet, which got cold when you went to the doorway with Ennu, are warming again now, slowly, the warmth creeping back into your toes.

Look up. Above you, the bones of the roof curve over, pale in the low light, and between them, the dark hides. Through the gap at the very top, where the smoke goes out, you can see a small, ragged patch of black sky, and in it, three stars.

And everything is very, very quiet.

Pim wakes first, briefly. He sits up, all at once, the way small children do, and looks around, blinking, and whispers that he needs to go out. Haro, half asleep, gets up, and takes him to the doorway, and lifts the hide, and Pim steps out into the snow in his boots and his little fur coat, and back again a moment later, gasping at the cold, and dives back under the furs. Haro tucks him in. And Pim, being six, is asleep again almost before his head is down. He lies curled on his side, with his knees drawn up and one hand under his cheek, breathing lightly and quickly, dreaming whatever six-year-olds dreamed of eighteen thousand years ago. The fox, perhaps. Or Smoke. Or the white hares.

Pim is asleep.

Here's a small, gentle fact about this camp, while the house settles. When the Leiden team chose the bones to date, the bones of foxes and hares and wolves, they were looking for small, short-lived animals that had clearly been brought into the camp while people were living there. And in doing that, without meaning to, they reached right into the everyday life of the place. A hare caught in a snare. A fox brought home on a cold evening, like Mael's. Those little bones, dated with such care in a laboratory in the twenty-first century, are the leftovers of suppers and of fur coats, of days just like the one we've just watched.

Tula goes next. She's lying on her back, with her eyes open, looking up at the three stars in the smoke-hole. She's thinking about the needle. About how the thread tangled, and then didn't. About how her mother didn't take the work away from her. About the fox, and what its fur might become. Her thoughts get slower, and slower, and more and more muddled, the way thoughts do at the edge of sleep, until the needle and the fox and the stars are all tangled up together into something soft that doesn't make sense any more. Her eyelids close. Her breathing deepens.

Tula is asleep.

Let's think for a moment about how you fall asleep, in your own home. Perhaps there's a fan humming, or a heater ticking, or the faint sound of traffic outside. Perhaps you listen to something, a podcast, or rain sounds, or, perhaps, a calm voice telling a long, slow story about a family in a house of bones. All of these are, in a way, trying to give us something that the people in this house never had to think about. They had the sound of other people breathing. The crackle of a fire. The creak of frost on the roof. The sigh of an animal outside the door. These are some of the oldest sleep sounds there are.

Let me tell you one small, comfortable thing about the furs these children are sleeping under, while the house goes quiet. Each hair of a reindeer's winter coat, as I mentioned earlier, is hollow, a tiny tube filled with air. And still air is one of the best insulators there is. So under a reindeer skin, you're lying beneath millions of tiny pockets of warm, still air, all holding your own warmth close to you. It's exactly what the down in a modern duvet does, or the fibres in a sleeping bag. The family's bedding was made by the reindeer itself, over a whole summer and autumn of growing its winter coat, and then by Siva's hands, in the scraping and softening and sewing. Under those skins, on a night like this, a body can stay warm right through until morning.

Mael turns over, and stops snoring, and mutters something in his sleep, and is quiet. He never really woke.

Haro, back by the wall, has lain down at last, properly, on his side, with one arm over Pim. He is thinking about the herd on the ridge, and whether they'll still be there in the morning. And then about Mael's boot, by the fire, and the seam that Siva mended. And then about the warmth of the furs, and how good it is to lie here, in the dark, with the work of the day done, and everyone he loves within reach of his arm. He lets out a long, deep breath.

Haro is asleep.

And here is a quiet scholarly puzzle, of the gentle kind, to keep you company while the house goes still. How long did families like this one actually stay at Mezhyrich? The old encyclopedia entries describe it as a winter settlement. And the Leiden team found that their dates fitted well with a single, fairly short period of occupation, though they were careful to say that they couldn't rule out people returning to the same place, again and again, over a few centuries at most. Some of the newspaper stories written about their work described the camp as a short stay rather than a long-lived village. The scientists themselves were more careful, and called for more samples, from more of the pits and houses, to be sure. So we don't know whether Ennu's family came here every winter of her life, or only for a few. It's an unhurried kind of mystery. It can wait.

Nia stirs again, in the furs against her mother's body. Not hungry, this time. Just stirring, the way babies do. Siva, without waking, puts a hand on her back, and pats it, slowly, three times. Nia sighs, a long, shuddering baby sigh, and her little body goes completely limp.

Nia is truly asleep.

And Siva, beside her, has her eyes closed, and her breathing is slow and even. Her needle is safe in its case. Her family is warm. The boots are mended. She drifts down into sleep the way a feather drifts down onto snow.

Siva is asleep.

Now there is only Ennu.

She sits by the fire for a little while longer. She looks at the bone burning there, and decides it will last. Then she does one last thing, the thing she does every night before she sleeps. She takes a flat piece of bone, like a little shovel, and draws the glowing embers together into a tight heap in the middle of the hearth, around the burning bone. And then, gently, she scoops soft grey ash from the edges of the hearth and lays it over the top, not smothering the fire, but covering it, the way you might pull a blanket over a sleeping child. Under that ash, the embers will go on glowing, very slowly, through the rest of the night. They'll give off a gentle warmth through the hearth stones. And in the morning, they'll still be there, waiting.

The light in the house goes down to almost nothing. A faint red glow, here and there, through the ash. That's all.

Ennu lies down, on her side, on the furs closest to the fire, facing it, the way she always does. She pulls her reindeer skin up over her shoulder. She looks one last time at the faint red glow in the ash. It's there. It's always there.

Imagine you're lying there too, in the dark, a guest in this house of bone on this one night out of all the nights in history. The furs are heavy and warm. The grass beneath you smells faintly of summer. The smoke smells of willow and bone. The air on your face is cool, but the rest of you is warm, wrapped up and held close in a nest of fur and grass and other people's warmth. Your breathing slows. It begins to match theirs. In, and out. In, and out.

Ennu's eyes close.

And Ennu is asleep.

The whole house is sleeping now. Seven people, and a grey animal outside the door. And the fire, under its blanket of ash, sleeping too, slowly breathing, faintly glowing, keeping the warmth of the evening folded up small and safe at its heart.

Outside, the night goes on without them, in its own slow way. The stars turn, very slowly, over the frozen plain. The frost grows on the hides of the roof, tiny white feathers of ice on every hair, so that by morning the bone houses will look as though they've been dusted with sugar. Down by the river, the ice is thickening, a fraction at a time, with small creaks and pings that nobody hears. The willows stand black and still. Far out on the plain, the herd on the ridge has lain down in the snow, great dark shapes, their long hair frosted white, their breath rising in slow clouds, the young ones pressed close against their mothers. An owl drifts low over the terrace, soundless, its wings spread wide, and is gone.

And far away, in every direction, across the whole wide width of the Ice Age world, other people are sleeping too. To the west, across the plains and the forests and the mountains, in the river valleys of what will one day be France and Spain, there are families sleeping in rock shelters and in tents of hide, beside fires banked with ash, much like this one. To the south, by the shores of a sea, there are people who have never seen a mammoth, sleeping to the sound of waves. To the east, across the endless steppe, there are families in houses of bone and hide like Ennu's, and beyond them, people in Siberia, wrapped in furs, perhaps carving little ivory figures of their children by the light of their fires. None of them know each other. Most of them will never meet. But all of them, on this night, are doing the same thing. Lying close together, in the dark, around a fire that someone has covered, trusting that it will still be alive in the morning.

In a few months, the spring will come to this valley. The days will lengthen, a little at a time. The snow will soften, and then melt, and run down to the river in a thousand little streams. The ice will crack and groan and break, and go grinding away downstream. The geese will come back, in great wavering lines, calling overhead. The first green will show on the steppe, and the herds will move north to find it, and the family will pack up their furs and their tools and their needles and their amber, and carry a few live embers from this hearth, and leave the house of bone standing empty on its terrace above the river, until the winter brings them back again. But that's all a long way off. Tonight, there's only the dark, and the warmth, and the sleep.

If you could rise up now, above the camp, and look down on it from high in the frosty air, you'd see almost nothing. A few pale humps on a white terrace. A faint red glow at the top of one of them, and then not even that, as the ash settles over the fire. You'd hardly know anyone lived there at all. And yet, under every one of those roofs of bone and hide, there are people breathing, and fires breathing, all through the long cold dark. A few small, warm, living worlds, tucked into the fold of a river valley, at the edge of the great white steppe, waiting for the morning.

And above them, very slowly, the stars turn.

## Part Twelve — Around Half Past Seven in the Morning: The Fire Wakes

The last small question of our night is the simplest of all. On a frozen winter morning, in a house made of bones, what is the very first thing anybody does?

Let's wait and see.

The long dark is ending. It's around half past seven in the morning, by our clocks, and the sun won't lift over the edge of the plain until nearly eight. But already, something has changed. The little patch of sky in the smoke-hole, which was black all night and full of stars, has turned a soft, deep blue, and then a paler, greyer blue, and the stars in it have gone out, one by one. Around the edge of the hide over the doorway, there's a thin line of grey light. And outside, very faintly, there are sounds. A raven, somewhere down by the river, calling its deep, croaking call. The creak of the frost. And Smoke, outside the door, getting up, and shaking the night's frost off his fur, and yawning a long, squeaky yawn.

Inside the house, everyone is still asleep. Pim is sprawled across the furs now, having kicked half of them off himself and onto his sister. Tula is curled up tight under what's left. Mael has his arm over his face. Haro is on his back, breathing slowly. Siva and Nia are wrapped together, Nia's small face just visible among the furs, pink-cheeked, one fist pressed against her mouth.

And Ennu opens her eyes.

She doesn't need the light to wake her. She has woken at this grey hour every winter morning for as long as she can remember. She lies still for a moment, listening, the way she did in the night. Everyone's breathing. Everyone's here. Then she pushes back her reindeer skin, and the cool air of the house touches her face like a hand, and she sits up, slowly, one stiff joint at a time, and pulls her fur around her shoulders, and turns to the hearth.

That's the answer to our last small question. The very first thing anybody does, on a winter morning in a house like this, is wake the fire.

Imagine you're kneeling beside her now, by the hearth, in the grey half-light. The flat stones around the edge of it are still faintly warm under your hands, after all these hours. In front of you is the low mound of soft grey ash, exactly where Ennu laid it down in the night. She takes the flat piece of bone, her little shovel, and very gently, the way you might brush snow off something precious, she draws the ash back from the top of the mound.

And there they are.

At the heart of the ash, still glowing, are the embers. A small heap of them, not much bigger than two cupped hands. Dull red at the edges, and in the centre, where the air reaches them now for the first time in hours, slowly brightening. Orange. Then gold. And among them, the last blackened, crumbling end of the bone she laid there in the middle of the night, still faintly glowing too. They've been alive all night. Through the deep cold, through the turning of the stars, through every breath of every sleeper in this house. Waiting for this.

Ennu reaches for a pinch of dry grass from the little store by the wall, and a few thin twigs of willow. She lays the grass gently on the embers. Then she leans down close, so close that you can feel the heat on her face, and on yours, and she blows. A long, slow, steady breath.

The embers brighten. The grass begins to smoke, a thin white curl of it. She blows again. The smoke thickens, and curls up towards the smoke-hole. She blows a third time. And then, with a soft sound, almost like a sigh, the grass bursts into flame.

It's a small flame at first, yellow and flickering. Ennu feeds it the willow twigs, one by one, and the twigs catch, and crackle. Then a thicker stick. Then a piece of greasy bone from last night's supper, laid carefully across the flames. It smokes, and hisses, and catches. And within a minute or two, the fire is burning again in the middle of the floor, just as it was at dusk last night, low and orange and steady. Its light moves on the curving jawbones of the walls, and on the hides of the roof, and on the faces of the sleeping children. And the sweet, scorched smell of burning bone rises once more into the house.

The fire is awake. And, one by one, so is the family.

Siva first, because Nia has woken, and is announcing it. Then Pim, who sits bolt upright and asks if it's morning, and whether Smoke is still there, and whether he can have one of the white hares. Then Tula, who tells him of course it's morning, and to give her back the furs. Then Mael, groaning. Then Haro, who sits up, and rubs his face, and looks at the fire, and looks at his mother, and nods, as if to say: there. We've done it again. Another night.

And Ennu, kneeling by the hearth with the firelight on her face, nods back.

Breakfast is simple. The last of last night's horse meat, which Ennu set aside near the hearth, where it stayed cool but didn't freeze, goes back on the embers to warm. There's a cracked marrow bone, saved for the morning, and the children share it, scooping the soft fat out with slivers of bone, and passing it from hand to hand. There are a few more of the dried roots from the net bag. And there's water, made from snow that Tula fetched from just outside the door and packed into a hide bag hung near the fire, where it has slowly melted in the night. It tastes of smoke and a little of hide. Pim drinks so much of it that he gets the hiccups, and everyone laughs at him, and he laughs too, and hiccups again.

Siva has taken out her little needle case, and opened it, and looked inside, just to be sure. The needle is there, pale and smooth and safe. She closes the case, and ties the cord, and tucks it away again, with a small private nod. Today, there's the fox to skin, and the hares, and a pair of mittens for Pim that are wearing thin at the thumbs. There will be plenty of sewing.

Mael is already at the doorway, pulling on his boots, the right one first, and then the left, with its newly mended heel. He pushes his foot down into it, and wriggles his toes, and says nothing at all. But when he thinks nobody is looking, he runs his thumb along the seam, just as his sister did last night. Siva sees him. She doesn't say anything either. She doesn't need to.

Let's set this beside your own morning, one last time. Perhaps your heating comes on by itself, on a timer, so that the house is warm before you wake. Perhaps your kettle boils at the press of a switch. You might wake to an alarm, or to the light of your phone, and walk into a kitchen that's already warm, and never once think about where the warmth came from. Ennu's morning has none of that. But she has something we've mostly lost. She has the knowledge, in her hands, of how to keep a small fire alive through the longest night of the winter, on a plain with almost no trees, with willow sticks and bones and ash, and how to wake it, with her own breath, in the morning.

There's a small, friendly disagreement I'll leave you with, as the house wakes up, because it's the gentle kind, and it's about the animals that built these walls. Why did the woolly mammoths eventually disappear? For a long time, scientists have argued about it. Some have pointed to the changing climate, as the Ice Age came to an end, and the great dry, grassy mammoth steppe gave way to forests and wetlands, where mammoths could no longer find enough of the food they needed. Others have pointed to people, and to hunting. Many now think it was probably some of both, working together, over thousands of years. But here's the part I find lovely. The mammoths didn't vanish all at once. On a few remote islands far to the north, small populations lived on for many thousands of years after our night, long after they had gone from this plain. On the night we've spent here, though, they are everywhere: in the walls, in the hearth, in the beads on Siva's cord, and out there on the ridge, lying in the snow, waiting for the sun.

The morning is coming on properly now. Siva is sitting by the fire, feeding Nia. Tula is folding the furs and laying them over the bones of the wall to air. Pim has gone to the doorway with his father, and lifted the hide, and is looking out.

Haro pulls on his boots, stuffed with fresh dry grass from the store by the wall, warm from where they've stood all night beside the hearth, and his outer coat, the one Siva made, fur side out, and his hood, and his mittens, on their cord around his neck. And he ducks out through the low doorway. And the morning comes in. A blast of bright, bitter cold, and with it, a flood of pale gold light. Because the sun, at last, is lifting over the edge of the plain.

Outside, the whole world is white with frost. Every hide on every roof, every tuft of grass, every willow twig down by the river, is furred with tiny feathers of ice, glittering in the low sun. The river is a ribbon of white. Smoke is standing in the snow, wagging his tail, or perhaps just stretching, in the way that wolves and dogs both do. The bone houses along the terrace are gold on one side and blue on the other. And from the smoke-hole of every house, one by one, as the other families wake their fires too, thin threads of grey smoke are rising, straight up, into the cold, clear, windless air of the morning.

Out on the ridge to the north, the herd is getting to its feet. Great dark shapes against the sunrise, shaking the frost from their long hair. Haro shades his eyes and looks at them for a long time.

And inside, the fire burns on. In the middle of the floor of the house of bones, in its shallow scoop of ash, just as it did at dusk last night, just as it will tonight, and tomorrow night, and through all the nights of this long winter. The fire that was fed with willow and with bone. The fire that was covered and kept, and that waited through the dark. Eighteen thousand years later, a farmer digging a cellar would break into the ground not far from here, and archaeologists would come, and find the ash of fires like this one, layer upon layer, still lying in the earth where they were left. But this morning, it's alive. The children are gathering around it now, holding out their hands to the warmth. Ennu is laying another stick across it. And the flames rise up, low and orange and steady, and the smoke drifts up through the roof, and the light of the fire moves softly on every face in the room.

They have survived the night. They knew how. And now, so do you.

Thank you for spending this long winter night with me, and with Ennu, and Haro and Siva, and Mael, and Tula and Pim and little Nia, and Smoke at the door, in their small, warm, smoky house of bone above the river at Mezhyrich. If this story helped you rest, or helped you drift off somewhere along the way, I'm truly glad. And if you'd like to come back for more nights like this one, you might consider liking this video and subscribing to the channel, gently, whenever you're ready. There's no hurry at all.

Next time, we'll travel much further back in time, to meet the other people of the Ice Age. We'll spend a night with a family of Neanderthals, in a rock shelter in the long ages before our own kind ever came to Europe, and find out how they kept warm, and fed their children, and cared for their old and their sick, through a winter of their own. They were not the brutes of the old picture books either. I hope you'll join me there.

Until then, sleep well. Keep warm. And may your own fire, whatever it is, still be glowing in the morning.

## How the sources were checked

- **The family is fictional.** Ennu, Haro, Siva, Mael, Tula, Pim and Nia, and the canid Smoke, are invented, and Part One says so: no Ice Age names can be known. No invented person is presented as appearing in any record, and no real scholar is given invented speech.
- **The setting is real.** The Epigravettian mammoth-bone dwelling site at Mezhyrich, Cherkasy Oblast, central Ukraine. The night is an unspecified midwinter night "around eighteen thousand years ago," following the 2025 Leiden re-dating (Chu, Shydlovskyi & Maier). The older "about 15,000 years" figure is explained in Part One as a radiocarbon (uncalibrated) age, and the difference is presented as a live disagreement.
- **How quotes were checked.** Direct page fetches are blocked here, so every quotation and record was checked through web-search result summaries of the pages listed below. There are only four verbatim quotations, all from scientists or their published abstracts, or press coverage of them (Kittler et al. 2003; Drake et al. 2015; Adovasio's team in 2000 press reports; Conard in *National Geographic*, 2009; Gilligan in 2024 press coverage). Everything else is paraphrase. Before publishing, each verbatim line should still be checked against the print source.
- **Search budget.** The shared web-search budget for this session ran out part-way through research. The items listed under "General background, not re-verified in this session" below are standard textbook facts. They were not individually re-checked, and the narration states them without precise citations.
- **What was cut or hedged for lack of verification.**
  - The often-repeated figure of "149 mammoths/bones" at Mezhyrich was cut because the sources contradict each other.
  - The engraved "map" on a Mezhyrich tusk was cut because its only source was user-submitted.
  - The source distance of the Mezhyrich amber and shells (350–500 km) is hedged in the narration as an unconfirmed secondary estimate, and their Black Sea origin is not claimed.
  - A Black Sea shell origin is attributed only to Mezmaiskaya Cave.
  - No stone lamp is claimed for Mezhyrich. The narration says Ennu's lamp is imagined.
  - Soffer's "natural freezer" wording was not quoted. The storage-pit reading is presented as the widely held interpretation, with the rubbish-pit caveat.
  - Bocquet-Appel et al. (2005) is cited for its method only, because its population figures could not be confirmed.
  - The authorship of the 170,000-year louse study (Toups, Reed et al.) is plausible but was not confirmed in the search results.
  - The Mezin "orchestra" bones are mentioned without naming the excavator.
  - The first names of the Venus fingerprint researchers were removed.
- **Computed, not quoted.** Midwinter sunset (about 3:57 pm), sunrise (about 7:59 am) and astronomical dusk (about 6:00 pm) were computed with PyEphem for a modern 21 December at latitude 49.6° N, longitude 31.45° E, and converted to local solar time. The narration says this was worked out with a modern astronomical program. Axial tilt 18,000 years ago differed slightly, so the times are approximate. The precession cycle (about 26,000 years), and the statement that Polaris was not the pole star 18,000 years ago, are standard astronomy.
