# How Vikings Survived the Coldest Arctic Nights | History for Sleep

## Part One — Around Half Past One in the Afternoon: The Last Blue Light

It is the middle of the day, and the sun has not come up.

It will not come up today at all. Over the long, low ridge of Borg, on the island we now call Vestvågøy, in the Lofoten Islands of northern Norway, the sky to the south has turned a deep, clear, glassy blue, and along the line of the mountains there is a band of pale rose and apricot, as if the sun were just below the edge of the world, thinking about rising, and deciding against it. To the north, the sky is already the colour of slate. The snow on the hillsides gives back what little light there is, so that everything glows faintly from below, the way a room glows when the lamp has been turned down very low. The sea, which is never far away here, lies grey and quiet in the fjords, and its breath, damp and salty, drifts up over the fields.

And on top of the ridge, long and low and humped like a sleeping animal, there is a house.

It is the longest house for a great many miles in any direction. Its walls are thick and soft-edged, and its roof is a long dark line against the blue. Thin threads of smoke rise from it into the still, cold air. Somewhere inside, a cow is lowing. And down below the house, on the shore of a small bay, a young man is hauling a boat up over the stones, his breath steaming, his hands raw.

This is Borg. The year is 890. And the night that is beginning now, in the early afternoon, is the longest night of the year.

When most of us hear the word Viking, we see a particular picture. A huge bearded man in a horned helmet, an axe in his fist, leaping from a dragon-headed ship onto a burning beach. We see raiders, and fire, and fear.

But that is not who we are going to spend tonight with. Not the Viking of the movies. Horned helmets, for a start, are a costume invention of the nineteenth century; no Viking Age helmet with horns has ever been found. And most of the people we call Vikings were not raiders at all. They were farmers, and fishers, and traders, and herders, and weavers, and boat-builders, people whose great struggle in life was not with monks or kings, but with winter. And this, here, at the edge of the Arctic, is about as deep into winter as a farming people has ever lived.

I'll be honest with you before we go in. If you were taken from your own bed tonight and set down on this hill in your nightclothes, with the light already going at half past one in the afternoon and some eighteen hours of darkness ahead, you would not have survived until morning. Not because the people here were made of something tougher than us. But because they knew a great many small and particular things that we have forgotten. And tonight, slowly, one at a time, we are going to learn them.

While we do, I'd like you to carry three small mysteries with you, like three smooth pebbles from the shore in your coat pocket.

The first is this. Why would people who lived beside an ocean that threw up driftwood on every beach, and under hillsides grown with birch, choose to build the walls of the greatest house in the north out of earth and grass?

The second. Why would a family rich enough to own twenty cows eat for their supper a fish so hard and dry that you could knock on it like a door, a fish that had never been near a grain of salt? And why might that very same kind of fish turn up on a table a thousand miles to the south?

And the third. In a place where nobody has seen the sun for weeks, why is no one in this house afraid that it will never come back?

I'll answer each of them in its own time. The first not long after the lamps are lit. The second at supper. And the third when the house is getting ready for sleep. There's no hurry at all. We have the whole of the longest night.

Now, before we go up to the door, I want to be clear with you about something, because it matters on this channel. The family you are about to meet is invented. Their names, their ages, their habits and their small kindnesses, I have made up. But the place is real. The house is real; its foundations were dug out of this ridge by archaeologists in the nineteen-eighties, and you can stand on the spot today. And the voices from this world that I'll bring into the room tonight, a sea captain speaking to an English king, a poem of advice, an Arab traveller, an old law book, a Greek historian, those are real, and I'll tell you their names and their dates as we go, so that you can look them up yourself, some wakeful afternoon.

So. Let's meet them.

The young man hauling the boat is Grím. He is twenty-four, a free man, not of the family, but of the household, the way farmhands were in this world. He has been out since the grey of the morning checking lines in the fjord, and he has three good fish to show for it, and frozen fingers.

Walking down to help him, with a heavy cloak around his shoulders, is the master of the house, Thórir. He is forty-one. He is what this part of the world would call a chieftain, a big man in the district, a man other farmers come to with their quarrels and their troubles. But do not picture a king on a throne. Picture a farmer with very large responsibilities, and good boots, and a worried eye on the hay.

Up at the house, standing in the doorway with a lamp not yet lit in her hand, is his wife, Ragnhild. She is thirty-seven, and at her belt hang the keys of the house, to the chests and the storerooms, and everyone on this farm knows exactly what those keys mean.

Inside, sitting nearest the long fire, as she always does, is Thórir's mother, Álfhild, sixty-four, with a face like weathered wood and a spindle that never seems to stop turning.

Then there are the children. Eyvind is fifteen, nearly a man, eager and restless, and he has been allowed out in the boat twice this winter. Sigrid is eleven, sharp-eyed and clever with her hands, and already a good weaver. Ketil is seven, and wants to know everything. And Bera is three, round and pink-cheeked in a little woollen hood, and she is, at this moment, trying to put the dog's tail in her mouth.

The dog is called Snati. He is grey and shaggy and patient, and he has decided to allow it.

And there is Oddný, thirty, who was born on a smaller farm across the fjord and came to Borg to work, and who knows the dairy and the byre better than anyone.

Nine people, then, in our story. A real chieftain's house like this one would often have held more, other workers, other kin, and almost certainly some who were not free, because this was a world that kept slaves, and I won't pretend it wasn't. But tonight, it's these nine we'll follow.

Here's a small question to start with, and we'll answer it before this part is done. Why is the house up here, on top of the ridge, in the wind, instead of down in the shelter of the bay?

Imagine, now, that you are walking up the slope beside Thórir, your boots crunching on the old snow. The cold is not as fierce as you expected. It's damp, and raw, and it gets into your sleeves, but it is not the iron, killing cold of the inland mountains. That's because of the sea. The great warm current that runs up the coast of Norway from the south keeps these islands far milder than other places at the same latitude. Borg lies at about sixty-eight degrees north, well over a hundred miles inside the Arctic Circle, on the same line as the frozen interior of Alaska and the north of Siberia. And yet the fjords here do not freeze in winter. The sea stays open. The fish stay close. That warm water is one of the reasons anyone can live here at all.

You reach the top of the ridge, and you turn, and you look back. And now you see why the house is here.

From this height, you can see everything. The bay below, with the boats drawn up. The fjord running out towards the open sea. The other farms scattered along the shore, their own small smudges of smoke. The mountains, blue-white and enormous, standing all around like a wall. Anyone coming by water, and almost everyone comes by water here, can be seen from this hill long before they arrive. And, just as importantly, this house can be seen by them. A long, high roof on a ridge says something to every boat that passes. It says, a great family lives here. Come with respect.

There's another practical reason, too. The ridge is dry. Down in the hollows, meltwater and rain gather, and a house built there would stand in wet ground every spring. Up here, the water runs away downhill, and the floors stay dry.

That's the answer to our first small question. The house sits on the hill to see, to be seen, and to keep its feet dry.

And the name itself may be a clue. In Old Norse, the word *borg* could mean a fortification, a stronghold, but also a rounded, rocky hill, a natural height that looks like one. Either way, it's a name about standing up above the land around you.

Now, a few words about how we know any of this.

In 1981, a farmer ploughing his field on this ridge noticed, so the story goes, that the soil in one long stretch was darker than the soil around it. Archaeologists began investigating in 1983, and from 1986 until 1989, a joint project of researchers from Norway, Sweden, Denmark and Iceland excavated the site. What they found astonished them. Under the turf lay the remains of a building some eighty-three metres long, by the figure most often given, and about nine and a half metres wide. That's nearly the length of an American football field from goal line to goal line, all under a single roof. It is among the largest buildings ever found from the Viking Age. The first house on the spot seems to have been put up in the five hundreds, and then rebuilt and lengthened in the seven hundreds, and people went on living in it until around the middle of the nine hundreds. So in 890, our year, it's already an old house, and a famous one.

And inside it, the excavators found things that do not belong to an ordinary farm. Pieces of glass drinking vessels, brought from far to the south in western Europe. Fragments of fine pottery from the Continent. And, buried in the holes where the great roof posts once stood, five tiny pieces of gold foil, each one stamped with figures, so thin and small that one could sit on your fingernail. We'll come back to them later tonight.

Now, here is our first place where historians disagree, and it's about the very word I've been using.

Is it right to call Thórir and his family Vikings at all? In Old Norse, the word *víkingr* seems to have meant a person who went raiding by sea, and to go *í víking* was to go on such an expedition. It was something you did, often for a summer or two in your youth, not something you were. A farmer who stayed at home and fished and kept cattle was not, in that strict sense, a Viking. Many scholars today prefer to speak of Norse people, or Scandinavians, of the Viking Age, and keep the word Viking for the raiders. Others argue that the word has simply become the everyday name for the whole culture, and that it's too late, and too useful, to take it back. Even where the word itself comes from is argued over. Some link it to *vík*, a bay or inlet, as in people who lurk in bays; others to a region of southern Norway called Viken; and others to other roots altogether. It's a debate that has gone on for well over a century, and it hasn't been settled. Tonight, I'll use the word in the broad, everyday sense, the way the title does. But I'd like you to remember, as we go in, that the man walking up the hill beside you would probably not have called himself one.

Let's put this beside your own home for a moment. Most of us live in houses that face the road, not the sea, and few of us think about who can see our roof from far away. But we do still build to be seen. A big front door, a tidy garden, lights in the window at Christmas. And we still think about drainage, if we've ever had a wet basement. Thórir's choice of a hilltop is the same choice, made with the materials of his world: the view, the pride, and the dry ground.

There is one man from this very coast, and from this very time, whose own words have come down to us, and I want you to hear them before we step inside. His name, in Old Norse, would have been Óttarr. The English wrote it Ohthere. He came from Hålogaland, the old name for this northern stretch of Norway, and at some point in the late eight hundreds, he sailed all the way to England and visited the court of King Alfred the Great. And Alfred's scholars wrote down what he told them, in Old English, and slipped it into a translation of a Latin history of the world by a writer called Orosius. It begins, in the original, like this:

"Ōhthere sǣde his hlāforde, Ælfrēde cyninge, þæt hē ealra Norðmonna norþmest būde."

Ohthere said to his lord, King Alfred, that he lived northmost of all the Northmen.

Northmost of all the Northmen. Scholars aren't sure exactly where his farm was, but it was somewhere north of here, probably in the region around what is now the town of Tromsø, a few days' sailing up the coast. He was a neighbour of Thórir's, in the way that people in a thinly peopled land are all neighbours. And everything he told King Alfred, about cattle and reindeer and furs and whales and the long sea road to the south, describes the world we're about to enter.

Thórir and Grím come up the last of the slope with the fish. Ragnhild, at the door, takes one look at Grím's hands and tells him to go straight in to the fire.

Behind them, the band of rose along the southern mountains is already fading. The blue is deepening. The midday twilight, which is all the day there will be, is beginning to close.

And we go in.

## Part Two — Around Half Past Two: The Beasts in the Dark

Here is the small question for this hour, and it's a farmer's question. If winter here lasts the better part of seven months, and the grass is under snow for most of it, how do you keep twenty cows alive until spring?

Hold onto that. Because before we see the fire, we're going to see the animals.

Imagine you have stepped in through the door behind Ragnhild. It's a low, heavy door of thick planks, and you have to duck your head. You're in a small entrance room, dim and cold, with a floor of trampled earth, pegs on the wall hung with cloaks, and a smell, rising up at you from somewhere ahead, that is unmistakable. Warm animals. Hay. Dung. Milk. And underneath it, smoke.

The house at Borg, as the excavators reconstructed it, was not one big room, but a row of rooms set end to end under the one long roof. At one end, the family's living quarters, with the hearth. In the middle, a great hall for feasts and gatherings. A main entrance room. Storerooms. And at one end, a room for animals, the byre, or stable. It's that room that Ragnhild takes you to first, because Oddný is already there, and it's milking time.

You step through, and the warmth hits you. Not the dry warmth of a fire, but a deep, damp, breathing warmth, like stepping into a cowshed on a winter morning, which is exactly what it is. In the gloom, lit only by a single small lamp hung on a post, you can make out the animals. A double row of cows, standing in stalls divided by low boards, their heads towards the walls, their tails towards a shallow channel running down the middle of the floor. Brown cows, black cows, a red one with a white face. They turn to look at you, slowly, with that mild, enormous curiosity that cows have. Their breath steams in the lamplight.

They are small. That's the first thing you'd notice, if you know modern cattle. Bones of cattle from Viking Age sites across Scandinavia show animals much smaller than the big dairy breeds of today, often not much taller at the shoulder than a large pony. They gave far less milk, too. And in the dark of winter, on winter feed, many of them would give little or none.

Oddný is on a low stool beside the red cow, her cheek against its flank, milking into a wooden pail. The sound is soft and rhythmic, a hiss and a hiss and a hiss. She doesn't look up. She's talking to the cow in a low murmur, the way people who work with animals always do.

Beyond the cows, in their own stalls, there are the horses, two of them, sturdy, shaggy, short-legged animals with long manes. There are sheep in a pen, their fleeces thick and grey and smelling of lanolin. And somewhere in the dark at the far end, you hear a grunting that tells you there are pigs, too.

The sheep deserve a moment of their own. They are not the big, soft, white sheep of a modern farm. They are small, tough, short-tailed animals, in greys and browns and blacks as well as white, with a double coat: long, coarse outer hair that sheds the rain and snow, and a fine, soft underwool next to the skin that keeps them warm. Their descendants still live in Norway today. An old breed, the Norwegian short-tailed sheep, sometimes called the old Norse sheep, still grazes on some of the coastal islands of the west, and in some places those flocks are left out of doors all year round, sheltering among the rocks and eating seaweed on the shore in the depth of winter. The wool from animals like these, often plucked or combed out by hand in early summer, when the old fleece loosens by itself, though iron shears were known too, was the raw stuff of nearly every piece of cloth in this house, and, as we'll see later tonight, of the sails of the ships.

The horses, too, are worth a second look. Ohthere's remark that he ploughed with horses is one of the small surprises of his account, because in much of Europe at this time, ploughing was ox work. But in the north, a horse was a practical choice: quick, strong, sure-footed on rough ground, and good for riding and hauling as well as for the plough. These two in Thórir's stable will pull sledges of firewood and hay across the snow this winter, when the ground is frozen hard and a sledge runs more easily than a cart ever could.

Here's the man from the north, Ohthere, again, because he gives us exactly this picture of a farm on this coast. When he told King Alfred about his wealth, he explained that it did not lie mainly in farm animals, as an Englishman might expect. In the account as it was written down, he says, and I'll give it in a plain modern paraphrase, that he had only about twenty cattle, twenty sheep and twenty pigs, and that the little land he ploughed, he ploughed with horses. To an English king, used to great estates with oxen by the hundred, that must have sounded small. But up here, on this narrow strip of green between the mountains and the sea, twenty cows was a great deal. Twenty cows had to be fed all winter long.

And that brings us back to our small question.

The answer begins last summer.

In the bright months, when the sun here never sets at all, from late May until the middle of July, everyone on the farm worked to put away food for the animals. The grass on the home fields, the meadows nearest the house, was cut with iron scythes and dried in the wind and carried in as hay. That hay is stacked now in a loft above your head, and in a barn outside, and it is counted, and watched, and worried over, the way a family in a hard year watches its bank balance.

But hay alone was not enough. And so the people of Norway did something that would surprise many modern farmers. They fed their animals on trees.

All through the summer, Thórir's household went out to the birch and the willow and the rowan on the slopes, and cut leafy branches, and tied them into bundles, and hung them up to dry, leaves and all. Leaf fodder, it's called. Dried, the leaves kept well, and in the depth of winter, a bundle of birch twigs, still rustling with dry leaves, could be thrown to the sheep and goats, who would strip it bare. In many parts of Norway, this custom lasted right into the twentieth century. Botanists can still see the shapes of old pollarded trees, cut again and again at head height, in the farm landscapes of the west.

And on the coast, there was the sea. Seaweed could be gathered from the shore and given to the animals. Fish heads and scraps, boiled up, were fed to cattle on the northern coasts in later centuries, a practice written down by visitors to this coast long after our night; whether Thórir's household did the same in 890, we can't be sure, though it would be a very natural thing to do in a land with more fish than grass.

So that's the answer. You keep twenty cows alive through a northern winter by spending the whole summer getting ready for it. Hay from the meadows. Leaves from the trees. Weed from the shore. And above all, by keeping them indoors, out of the wind, here, in the warm dark, under the same roof as the family.

There's one more thing about these animals that I want you to know, because it's a little sad, and also very true. By the end of a long winter, in the leanest years, cows on Norwegian farms could become so weak from poor feeding that when spring came they could barely stand. In later centuries, there are accounts of farmers having to help, or even carry, their cows out of the byre to the first green grass. That comes from records long after our night, so I won't say it happened at Borg. But it tells you how close to the edge a winter's fodder could run. Every armful of hay that Grím forks down from the loft tonight is a piece of next spring.

Imagine you are standing beside Oddný now, and she hands you the pail to hold while she moves to the next cow. It's warm. You can feel the warmth of the milk right through the wooden staves. There isn't much of it. A few pints, perhaps, from the cows that are still milking at all. Tonight it will go into the dairy, to be set aside for cream, and for the making of the sour, thick, cultured milk foods that kept these households alive. We'll taste some of that at supper.

Now, let's set all of this beside your own home. Your food, and the food of the animals that feed you, arrives on lorries and trains and ships, from all over the world, every day of the year. If a harvest fails somewhere, the shelves are filled from somewhere else. Thórir's shelves are this barn and this loft. There's no somewhere else. What he put away in the summer is all there is, until the snow goes.

There's a point here where historians disagree, and it's about Ohthere himself, and where exactly his farm was.

He said he lived northmost of all the Northmen. But where is that? The account doesn't name his farm. Over the years, scholars have put it in several places along the coast of what is now Troms, in northern Norway. Some have pointed to the island of Bjarkøy, because about a century after Ohthere's time, a powerful chieftain family lived there, the most important family in the far north. Others favour the area at the mouth of the Malangen fjord, around Hillesøy, which has rich archaeological finds and was later recorded as the site of a chieftain's farm. And if you take Ohthere at his word, that he lived further north than anyone, you might push his home even further, towards islands like Kvaløy or Karlsøy. The archaeologist Inger Storli, writing in a collection of essays about Ohthere published in 2007, concluded that his farm should be placed somewhere between the chieftain's centre on Bjarkøy in the south and the northernmost limit of growing grain, on the coast of Kvaløy, in the north. So we have a range, not a point. It's a lovely, unhurried kind of puzzle. And it doesn't change anything for us tonight. Wherever he lived, he would have known a byre exactly like this one.

Grím comes in now, with his hands still red from the fjord but with some life back in them, and climbs the short ladder to the hay loft, and begins to fork down hay into the mangers. The cows shift and turn their heads towards the sound. The horses blow through their noses. The sheep, who know exactly what that sound means, all begin to call at once.

And this is the other great reason the animals live inside the house, and it's one we'll come back to, more than once, before the night is out. They are warm. A full-grown cow, digesting a belly full of hay, gives off a great deal of heat, by modern farm estimates something like a kilowatt, about as much as a small electric heater, and she does it all night without any fuel but what she's already eaten. Twenty of them, in a closed room under a thick roof, are a furnace. Some of that warmth leaks through the walls between the rooms. Some of it rises into the roof space. On a night like this, it all matters.

Ketil has followed you in. He's seven, and he has a job, which is to make sure the calf in the corner pen has water that hasn't frozen over, and he takes it very seriously. He breaks a thin skin of ice on the trough with his fist, and shows you, very proudly, the little round window of ice, and then drops it in the straw.

Bera has followed Ketil. She is not supposed to be in the byre. She stands in the doorway with her thumb in her mouth, staring at the horses, until Oddný, without looking round, says her name in a particular tone of voice, and Bera turns and toddles back towards the warmth of the fire.

Every few days, the dung must be shovelled out of the channel and carried to the great heap outside, where, in spring, it will go out onto the home fields. Without it, the hay meadows would not grow so thick, and next winter there would be less hay. So even the dung is a kind of store. It's part of a circle, from the meadow to the cow to the heap and back to the meadow, that turns round once a year and keeps this whole household alive.

Thórir comes in last. He walks down the line of cows slowly, the way a farmer does, looking at each one. He runs his hand along a flank here, looks at an eye there, checks a hoof. He stops at the red cow with the white face, the oldest, and stands with her a while. She's due to calve in the spring, if she lasts. He says something to Oddný about giving her a little extra. Oddný nods.

Then he looks up at the loft, where Grím is working, and at the stacked hay, and you see him doing the sum in his head that every farmer in the north does every winter evening. How much hay is left. How many weeks till grass. How many mouths.

The answer seems to satisfy him, for now. He turns and goes out towards the living room and the fire.

Out beyond the walls, the blue in the sky has deepened almost to night. Only a faint, cold glow lingers along the southern mountains, and the snow still holds a little of it, but the peaks are black shapes now, and in the north the first stars are beginning to show, one by one. It's not yet three o'clock in the afternoon.

And the house has begun to settle into its long night.

## Part Three — Around Half Past Three: The Long Fire and the Little Lamps

Here is this hour's small question. When the day has gone by the middle of the afternoon, and the dark will last until the middle of the next morning, what do you burn to light the room, in a land where wood is precious?

Let's go and find out. Let's go to the fire.

Imagine you are walking out of the byre now, behind Thórir, through the entrance room and on into the living quarters at the other end of the house. The first thing you notice is the change in the air. It's warmer here, drier, and it smells of woodsmoke and wool and something cooking. The second thing you notice is the light. It's orange, and low, and it moves. And the third thing you notice is the fire itself.

It is not a fire in a fireplace. There is no fireplace. There's no chimney anywhere in this house. Instead, right down the middle of the room, in the centre of the floor, there's a long, narrow hearth, edged with flat stones set on their sides, perhaps two or three paces long, and along it, a low bed of glowing wood and embers, with small flames running here and there. The Norse called a hearth like this a *langeldr*, a long fire. It is the heart of the house. Everything in this room turns towards it.

Above it, high in the roof, there's an opening, a smoke-hole, and the smoke from the long fire rises slowly towards it, in a soft blue-grey layer under the rafters, and finds its way out into the night. Not all of it. Some of it lingers, and drifts, and stings your eyes for a moment when you first come in. Then, strangely, you stop noticing it.

There's a small treasure hidden in that opening in the roof, and it's in the language. Openings like this one, and the small holes in the walls of Norse houses that let in a little air and light, could be called *vindauga* in Old Norse. *Vindr*, wind, and *auga*, eye. A wind-eye. The Norse settlers who came to England brought the word with them, and the English borrowed it, and over the centuries, it wore smooth into a word you use every day. Window. Every time you look out of a window, you're using a word that once meant a hole in a Norse roof where the wind looked in.

In the dark of a December afternoon, the wind-eyes of this house are mostly closed. Some would have been stopped with a wooden shutter. Others might be covered with a thin, stretched membrane, perhaps the dried birth-sac of a calf, or a scraped skin, which let through a faint, milky glow in daytime, but no draught. Glass, in a window, would have seemed to Thórir an unbelievable extravagance. The only glass in this house is in the drinking vessels that came from the south, and those are kept very carefully indeed.

Along both long walls of the room, there are wide raised platforms of earth faced with planks, about knee-high, running the length of the room. These are where people sit by day and sleep by night. They're covered with skins and woollen blankets and rugs. Álfhild is sitting on one now, at the end nearest the fire, as she always does. Sigrid is beside her. Bera is on the floor between them, playing with a little carved wooden horse. And Grím, his hands still stinging, is crouched right at the edge of the hearth, holding his fingers out to the embers.

There's a verse I want you to hear now, because it could have been written about Grím at this exact moment. It comes from a poem called the Hávamál, which means something like the Sayings of the High One, the High One being the god Odin. It's a long poem of advice, about hospitality, friendship, caution, and how to behave in someone else's house. It survives in a single manuscript, called the Codex Regius, which was written in Iceland around 1270, almost four centuries after our night. But many scholars believe that parts of the poem are much older, and were spoken aloud long before anyone wrote them down. The third verse begins like this, in Old Norse:

"Elds er þörf, þeims inn er kominn ok á kné kalinn."

And in Henry Adams Bellows's translation of 1923, it goes:

"Fire he needs who with frozen knees has come from the cold without."

Frozen knees. Not frozen hands, or a frozen face, which is what most of us would think of first. But anyone who has walked a long way through deep snow, or sat in an open boat in winter with spray coming over the side, knows that it's the knees that go first, and ache longest. The poet knew that, too. And Ragnhild, who knows it as well as any poet, is already bringing Grím a bowl of something hot.

Now, about the light.

The long fire gives most of it. For the whole of this long evening, the household will live within its glow, the way the family lives within the glow of a television in a darkened modern room. But firelight flickers, and doesn't reach far, and some work needs a steadier light. For that, there are lamps.

Ragnhild takes one down from a peg. It's a small, shallow bowl, about the size of your palm, made of iron, with a long iron spike beneath it, so that it can be stuck into a post or the wall, or the ground. Lamps like this have been found at Viking Age sites all over Scandinavia. In other houses, and especially here in the north, the bowl might be carved from soapstone, a soft grey stone that can be shaped with an iron knife and that holds heat well. Into the bowl, Ragnhild pours a little oil from a jar. Then she lays in a wick, a little twist of fibre, perhaps of the soft white fluff of cotton grass, which grows on the boggy ground here and was used for lamp-wicks in the north for centuries, and touches it to an ember from the fire, and it catches with a small, steady, yellow flame.

And the oil, the answer to our small question, comes from the sea.

It's fish oil. Oil from the livers of cod, and other fish, rendered down, or blubber from seals. All along this coast, for as long as anyone can remember, the livers of the fish caught in winter were saved, and left in barrels or tubs, where the oil slowly rose to the top and could be skimmed off. Later generations in Norway called it *tran*. It's the same cod liver oil that generations of children in the twentieth century were made to swallow on a spoon every morning in winter, for their health. Here, it's mostly for light. It burns with a small, smoky, smelly flame. But it burns. And in a land where trees are few and every log is counted, a lamp that burns fish is a lamp that doesn't eat the woodpile.

So that's our answer. In the long dark of Lofoten, you light the house with the long fire and with the sea.

And what does the long fire itself burn? Some wood, certainly. Birch from the slopes, and juniper, and willow. And driftwood, great logs of it, which the currents carry here across the Arctic Ocean from the vast forests of Siberia and leave on the beaches, silver-grey and salt-soaked and dried by the wind. People on treeless northern coasts and islands, all the way from here to Iceland and Greenland, relied on driftwood for centuries. But in many places in the north, the fire also burned peat or turf, cut from the boggy ground in summer and dried in stacks. It burns slowly, with a sweet, earthy smoke, and it's the smell that hangs in the cloaks of everyone in this house.

Let me tell you something about how this fire was lit in the first place, because no one in this house has a match. They have a strike-a-light. It's a small curved piece of hard steel, often with a loop for the fingers, and a piece of flint or quartz. You strike the steel against the edge of the stone, and a shower of tiny sparks flies off. You catch them on a piece of tinder, often a slice of a dried fungus that grows on birch trees, the tinder fungus, specially prepared so that it smoulders the moment a spark lands on it. Then you breathe on it, gently, and add dry grass, and twigs, and the fire is born. It's a slow, careful, fiddly job, especially with cold hands. Which is why, in a house like this, people tried very hard never to let the fire go out at all.

Let's set that beside your own home for a moment. You flick a switch, and the room fills with light. You turn a dial, and the radiators warm. You might not have struck a flame by hand in years. A modern LED bulb gives off something like eight hundred lumens of light. A small oil lamp like Ragnhild's might give perhaps a dozen. So this whole room, with its long fire and two or three little lamps, is lit by less than a single bulb in your kitchen. And yet everyone in it can see perfectly well. Our eyes, given twenty or thirty minutes in the dark, open themselves up remarkably, as the light-sensitive cells in the back of the eye slowly gather their strength. The people in this house lived much of their lives in that kind of soft half-light. Our bright modern rooms at night would have seemed to them almost unbearably dazzling.

Here's someone else, from another corner of the Norse world, who knew all about the cold. In the year 922, about thirty years after our night, an envoy named Ahmad ibn Fadlan travelled from Baghdad, sent by the caliph, on a long journey north to the king of the Volga Bulghars, in what is now Russia. He wrote an account of his travels, and it's one of the most famous documents of the age, because along the way he met the Rus, Scandinavian traders on the Volga river. We'll hear what he made of them later tonight. But first, he had to get through a winter on the edge of the steppe, in the land of Khwarazm, south of the Aral Sea. And I'll paraphrase what he said about it, from the translation published in English by Caroline Stone and Paul Lunde. He describes washing, and finding afterwards that his beard had frozen into a single lump of ice, so that he had to thaw it out in front of the fire. He describes sleeping under many blankets and still waking to find his face stuck to the pillow by frost. He was a man from the warm cities of Iraq, and he had never imagined anything like it.

The cold of Khwarazm, in the middle of a continent, was in some ways harsher than the cold here on the coast. But I like to think of ibn Fadlan, with his frozen beard, crouching in front of a fire, and Grím, with his frozen knees, crouching in front of this one, in the same century, at opposite ends of the same great cold.

And that brings us to a place where scientists disagree, and it's about the weather of this whole age.

You may have heard that the Viking Age fell during a warm period in the climate of the North Atlantic, sometimes called the Medieval Warm Period. The name was made popular by the British climatologist Hubert Lamb, in the 1960s, who argued that the centuries around the year 1000 were unusually mild in Europe. That warmth, it's often said, helped the Norse to farm in Iceland and Greenland and to sail the northern seas. But in the decades since, many climate scientists have argued that the picture was more patchy than Lamb believed. In 1994, the scientists Malcolm Hughes and Henry Diaz published a paper asking, in its very title, whether there had really been a Medieval Warm Period at all, and showing that the warmth came at different times in different places, and was not the same everywhere. Many researchers now prefer the more cautious name, the Medieval Climate Anomaly. Tree rings and ice cores and lake mud from all over the world have been studied to try to settle it, and the picture they give is of a time that was warmer in some regions, cooler in others, and changeable. Whether Thórir's winters were milder than his grandfather's, we can't really say. What we can say is that tonight, it is cold enough, and dark enough, and the fire is very welcome.

Grím has finished his bowl. He sets it down by the hearth and sighs, the long, deep sigh of a man whose knees have finally stopped hurting. Álfhild tells him that young men are always forgetting to wrap their legs, and he tells her, politely, that he had his legs wrapped, and she tells him to wrap them better. Sigrid laughs.

Thórir sits down on the platform across the fire from his mother. He leans forward and lays a split piece of birch across the embers, carefully, crosswise, so that it won't roll. It catches. A small new flame runs along its underside, blue at the root, then yellow, then orange. Bera watches it, fascinated, all the way along.

And the long fire, which has been burning in this spot, in one house or another, for something like three hundred years, burns on.

## Part Four — Around Half Past Four: Walls of Earth and Grass

Here is the small question for this part of the evening. A roof eighty metres long, heavy with snow, over a house with no stone walls and no steel, what is holding it up?

Look up.

Imagine you are lying back on the wide sleeping platform beside Álfhild, on a heap of sheepskins, with your hands behind your head, looking up into the roof. It's high above you, lost in smoke and shadow, but as your eyes adjust, you can see its bones. Two rows of great wooden posts run the length of the house, one row on each side of the long fire, standing a little in from the walls, like the pillars of a church. They're thick, as thick as a man's body, and dark with smoke. On top of them run long beams, and from the beams, the rafters slope up towards the ridge and down towards the walls. The posts carry almost all the weight of the roof. The walls, as you'll see in a moment, carry very little.

That's the answer to our small question. It's the posts. Two long rows of timber pillars, sunk deep into the ground, each one set in its own pit and packed around with stones. It's in the holes left by those very posts, the postholes, that the archaeologists found the five tiny gold foils I mentioned at the beginning of the night. Someone, long ago, had pressed them into the ground where the posts stood, perhaps when the house was built or rebuilt. We'll come back to why, a little later on.

And these posts are a reminder of something. Timber is not absent here. It's precious. Great straight posts like these may have come from far away, from the forested valleys of the mainland, or as driftwood, or traded down the coast. Every one of them is worth a great deal. Thórir's grandfather would have known exactly where each one came from.

Now. Reach out your hand to the wall beside you.

It's not wood. Behind a lining of planks, the wall is soft and cool and very slightly damp to the touch, and if you dig your fingernail into a gap, it comes away dark with soil. And if you went outside and walked round the house, you'd see what the archaeologists saw when they first came to this ridge: that the outer walls are built of turf. Thick slabs of earth, cut from the ground with the grass still growing on them, stacked one upon another, like enormous bricks, grass side down, on a footing of stones. The turf walls of houses like this were thick, often more than a metre, sometimes nearly two. In some places they were built in neat courses, and in others laid in a slanting pattern, like the bones of a fish.

And so here, at last, is the first of our three mysteries. I asked you at the beginning of the night: why would people who lived beside an ocean that threw up driftwood on every beach, and under hillsides grown with birch, choose to build the walls of the greatest house in the north out of earth and grass?

There are three answers, and they're all true at once.

The first is warmth. A thick turf wall is, in a sense, a wall made mostly of tiny pockets of still air, trapped between the soil and the matted roots of the grass. Still air is one of the best insulators there is. A metre or more of packed turf keeps the warmth of the fire and the animals in, and keeps the bitter wind out, far better than a single thickness of wooden planks. A plank wall has joints, and joints let in draughts, and in a gale off the Norwegian Sea, a draught can feel like a knife. A turf wall has no joints at all. It's one thick, soft, solid, windproof blanket of earth, wrapped all the way round the house.

The second answer is that turf is everywhere. You don't have to trade for it, or sail for it, or wait for the sea to bring it. It's lying under your feet. A household could cut what it needed from the home fields and the moorland in the summer, with an iron spade, and stack it, and dry it a little, and build. Timber, on these islands, was for the frame and the posts and the boats, the things that only timber could do. To use it for whole walls, when the earth would do the job better, would be wasteful.

And the third answer is that a turf wall can be mended. If a section slumps, or the frost heaves it, or a storm tears at the outer face, you cut more turf and patch it. The house can go on for generations, renewed piece by piece, with the same great posts inside and fresh earth outside, the way a ship is kept going with new planks.

So there's our first mystery solved. The greatest house in the north has walls of earth because earth was warmer than wood, because earth was free, and because earth could always be renewed.

And there's something else. A house like this, from outside, would have looked almost like part of the land. Green in summer, white with snow in winter, its long humped walls softened and rounded by the weather. The Norse did not build against the landscape. They built out of it.

Let's set that beside your own home. The walls of a modern house in a cold country are built around insulation, often fibreglass, or mineral wool, or foam, materials designed to do exactly what a turf wall does: to trap still air. Builders today measure how well a wall resists the flow of heat, and the thicker and airier the insulation, the better. When you push a roll of fluffy insulation into a loft, you're doing, with factory-made materials, what Thórir's ancestors did with a spade and a meadow.

But here's a point where the experts don't entirely agree, and it's about how good turf really is.

Turf has a long and honourable reputation as an insulator, and in the cold, dry conditions of an Icelandic winter, people lived in turf houses until well into the twentieth century. But turf can also hold water. A turf wall that gets soaked through in a wet autumn, and then freezes, is much less warm, because water carries heat away far better than air does. Researchers who have measured old turf buildings in Iceland and Norway, and those who build new ones in the traditional way, have argued about just how much warmth such walls really saved, and how much depended on keeping them dry, with good stone footings and a roof that overhung properly. A turf wall, in other words, was only as good as the care that went into it. And here on the wet, windy coast of Lofoten, that care never ended.

And at Borg itself, the archaeologists faced their own puzzle. The walls were clearly turf, but what about the roof? A roof could be covered with turf, too, laid over a layer of birch bark to keep the water out, and that was very common in Norway for centuries. Or it could be covered with wooden shingles, small overlapping tiles of split wood. At Borg, some accounts of the excavation suggest that the great roof was probably covered with shingles, a sign of real wealth, because it meant using a great deal of good wood. But the roof itself rotted away more than a thousand years ago, and the evidence is not as clear as it would be for the walls. So when you picture Thórir's roof tonight, you can picture it either way, and you'll be in good company.

Now, a house built of timber and earth, with a long open fire burning in the middle of it day and night, has one great danger, and it's fire.

We have a law book from this world that shows us how seriously people took it. It comes from Iceland, which the Norse had begun to settle only a few years before our night, in the decades around 870. The laws of medieval Iceland were spoken aloud for generations before they were written down, and the collection we have, which historians call Grágás, the Grey Goose, survives in manuscripts of the thirteenth century. So again, it's later than our night, and from a different land. But it reflects old customs. And one of the things it set out was that the households of each local district in Iceland, a unit called a *hreppr*, were bound together in a kind of mutual insurance. If a member lost his house to fire, or lost a large part of his herd to disease, the other households of the district were to help make good some of the loss. The historian William Ian Miller described these arrangements in his book Bloodtaking and Peacemaking, published in 1990. I'll paraphrase, because the details are complicated, and varied with the buildings and the losses involved. But the principle is clear. Fire was such a common and terrible danger that whole communities organised themselves against it.

In a house like this, the first defence was simply watchfulness. Álfhild watches the fire the way an old sailor watches the sky. Every spark that rises towards the roof, she follows with her eyes until it winks out. She has done it every evening for fifty years.

Imagine now that you go outside with Eyvind for a moment, to fetch an armful of wood. You pull on a borrowed cloak and duck through the door and the cold takes your breath. It's full dark now, though it's not yet five in the afternoon. The stars are out, sharp and many, and the snow underfoot creaks. Eyvind leads you along the side of the house, and you put your gloved hand on the outside of the wall, and you feel it: cold, hard, frozen earth, rough with dead grass, curving away from you into the dark. It's like touching the flank of a hill. And then you go back in through the door, and the warmth and the smoke and the firelight close around you, and you understand, in your body, what that wall is doing.

There's one more detail about turf that I love. Because the walls are made of the very ground of the farm, the archaeologists who dig such houses sometimes find, inside the turf, the traces of what was in the soil when it was cut. Ash layers from old fires. Tiny bits of charcoal. And in Iceland, where volcanoes have dropped layers of ash across the land again and again, the turf in old walls can carry thin bands of that ash within it, which scientists can sometimes match to particular eruptions, and so date the building. One famous layer of ash, which lies just beneath many of the earliest farms in Iceland, has been dated, with the help of the ice cores drilled from the Greenland ice sheet, to the 870s. The earth in the walls keeps a record of its own past.

And far to the west, the same story was repeated. A century after our night, Norse settlers would reach Greenland and build there, too, with turf and stone and driftwood. In the 1990s, archaeologists in Greenland's Western Settlement excavated a Norse farm that had been buried for centuries under windblown sand and silt from a glacial river; it became known as the Farm Beneath the Sand. They found turf walls still standing, and rooms rebuilt and rearranged again and again over about three hundred years. The same idea, in a land even colder than this one.

Before we leave the walls, look once more at the posts, because two of them are not like the others. In the middle of the long platform on one side of the hall, where the master of the house sits on feast nights, there's a seat of honour, the high seat, and it's flanked by a pair of carved posts. The Norse called them *öndvegissúlur*, the high-seat pillars, and they were among the most precious things a family owned. In the Icelandic Book of Settlements, Landnámabók, written down in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, there's a famous story of the first settler, Ingólfr Arnarson, throwing his high-seat pillars overboard as he neared the coast of Iceland, and vowing to make his home wherever the sea carried them ashore. Whether that happened just as it's told, we can't know, because the book was written down centuries later. But the story shows how much those posts meant. They were not just timber. They were the household itself, its luck and its honour, carried from one land to the next. And at Borg, one account of the excavation says that the little gold foils were found under the post where the high seat had stood.

Eyvind drops his armful of birch and driftwood by the hearth. Thórir nods at him. Álfhild says nothing, but she moves the wood a little further from the edge of the fire, and Eyvind sees her do it, and goes a little red, and says nothing either.

The fire burns on, low and orange in its long bed of stones. The smoke drifts up into the roof. And the thick earth walls hold the warmth in, all the way round, the way two cupped hands hold a candle flame out of the wind.

## Part Five — Around Half Past Five: The Loom and the Keys

Here is the small question for this hour. Ragnhild has a bunch of iron keys hanging at her belt, and they clink softly every time she moves. What do they open, and what do they mean?

We'll come to them. But first, listen.

Above the soft crackle of the long fire, there's another sound in the room, a sound you might not recognise at first. A rhythmic clacking, and a soft thud, and a pause, and a clacking again. It's coming from the far end of the room, near the wall, where the lamplight is brightest. Sigrid is weaving.

Imagine you go and stand beside her now. In front of her, leaning against the wall at a slant, is a loom. But it's not like any loom you've seen in a museum of the industrial age. It's tall, taller than Sigrid, two upright wooden posts with a beam across the top, and from that beam, hundreds of woollen threads hang straight down, side by side, like a curtain of strings. At the bottom, they're not tied to anything. Instead, they're gathered into little bunches, and each bunch is weighed down by a stone. A row of them, dangling just above the floor, swaying slightly, clicking against one another. Loom weights, made of baked clay, or soapstone, each one about the size of a small apple, with a hole through the middle.

This is a warp-weighted loom, and it's how cloth was made across the Norse world. The hanging threads are the warp. Sigrid passes another thread, the weft, back and forth between them, and then beats it upwards, towards the top, with a long, flat, smooth blade of wood or whalebone, the weaving sword. Thud. That's the sound you heard. Then she shifts a wooden rod that moves half the warp threads forward, so that the weft can pass back the other way. Clack. And again. Thud. Clack. The cloth grows downwards from the top, inch by slow inch.

The loom weights are among the most common things archaeologists find on Viking Age farm sites. Sometimes they're found still lying in a row on the floor, exactly where they fell when the loom collapsed or the house was abandoned, and you can see from their spacing how wide the cloth on the loom once was.

Sigrid is eleven, and she's good. Her grandmother taught her, and Álfhild is watching from the platform now with one eye, spindle turning in her hands, ready to say something if the edge of the cloth begins to wander. It doesn't.

The cloth on the loom is a heavy, tightly woven woollen fabric, the kind the Norse called *vaðmál*. It's the everyday cloth of the north, brown and grey and undyed cream, woven in a twill pattern, a diagonal weave, that makes it strong and dense and warm. It was so important, and so standard, that in Iceland in later centuries, lengths of *vaðmál* of a fixed quality were used as a kind of money, to pay debts and fines and rents. A household's wealth could be measured in cloth.

And now the keys.

Ragnhild is at the far end of the room, by a big wooden chest with an iron lock. She chooses a key from the bunch at her belt, opens the chest, and takes out a folded length of finer cloth, something she's been saving. She holds it up to the lamplight to look at it, and then folds it again and puts it away, and locks the chest.

Those keys are the answer to our small question. They open the chests where the best cloth and the silver are kept, the storerooms where the food for the winter lies, and the doors that matter. And they mean that Ragnhild is the mistress of the house. In the Norse world, the woman who held the keys held authority over the household's stores and its inner life, the food, the cloth, the dairy, the work of the women and the servants. When archaeologists find a Viking Age woman buried with keys, they often read it as a sign of exactly that: she was the one who ran the house. Thórir may speak for this household at the assembly, among the men of the district. But in here, among the barrels of fish and the tubs of butter and the bolts of cloth, it's Ragnhild's word that counts.

And keeping those stores, in a winter like this, is no small thing. Every morning, she decides how much of everything can be used. How much meal, how much butter, how much dried fish. How much hay can go to the cows. If she gets it wrong in December, the household will feel it in April.

Let's look at what everyone is wearing, because it's all come off that loom, or one like it.

Next to the skin, a long shirt or shift, of linen if you can afford it, made from flax, or of fine wool. Over that, for the men, a woollen tunic to the knees, belted at the waist, and woollen trousers, and on their lower legs, long strips of woollen cloth wound round and round from the ankle to the knee, like bandages. Leg wraps. Grím should have wound his tighter this morning, as Álfhild told him. For the women, a long woollen dress, and over it, a kind of apron dress, a pinafore, held up by two shoulder straps fastened at the front with a pair of large, domed, oval brooches of bronze, one below each collarbone. Strings of glass beads are often hung between them. Archaeologists have found pairs of these brooches by the thousand, in women's graves all over Scandinavia, and they are one of the most recognisable things in all of Viking Age archaeology. Over everything, for both men and women, a heavy cloak, pinned at the shoulder. And here in the north, furs. Sheepskin. Fox. Perhaps the skin of a bear.

That's four or five layers of wool, with air trapped between them, and every one of them spun by hand and woven on a loom like Sigrid's.

Not quite every one, though. On Ketil's feet, under his shoes, there's a pair of thick, lumpy, wonderfully warm socks, and they weren't woven at all. Álfhild made them with a single large needle of bone and a length of woollen yarn, looping the yarn through itself again and again in a way that looks a little like knitting, but is older, and slower, and very hard to unravel. It's called needle-binding, or in its Scandinavian name, *nålbinding*. Knitting as we know it hadn't yet reached the north. One of the most famous finds of all from the Viking world is a single needle-bound sock, found in the 1970s and 80s during the excavations at Coppergate, in the city of York, in the north of England, where Norse settlers lived in the nine hundreds. It's a plain, ordinary, well-worn woollen sock. And it has survived more than a thousand years in the wet ground of York, so that today you can see exactly what kept a Viking Age foot warm.

And the colours. Much of the cloth in this house is the natural colour of the sheep, the greys and browns and creams. But not all of it. The Norse knew how to dye. Blue came from woad, a plant whose leaves, fermented in a vat, give a blue that ranges from the palest sky to a deep navy. Red came from the roots of madder, which had to be grown or traded from the south, or from certain lichens gathered from the rocks. Yellow came from many wild plants. The dyeing was done in summer, outdoors, in great pots, and it smelled terrible. A cloak of good, deep, even blue or red was a sign that someone in this house had money, and time, and skill. Ragnhild's best cloak, folded away in that locked chest, is red.

The spinning that makes all of it possible goes on in the background, all evening, all winter, in every spare moment. Álfhild's spindle is a slender stick of wood, about as long as her forearm, with a small round weight near the bottom, a whorl, made of soapstone or of bone, to keep it turning. She sets it spinning with a flick of her fingers, lets it drop, and draws the wool out from a stick tucked under her arm, the distaff, so that the twist runs up into the fibres and turns them into thread. Spindle whorls, like loom weights, are found by the hundred on Viking Age sites. Every one of them was once in a woman's hand, turning, on an evening like this one.

Let's set that beside your own wardrobe. You probably own more clothes than everyone in this house put together. Much of what you wear on a cold day is made of fibres that would astonish Ragnhild: polyester fleece, nylon, down jackets in quilted shells. And a single jacket might cost you a few hours' wages. A cloak like Thórir's, from the shearing of the sheep to the last stitch, would have taken many weeks of somebody's work. But Ragnhild understood exactly what the makers of modern outdoor clothing understand: warmth comes from layers, from wool, and from the air held still between them.

And here's the most remarkable thing these looms made. Sails. The great square sails of Norse ships were woven of wool, on looms like this one, by women. When modern researchers and craftspeople have tried to make such sails again, in the traditional way, from the sheep to the mast, they have found that a single large sail could take an enormous amount of labour, many months, even years, of spinning and weaving. So when you picture a Norse ship on the sea, with its sail full of wind, you're looking at one of the greatest achievements of women's work in the early medieval world. Every voyage that left this coast, to the south, to England, to the islands of the west, was carried on cloth like this.

Now, I promised you earlier that we'd hear what the Arab traveller ibn Fadlan made of the Norse traders he met on the Volga in 922. Here it is. He called them the Rus, and in one well-known English translation, he writes that he had never seen more perfect physical specimens, tall as date palms, blond and ruddy. Another translation, by the scholar James Montgomery, says that they were like palm trees. And then, in almost the same breath, he describes them as the filthiest of God's creatures, and complains at length about their washing habits, which he found disgusting. He also tells us that each man carried an axe, a sword and a knife, and was never without them. I mention this because it's one of the very few eyewitness descriptions of Scandinavians from this period by someone from outside their world. And because it shows you how a single traveller could be dazzled and horrified at once. Ibn Fadlan's Rus were traders, far from home, living rough on a riverbank. I suspect that Ragnhild, who has a good comb of carved antler and makes her household wash, would have had something to say about it.

And there's a very different view, from the other side of the North Sea, which makes a nice balance. Combs made of antler are among the most common objects found on Viking Age sites, and they seem to have been used constantly, by men and women alike. And an English chronicle attributed to a writer known as John of Wallingford, from the thirteenth century, long after the events it describes, complains about the Danish settlers in England in a way that I find rather charming. I'll paraphrase: he grumbles that the Danes combed their hair every day, bathed every Saturday, and changed their clothes often, and that, by these means, they won the attention of English women. Whether the chronicler was fair to anyone is another matter. But the old Norse name for Saturday was *laugardagr*, which means washing day, and in Iceland, Saturday is still called by that name today.

And here's a point where archaeologists have disagreed very sharply in recent years.

In the 1870s, a grave was excavated at Birka, the great Viking Age trading town on an island in Lake Mälaren in Sweden. It's known as grave Bj 581. It held a sword, an axe, a spear, arrows, two horses, and a gaming board with pieces. For more than a century, everyone assumed it was the grave of a high-ranking male warrior. Then, in 2017, a team led by the archaeologist Charlotte Hedenstierna-Jonson published a study of the skeleton's DNA, and showed that the person buried there was genetically female. They argued that she had been a warrior, and perhaps an officer, a woman who held a role usually thought of as men's. The study caused a sensation. But other scholars questioned it. Some asked whether the bones that were tested really belonged to that grave, since the old excavation records were not perfect. Others, while accepting that the person was female, asked whether weapons in a grave meant that the person had actually fought, or whether they meant something else, about status, or family, or honour. The team published a further study in 2019, answering their critics. And the debate goes on, carefully, among people who respect each other's work. It doesn't tell us what most Norse women did, which was the work we're seeing in this room. But it reminds us that the Norse world may have held more variety than our tidy categories allow.

Álfhild's spindle turns and turns. Bera has fallen asleep on the sheepskins beside her grandmother, a small, round, warm heap with her thumb in her mouth, the carved wooden horse still clutched in her other hand. Snati the dog has curled up against her back.

Because it's nearly time to think about supper.

## Part Six — Around Half Past Six: Furs, Feathers and the People of the North

Here is the small question for this part of the night. Hanging on a peg in the storeroom, coiled like a sleeping snake, there's a long, heavy rope, dark and smooth and slightly greasy to the touch. It's not made of hemp, or flax, or anything that grew in a field. So what is it made of?

Let's go and look.

Imagine you follow Ragnhild now, with a lamp held up in her hand, out of the warm living room and along the cold passage to one of the storerooms. The air in here is icy. Your breath clouds in front of the lamp. And the room is full. Barrels and tubs line the walls. There are sacks of meal, and stacks of dried fish, and wooden boxes. And on pegs and poles, high up, out of the reach of mice, there are skins. Bundles of them. The thick, grey-brown pelts of reindeer. The glossy, dark, impossibly soft skins of pine marten, dozens of them, tied together in bundles. A white fox. Otter. And, folded over a pole by itself, heavy and shaggy and enormous, the skin of a bear.

And there's the rope, on its peg.

Ragnhild runs her hand along it. It's made of hide. One coil is made of strips cut from the skin of a whale, and another of sealskin, cut in a long continuous spiral from the animal's body, so that a single skin gives a single, very long, unbroken strip, which is then twisted and stretched and greased. A rope like this is enormously strong, and it doesn't rot in salt water the way a plant-fibre rope can. It's the rope you want on a ship in the northern seas.

That's the answer to our small question. And we know it because Ohthere told King Alfred about exactly these ropes, more than eleven hundred years ago.

Here's the heart of his account, and I'll give it in close paraphrase from modern translations. His wealth, he explained, came mainly from the tribute paid to him by the people he called the Finnas. The richest of them, he said, had to pay each year fifteen marten skins, five reindeer skins, one bear skin, and a coat made of bearskin or of otter skin, and two ship's ropes, each of them sixty ells long, one made of whale hide and the other of sealskin. And a quantity of feathers. Translations differ on how much: some give it as ten measures, and older ones as forty. The Old English word for the measure, *amber*, is itself uncertain. But the rest of the list is clear, and it's like an inventory of this storeroom.

An ell was a measure based on the length of a forearm, so sixty ells would be something over twenty-five metres of rope. Two of them, from one family, every year.

The Finnas, in Ohthere's account, are the people we now call the Sámi, the Indigenous people of the far north of Scandinavia, who have lived here for thousands of years. In Ohthere's time, many of them lived by hunting, fishing, trapping and fowling, moving through the great interior and along the coasts with the seasons. They knew the land in ways the Norse farmers did not. They knew where the marten ran and where the wild reindeer moved, how to trap and hunt through the winter, and how to travel over snow.

And Ohthere tells us something else, which surprises many people. He had reindeer of his own. About six hundred tame reindeer, he said, which had not yet been sold when he came to the king. And among them were six decoy reindeer, specially valuable to the Finnas, because they were used to lure and catch wild reindeer. So already, in the late eight hundreds, there were herds of tame reindeer in the north, and a farmer-chieftain like Ohthere was dealing in them.

Ohthere was not only a trader and a herder. He was also a voyager, and the most famous part of his account is a journey he made, out of curiosity, he said, to find out how far the land ran to the north, and whether anyone lived beyond the waste. As the account tells it, he sailed north along the coast, with the empty land always on his right and the open sea on his left, for days, past the furthest places where the whale hunters went, until the coast turned east, and then south, and he came at last into a great sea and to the land of a people the English text calls the Beormas, somewhere around what we now call the White Sea, in the far north of Russia. Part of his reason for going, the account says, was the walrus, because their teeth are made of a very fine bone, and he brought some of those teeth to the king as a gift. I'm giving this in paraphrase, because the Old English is difficult in places and translators differ in the details. But imagine it: a farmer from this coast, in an open ship, rounding the very top of Europe, more than a thousand years ago, and then sailing all the way to England to tell a king about it.

And he made a southern journey too. He described sailing south along the length of Norway to a trading place he called Sciringes heal, which scholars identify with Kaupang, on the Oslo fjord in the south of the country. It took him, he said, a month, if you camped at night and had a fair wind. And from there, he sailed on to the great market town of Hedeby, at the base of the Jutland peninsula. That's the sea road the furs in this storeroom will follow.

Long before that, another writer had heard of these northern people. In the sixth century, in Constantinople, the historian Procopius, writing his History of the Wars, included a description of a great island far to the north that he called Thule, which most scholars think was really the Scandinavian peninsula. Among its peoples, he said, were the Skrithiphinoi, who lived a wild life by hunting in the forests and mountains, and among whom, as one translation puts it, the men and the women together engage in nothing but hunting. The name is often understood as something like the skiing Finns, from an old Norse root meaning to slide or glide. It's one of the earliest descriptions of the Sámi anywhere, written down more than three centuries before our night by a man who never came within a thousand miles of them.

Imagine now that Ragnhild hands you one of the marten skins from the bundle, so that you can feel it in the lamplight. It's light, almost weightless, and the fur is so soft and so thick that your fingers sink into it and disappear. The colour is a deep, warm brown, almost black along the back, with a creamy patch at the throat. In the courts of England and France and the great markets of the south, a skin like this was a luxury of the very highest order. Kings and churchmen wore marten and sable at their collars and cuffs. Some of these skins, once they've been traded south down the coast, may end up on the shoulders of someone who has never heard of Lofoten.

So that's the economy of the far north, in one cold storeroom. Fish and fodder for the household itself. And furs and hides and feathers and ropes, gathered from the Sámi, and from the hunt, and from the sea, to be carried south and traded for silver, and glass, and fine pottery, and wine. That's how a chieftain on a narrow green strip between the mountains and the sea could afford glass beakers on his table and gold foil in his postholes.

Let's set that beside your own life for a moment. The things in your home come from all over the world. Your phone, your clothes, your coffee, your furniture. Most of us have no idea who made them, or where the raw materials came from, or how many hands they passed through. In Thórir's world, the chain was shorter and more visible. The marten in this storeroom was trapped by someone whose name Thórir knows, and it will be sold to someone whose ship he has seen. But it's the same human story. Goods moving across great distances, from the edge of the world to its centres, connecting people who will never meet.

And here's a point where historians disagree, and it's an important one.

How should we understand the relationship between the Norse and the Sámi? Ohthere describes it as tribute, paid to him by the Finnas, and paid according to their rank. To some modern readers, that sounds like domination: a powerful Norse chieftain taking wealth from a weaker people by threat. And there's certainly evidence of that kind of pressure in later centuries, and in the sagas. But other scholars have argued that the reality was more complicated, and more mutual. In their book Hunters in Transition, published in English in 2014, the historian Lars Ivar Hansen and the archaeologist Bjørnar Olsen argued that for much of this period, Norse and Sámi societies in the north were bound together by exchange, by marriage alliances, by shared trade, and by relationships in which both sides gained something, as well as by tribute. In this view, what Ohthere called tribute may have looked, from the Sámi side, rather more like a trading partnership with obligations on both sides, and the Norse chieftains of the north may have depended on the Sámi for their wealth at least as much as the Sámi depended on them. Archaeologists have also found that the line between Norse and Sámi on the ground was not always sharp, with objects and customs from both cultures turning up at the same sites. It's a debate that matters a great deal in northern Norway today, and it's carried on with care.

What's clear is that a house like Borg did not stand alone. Its wealth, its glass, its gold, rested on a web of relationships reaching up the coast and into the mountains, with people who spoke another language and lived another life.

There's one more thing on Ohthere's list that I love, and it's the feathers. Down and feathers from seabirds, from the eider ducks that nest along this coast in their thousands, were valuable for stuffing pillows and coverlets. The softest, the eiderdown, is still among the warmest natural materials known. Tonight, on the sleeping platforms back in the living room, some of the coverlets that Thórir's family will sleep under are filled with exactly that, and we'll feel them when it's time for bed.

Leaning in the corner of the storeroom, behind the barrels, there's one more thing you might not expect to find in a Viking house. A pair of skis. Long, narrow boards of pine, one a little longer than the other, with a leather strap across the middle for the foot, and beside them a single stout pole. People in the north had been travelling on skis for thousands of years before Thórir was born. Some of the oldest skis ever found, preserved in the peat bogs of northern Sweden and Finland and Russia, are many thousands of years old. In winter, when the snow lay deep on the hills, a pair of skis turned a day's hard wading into an hour's easy gliding. Eyvind uses these to go up into the birch woods after grouse, and to check the snares.

And skiing had its own gods. In the Norse myths, as they were written down in Iceland in the thirteenth century, there's a god called Ullr, a hunter and archer, said to be so skilled on skis that no one could match him. And there's a giantess, Skaði, who comes from the mountains, hunts on skis with a bow, and loves the snow and the howling of wolves. In one story, she marries a sea god, Njörðr, and they try to live together, but she can't bear the cry of the gulls on the shore, and he can't bear the wolves of the mountains, and in the end they part. It's a story about the mountains and the sea that might almost have been made on this very coast, where the two stand so close together. And there's an old poem, called Háleygjatal, the list of the people of Hålogaland, composed in the tenth century for the great earls of northern Norway, which traced their family line back to the god Odin and to Skaði herself. The powerful families of this coast, in other words, liked to say that they were descended from the giantess of the snow.

Ragnhild takes down a dried fish from the stack, then another, and another, and tucks them under her arm, and holds the lamp up one last time to look around the storeroom, the way Thórir looked at the hay in the byre. Counting. Checking. Then she nods, and turns, and the lamplight swings away, and the furs and the ropes and the barrels sink back into the cold dark.

Back in the living room, the warmth meets you like a hand on your shoulder. Thórir is mending something by the fire with an awl. Eyvind is sharpening a knife on a whetstone, slowly, with a soft, rasping sound. Ketil is lying on his stomach on the edge of the platform, watching him. Grím has gone to help Oddný in the dairy. And Bera is still asleep, curled up against the dog.

Ragnhild lays the dried fish on a board by the hearth. Ketil looks at them, and then at his mother, and asks, as children have always asked, whether it's nearly suppertime.

It is.

## Part Seven — Around Half Past Seven: Supper, and the Fish That Never Spoils

The small question for this part of the night is about time and hunger. In this house, how many meals a day do people eat, and when?

The answer, for most of the Norse world, was two. Two main meals. The first was called the *dagverðr*, the day-meal, eaten in the morning, after the first round of work was done, after the animals had been fed and milked. And the second was the *náttverðr*, the night-meal, eaten in the evening, when the day's work was over. The words survived for a very long time in the Scandinavian languages. There might be a bite of something in between, a crust or a cup of whey for someone working hard, but the household came together, properly, twice a day. And this is the second time.

That's the answer to our small question. Two meals, morning and evening. And in the long dark of December, when the morning and the evening are both dark, it's the rhythm of the meals, as much as anything, that tells the household what time of day it is.

Imagine you are sitting on the platform now, between Ketil and Sigrid, with your back against the warm planks and a wooden bowl in your hands. In front of you, Oddný and Ragnhild are serving. There's a big iron cauldron hanging over the long fire, on a chain from a beam, and from it comes a thick, steaming porridge, made of barley meal, cooked slowly in water and milk, with a lump of butter melting in the middle of each bowl. Barley could be grown here, in small patches on the best ground, in a good year, though this far north it was always a gamble. What isn't grown here is bought, or traded, from further south.

There's a dish of something white and thick and sour, which tastes a little like a very strong yoghurt. That's made from the milk of the summer, cultured and drained and kept. In Iceland, a food of this kind became famous as *skyr*, and it's still eaten there today. And there's a jug of sour whey, the thin, sharp liquid left over from cheese-making, which was drunk, and also used to preserve meat and other foods, because its acidity kept them from spoiling. In Iceland, the whey kept for this purpose was called *sýra*.

There's a little meat tonight, too. A few pieces of boiled mutton. And some seal, dark and rich and oily, which Ketil loves, and Sigrid hates.

And there, on the board by the hearth, is the fish.

Ragnhild picks up one of the dried fish from the storeroom. It's a whole cod, headless, split down the middle, and dried until it's grey-brown, and stiff, and as hard as a piece of wood. If you knocked on it with your knuckle, it would sound like a plank. She lays it on a flat stone by the hearth and takes up a wooden mallet and begins to beat it. Thud. Thud. Thud. Slowly, the hard flesh loosens, and breaks into fibres, and she can tear it apart with her fingers into long, pale, chewy strips. Then she hands them round, and everyone takes a piece, and dips it in melted butter, and eats.

It tastes of the sea. Strong, faintly sweet, and very chewy. A little like the smell of the shore at low tide, concentrated into something you can hold in your hand. Ketil tears his piece with his teeth, the way children do. Álfhild, who has fewer teeth than she used to, soaks hers in a bowl of whey first, to soften it.

This is stockfish. In Old Norse, *skreið*.

Before we go further with the fish, look around at the way this meal is eaten, because it's a little different from your own supper table.

There's no table, for a start, at least not a fixed one. On a night like this, the family simply sits on the platforms with bowls in their laps, or a board is set up on trestles in front of them and taken down again afterwards. Nobody has a fork. Forks won't come into ordinary use in the north for many centuries. Everyone has a spoon, often of horn or wood, and everyone past childhood carries their own knife, at the belt, all day, for everything: cutting rope, gutting fish, whittling a peg, and eating. Thórir's knife has a handle of antler and a blade he has sharpened so often that it's grown thin in the middle, like a crescent moon.

There's something to drink. Water, and whey, and on most evenings a little ale, brewed from malted barley, thick and cloudy and a little sweet. Tonight there's a jug of it, warming by the fire. In a few days' time, at Yule, there'll be a great deal more, and we'll talk about that later tonight. Mead, made from fermented honey, is a luxury here, because honey has to come from the south, where there are bees and flowers enough to make it. A chieftain might keep some for the feast days, poured into a drinking horn or one of those precious glass beakers. But not tonight.

And there's good advice about drinking in the Hávamál, too, which Thórir would know by heart. In Bellows's translation of 1923, one verse begins: "Less good there lies than most believe in ale for mortal men." The poem goes on to warn that the more a man drinks, the less he knows his own mind. It's advice for a guest in someone else's hall, who wants to keep his wits about him, and his good name. Many of the verses of the Hávamál are like that: practical, a little wry, the kind of thing a sensible grandmother might say. Be careful. Be generous. Don't stay too long. Don't drink too much. Speak when you have something to say.

The order of sitting matters, too, even at a family supper. Thórir sits in the middle of his side of the fire, where the high seat would be on a feast night. Álfhild sits at his right, closest to the warmth, by long habit and by right. The children are along the platform, the youngest nearest their mother. Grím and Oddný sit a little further along, part of the household, eating the same food from the same pot, but not quite in the same place. In a hall full of guests, those places would be carefully arranged by rank, and getting it wrong could cause offence that lasted years. The sagas are full of quarrels that began with who sat where.

And when the bowls are passed, nobody eats before the others are served. Ragnhild serves Álfhild first, then Thórir, then the children, then the household, and herself last. It's a small thing. But it's the shape of a family, made visible every evening around the fire.

Now. Back to the fish.

And so here, at last, is the second of our three mysteries. I asked you at the beginning of the night: why would a family rich enough to own twenty cows eat for their supper a fish so hard and dry that you could knock on it like a door, a fish that had never been near a grain of salt? And why might that very same kind of fish turn up on a table a thousand miles to the south?

Here's the answer, and it begins in the sea.

Every winter, beginning in the new year, great shoals of cod come down from the cold waters of the Barents Sea, in the far north, to spawn here, around the Lofoten Islands. They come in their millions. The Norwegians call this migrating cod *skrei*, from a word that seems to be related to moving or wandering. From January until spring, the fjords and the sounds between these islands are full of fish, and the people of the coast have fished them for as long as anyone knows. It's one of the great natural events of the northern world, and it's happening, every year, right on Thórir's doorstep.

But a fish is no good to you if it rots. So how do you keep it?

In most of Europe, you'd salt it. But salt, here, is precious. It had to be made by boiling seawater, which takes a great deal of fuel, and fuel, as we've seen, is precious too. And it turns out that in this particular place, you don't need salt at all. Because of the weather.

From late winter into spring, the air in Lofoten is cold, and dry, and windy, and the temperature hovers around freezing, a little above by day, a little below by night. And in those conditions, something remarkable happens to a cod that's been gutted and hung up in the open air on a wooden rack. It doesn't rot. It's too cold for the bacteria that cause rot to do their work, and too cold for flies. But it isn't so cold that it simply freezes solid and stays wet. Instead, over weeks and months, with the wind blowing through it, and the frost and the thaw working at it, the water slowly leaves the fish. By the summer, it has lost most of its weight, nearly all of it water, and what's left is hard, light, and very nearly everlasting. Kept dry, stockfish can last for years.

That's why Thórir's family eats it. Not because they're poor, but because it's the cleverest food in the north. It's last spring's fish, caught by Grím and Thórir and Eyvind in the cold months, hung on racks on the shore, and brought in, dry and hard, in the summer. It's this household's store of protein for the whole winter. It needs no salt, no smoke, no cooking, no barrel. It's light enough to carry. And it doesn't spoil.

And that's why it might turn up a thousand miles to the south. Because a food that's light, and doesn't spoil, and is full of nourishment, is the perfect thing to trade.

For a long time, historians believed that the great trade in stockfish from northern Norway began in the Middle Ages proper, after about 1100, when the merchants of Bergen and later the German traders of the Hanseatic League shipped it in vast quantities across Europe. It became a food of Lent, when meat was forbidden to Christians, and it was eaten from Germany to Italy to Portugal. Even today, dried cod from Lofoten is a traditional dish in Italy, where it's called *stoccafisso*.

But in 2017, a team of scientists led by Bastiaan Star, of the University of Oslo, published a study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences that pushed the story back much further. They took ancient DNA from cod bones excavated at Haithabu, the Viking Age trading town also known as Hedeby, near the modern border of Germany and Denmark, from layers dated to between about 800 and 1066. Haithabu is on the Baltic, a long, long way from Lofoten. And the DNA showed that the cod had not been caught in the Baltic. They belonged to the North-East Arctic cod population, the very same population that comes every winter to Lofoten to spawn. The fish had been carried, dried, something like a thousand miles to the south. Remember that Ohthere told King Alfred that he had sailed from his home to Hedeby. And here, in the mud of Hedeby's harbour, were the bones of fish from his own seas.

So the answer to our second mystery is this. The family eats a fish as hard as wood because the cold, dry wind of Lofoten has turned it into a food that never spoils. And that same fish turns up a thousand miles to the south because, even in the Viking Age, the people of this coast were already trading it.

And here, of course, is where the experts disagree.

Before that study, many historians had doubted that there was any large-scale trade in stockfish from the north as early as the Viking Age. And the press release that accompanied the study noted, quite openly, that how old the fish trade with northern Norway really is had long been disputed. Five bones is a small sample. A few fish carried south by a trader like Ohthere, as provisions for a voyage, or as a gift, is not the same as an organised trade. Some scholars have argued that the evidence shows the beginnings of the trade, but not yet anything like the great commercial flow of later centuries. Others see it as proof that a regular long-distance trade in dried fish was already running from the far north by the eight or nine hundreds, and that it was one of the hidden foundations of chiefly wealth on this coast. More bones, from more sites, are being studied. The answer is still coming in.

Let's set this beside your own kitchen for a moment. You probably have a refrigerator, and a freezer, humming away quietly in the corner, keeping your food from spoiling. They use electricity, every hour of every day. Thórir's freezer is the weather. His refrigerator is the north wind. And the food it makes lasts longer than anything in yours.

There's that poem again, the Hávamál, and the same verse we heard by the fire, about the traveller with frozen knees. Here's the second half of it. In a modern translation by the scholar Carolyne Larrington, it goes: "food and clothing are necessary for the man who's journeyed over the mountains." Fire first, then food and clothing. It's the oldest rule of hospitality in the north, and every guest who has ever come in out of the dark to this house has been treated by it.

Ketil wants more fish. He gets it. Bera has woken up for supper, and she sits on her mother's knee eating porridge from a horn spoon, and then, without any warning at all, falls asleep again with the spoon in her hand. Ragnhild takes it from her gently and lays her back down on the sheepskins beside the dog.

The bowls are wiped out with a handful of straw. The scraps go to Snati, who has been waiting, with great politeness, for exactly this moment. And the household settles back against the warm platforms, full, and warm, and drowsy, in the glow of the long fire.

It's a little after eight o'clock in the evening. Outside, the night is clear and very still. The stars are thick. And somewhere out over the northern mountains, very faintly, something is beginning to move in the sky.

But we'll come to that later.

## Part Eight — Around Half Past Eight: Stories, Runes and the Gold in the Postholes

Here is this hour's small question. In a house with no books, where nobody can read or write a page of text, how does a family remember its own history, back five, six, seven generations?

Listen to Álfhild, and you'll hear the answer.

Supper is over. The fire has been built up again, a little, with birch and a slow-burning slab of turf. The lamps are burning low. Sigrid has left her loom. Eyvind has put down his whetstone. Ketil is lying on his back on the platform with his head in his sister's lap. And Álfhild, sitting at the end nearest the fire, with her spindle at last laid aside, has begun to talk.

She's telling them about her grandfather's grandfather. A man who came up the coast from the south, long ago, in a ship with a striped sail, and who took land on this island, and who was so strong, she says, that he could carry a full-grown ram under each arm up the slope from the shore. She tells them who he married, and what their sons were called, and which of the sons went west over the sea and never came back. She tells them about a feud, settled with silver, three generations ago, and about the man whose sword is still kept in the chest by the wall. She doesn't hesitate. She doesn't forget. She's told these stories before, many times, on many winter nights, and she heard them from her own grandmother in this same room.

That's the answer to our small question. A family like this remembers by telling. Over and over, on long winter evenings, by the fire. The names, the marriages, the quarrels, the voyages. Old people were the libraries of the north. And the long dark of winter, with its many hours of firelight and very little work that could be done outdoors, was the great season of telling.

Some of it was in poetry. The Norse had a special kind of verse, called skaldic poetry, made by court poets, the skalds, for chieftains and kings. It was fiendishly complex, full of rules about rhythm and rhyme and alliteration, and full of riddling poetic names for things, called kennings. The sea might be called the whale's road. A ship, the horse of the waves. Gold, the fire of the sea. Blood, the dew of the sword. And the kennings could be stacked, one inside another, until a listener had to work them out like a riddle, step by step, with a pleasure that is hard for us to imagine now. A good skald could make a whole hall go quiet, and then roar with approval, when the meaning clicked into place.

The skalds were rewarded well for it. A poem praising a chieftain's generosity might earn the poet a gold ring, or a fine sword, or a ship. And a poem mocking him might cause a feud. Words, in this world, were taken very seriously indeed. Under the later Icelandic laws, certain kinds of insulting verse about a person were treated as a serious offence. A verse, once spoken, could not be taken back. It would be remembered. Because the rules were so strict, a skaldic verse was very hard to change without breaking it. And that made it a remarkably good way of carrying a memory, unaltered, across centuries. Many of the poems composed in the Viking Age for the great men of Norway were only written down in Iceland two or three hundred years later, by people who had learned them by heart from people who had learned them by heart.

And there was writing, of a kind. Thórir can carve runes. The runic alphabet used in Scandinavia in the Viking Age, which scholars call the Younger Futhark, had only sixteen letters, fewer than our own. Runes were carved into wood, bone and stone, not written with ink. They were used for short things: a name on a comb, a message on a stick, a memorial on a standing stone, raised by a family for someone who had died, often with a line about where they had travelled. Thousands of rune stones survive, most of them in Sweden, and many of them from later than our night. But you don't write a family's whole history in runes. For that, you have Álfhild.

Imagine you are lying on the sheepskins now, at the edge of the firelight, listening. You can't follow all the names. There are too many. But you can follow the voice, slow and steady and sure, rising a little at the exciting parts, falling a little at the sad ones. And you can see the children's faces, turned towards her, orange in the glow. Ketil's eyes are wide. Sigrid's lips move silently as she repeats the names to herself, trying to fix them, because one day she'll be the one telling them. The fire crackles. Outside, the wind has begun to rise a little, and you can hear it, faintly, moving over the turf of the roof.

Let's set this beside your own life for a moment. Many of us know the names of our grandparents, and perhaps our great-grandparents. Beyond that, for most families, the memory runs out, unless someone has gone looking in old records. We have photographs, and videos, and family trees online, and all of it sits quietly in boxes and on hard drives, mostly unlooked-at. Álfhild has none of it. But she can name seven generations, with their wives and their children and their quarrels, without pausing. She is the hard drive, and the album, and the archive, and she's sitting right there by the fire.

Some of the stories told on this coast survived, in their own way, far longer than Álfhild. Centuries later, in Iceland, writers set down long prose stories about the people of the Viking Age, and some of them are set right here, in Hålogaland, in Thórir's own time. We call them the sagas.

One of the greatest is Egils saga, the saga of Egill Skallagrímsson. It was written down in Iceland in the thirteenth century, probably some time between about 1220 and 1240, and many scholars think its author may have been the great Icelandic writer Snorri Sturluson, though that's not certain. And its early chapters take place on this very coast, in the reign of King Harald Fairhair, in the late eight hundreds, the age of our story. They tell of a man called Thorolf Kveldulfsson, who came north and married the widow of a Hålogaland chieftain, and so became master of a great farm at Sandnes, on the coast to the south of here. In the saga, Thorolf collects tribute from the Sámi in the far north, and trades in furs and skins, and sends a ship south laden with the goods of the north. And here's our point of disagreement tonight, and it's one of the oldest arguments in all of Norse studies. How far can we trust the sagas?

They were written down two, three, even four hundred years after the events they describe. They're full of vivid dialogue, private conversations, and inner thoughts, which no one could possibly have remembered word for word across the centuries. And yet they're also full of names, places, family lines and details that sometimes match what archaeologists find in the ground. In the early twentieth century, scholars argued fiercely about this. One side, associated especially with the Swiss scholar Andreas Heusler, who named the two positions, imagined the sagas as written versions of stories that had been passed down by word of mouth, more or less faithfully, for generations, before being written down. Heusler called this the free-prose theory. The other side, the book-prose view, championed by the Icelandic scholar Sigurður Nordal and others, argued that the sagas were essentially literary works, composed by thirteenth-century authors who used old traditions as raw material, but shaped them, invented freely, and wrote them as artists, for their own time. Nordal made a famous case study of one short saga, Hrafnkels saga, published in 1940, to argue that it was largely fiction. Most scholars today take a middle way. The sagas, they'd say, are thirteenth-century works of art, but built on real traditions, real names and real memories, some of which go back to the Viking Age. ## Part Nine — Around Nine O'Clock: The Lights in the North

Here is the small question for this part of the night, and it's one that people asked for thousands of years. What are those lights in the sky?

Let's go and look at them.

Imagine you are following the children out through the door now, pulling a borrowed cloak tight around you, your feet pushed hastily into someone's shoes. The cold catches in your throat. Ragnhild is standing a few paces from the house, with Bera, wrapped up in a fur, in her arms. Sigrid and Ketil run out past her and stop. Eyvind follows, and then Thórir, and even Álfhild comes to the doorway and leans there, her shawl around her head.

There's no moon tonight. I worked that out, by the way, with a modern astronomical model, because no one wrote down the moon over Borg on this night in 890. The new moon fell two days ago, and the thin crescent that follows it doesn't climb above the mountains at all tonight. So the sky is as dark as a sky can be, and full of stars. More stars than you've probably ever seen. The Milky Way runs across the sky like a frosted river. The great group of stars the English call the Plough, and Scandinavians have long called a wagon, is wheeling up in the north-east, around the Pole Star, which here, so far north, sits high up in the sky, not low near the horizon as it does in southern England or most of the United States.

A dog barking, very faintly, on a farm on the other side of the bay. The creak of the snow under someone's foot. And, if you hold your breath, the sound of your own heart.

The cold out here has a particular feel. On a clear, still, moonless night like this one, the ground loses its warmth to the open sky hour after hour, and the air settles, colder and colder, into the hollows. The hairs inside your nose begin to stiffen when you breathe in. Your eyelashes feel heavy. If you stood here for an hour without moving, you would begin to be in real trouble. But you won't. Nobody here would let you.

And over the northern mountains, there's a light.

It begins as a faint, pale, greenish arc, low across the northern sky, like the glow of a town beyond the hills, except that there's no town, no town anywhere for hundreds of miles. Then, as you watch, the arc brightens. It grows. It lifts. It begins to move. Long, folded curtains of light, green, pale as frost at the bottom edge, ripple slowly across the sky from west to east, like a cloth being shaken out very slowly in a wind you can't feel. Here and there, at the tops of the curtains, there's a faint wash of red, or violet. The light brightens and dims and brightens again. The snow on the hills turns faintly green. Ketil's mouth is open. Bera, in her mother's arms, points at it, and says something no one can understand, and everyone laughs, very softly, as if they might frighten it away.

The northern lights. In Old Norse, they're called *norðrljós*, northern light.

And we have one medieval Norse text that tries to explain them. It's called the King's Mirror, in Old Norse *Konungs skuggsjá*, a book of instruction written in Norway around the middle of the thirteenth century, in the form of a conversation between a father and his son. The father teaches his son about trade, and the sea, and the duties of a king's man. And when the son asks about the wonders of the far north, the father tells him about Iceland, and Greenland, and the northern lights. It was translated into English by Laurence Marcellus Larson, in 1917.

I'll paraphrase, because the passage is long. The father says that the lights grow brighter as the night grows darker, and that they appear only at night. And then he admits that no one really knows what causes them. He gives three explanations that people have offered. Some say, he reports, that fire circles round the ocean that surrounds the world, and that its glow reaches up into the sky in the north, where the land ends. Others say that the light of the sun, travelling under the edge of the world at night, throws up a glow from below. And a third explanation is that the ice and the frost of the far north grow so great that they give out a light of their own. The father doesn't choose between them. He sets them out, carefully, and leaves the question open. And I find that lovely. Here's a thirteenth-century Norwegian, on the very edge of the known world, looking at one of the great mysteries of the sky, and saying, honestly, here are the possibilities, and I don't know.

And the answer to our small question, as we now understand it, is stranger than any of his three. The sun is constantly throwing off a thin stream of electrically charged particles into space, the solar wind. When those particles reach the Earth, most of them are turned aside by the Earth's magnetic field. But near the magnetic poles, some of them are funnelled down into the upper atmosphere, a hundred kilometres and more above the ground, where they collide with the oxygen and nitrogen of the air and make it glow, the way the gas in a neon sign glows. Oxygen at those heights gives off that pale green light, and at greater heights, a deep red. Nitrogen can add blues and violets. And because the lights cluster in a great ring around the magnetic pole, a ring that passes right over northern Norway, Borg is one of the best places on Earth to see them.

So the King's Mirror was not entirely wrong, in a way. The lights do come from the sun. Just not in the way anyone in the thirteenth century could have guessed.

Let's set this beside your own life. If you live in a town or a city, you may never have seen the northern lights at all, and you may not have seen the Milky Way either, because the glow of streetlights washes them out of the sky. Many people now travel thousands of miles, to Norway, to Iceland, to Canada, and watch apps on their phones that forecast the aurora, just to stand in the cold for a few minutes and see what Thórir's children are seeing, for free, from their own front door, on any clear winter night.

Now, the King's Mirror has more to say about the far north, and it brings us to Greenland, and to one of the great unsolved questions of Norse history.

In 890, Greenland is still unknown to the Norse. But about a century from now, in the 980s, according to the Icelandic sources, a man called Eirík the Red will lead settlers there from Iceland, and they'll build farms in the green fjords of the south-west, in two areas that came to be called the Eastern and the Western Settlements. They'll farm cattle and sheep and goats, hunt seals and caribou, and sail far to the north to hunt walrus for their ivory. For more than four hundred years, the Norse will live in Greenland. And then they'll vanish. The Western Settlement seems to have been abandoned by around the middle of the thirteen hundreds. The last written record from the Eastern Settlement is of a wedding, held at the church at Hvalsey in 1408. After that, nothing. When Europeans came looking again, centuries later, they found only ruins.

Why did they disappear? Here, the scholars disagree, and they have for a very long time. In his book Collapse, published in 2005, the geographer Jared Diamond argued that the Greenland Norse were partly undone by their own choices: clinging to cattle-farming and to European ways in a land that was growing colder, damaging their fragile soils, and failing to learn from the Inuit, who were thriving nearby. Many archaeologists who have worked on the Greenland sites for decades have pushed back against parts of that picture, showing that the Norse in fact adapted a great deal, eating far more seal and much less beef as the centuries went on. Others have pointed to wider causes: the cooling of the climate in the later Middle Ages; the collapse of the European market for walrus ivory, which had been one of Greenland's main exports, as elephant ivory from Africa became easier to get, an idea supported by studies of ancient DNA from walrus ivory in recent years; and, in a study published in 2023, the possibility that the land itself was sinking, and the sea rising along the coast, as the great ice sheet nearby grew heavier. Most scholars today think that many things came together, slowly, and that the end may have come not in a sudden disaster, but in a gradual leaving, as families sailed away and did not return. It's a sad, quiet mystery. And it's still being worked on, at the ruins and in the laboratories, every year.

But that's all far in the future. Tonight, the lights are moving over Borg.

Now, that study of the Rök stone I mentioned, about a fear of a winter that would not end, brings us to one more disagreement, and it's about a real winter, three and a half centuries before our night.

In the Norse myths, the end of the world, Ragnarök, is said to be preceded by a terrible winter, three winters in a row with no summer between them, called the Fimbulwinter, the great or mighty winter. For a long time, that was simply taken to be a myth. But in recent decades, scientists studying tree rings and the ice cores drilled from Greenland and Antarctica have found clear evidence that in the year 536, and again a few years later, around 540, great volcanic eruptions threw so much ash and sulphur into the atmosphere that the sun was dimmed across much of the northern hemisphere. Summers failed. Tree rings in Scandinavia and elsewhere grow very thin in those years. A study led by the scientist Michael Sigl, published in the journal Nature in 2015, used the ice cores to date the eruptions precisely. In Scandinavia, archaeologists have found signs that many farms were abandoned in the sixth century, and that the population may have fallen sharply.

So did the memory of those terrible years live on, for centuries, in the story of the Fimbulwinter? In 2012, the archaeologists Bo Gräslund and Neil Price published a paper in the journal Antiquity arguing that it might have done: that the dust veil of 536 was a disaster so great that it left its mark on Norse myth. Other scholars are more cautious. Myths of a world-ending winter, they point out, could arise in many ways, and the poems that describe Ragnarök were written down seven hundred years after 536. Linking a particular myth to a particular eruption is very hard to prove. It's a fascinating idea, and it's still being argued over. And it's worth remembering that this very house at Borg was first built in the five hundreds, in that same century. Its first builders may have lived through those dark summers.

In the Norse myths, as Snorri Sturluson set them down in Iceland in the thirteenth century, the stars were not all just stars. Some of them had stories. ## Part Ten — Around Ten O'Clock: Counting the Nights

Here is the small question for this hour. In the corner of the living room, near the warm end of the long fire, there's a great wooden vat, covered with a cloth, and every so often Ragnhild lifts the cloth and looks inside and sniffs. What's in it, and what is it for?

Let's go and see.

Imagine you are standing beside Ragnhild as she lifts the cloth. A warm, sweet, yeasty smell rises up at you, like bread dough, like a bakery in the early morning, with something sharper underneath. Inside the vat, a thick, brown, cloudy liquid is slowly working, with a pale foam on its surface and tiny bubbles rising through it, popping softly. It's alive. You can hear it, if you lean close. A faint, endless, fizzing whisper.

It's ale. And it's not for tonight.

It's for Yule.

That's the answer to our small question. In a few days' time, the household at Borg will keep the great midwinter feast, and for that, there must be ale, and plenty of it. The barley was malted weeks ago, soaked and sprouted and dried by the fire. It was ground and mashed with hot water, and the sweet liquid strained off, and herbs added, and then the yeast, saved from the last brewing. Now it's fermenting, here by the warmth of the fire, where it won't get too cold to work. In a few days it will be drawn off into barrels. And at Yule, the whole district will come to Borg, to the great hall in the middle of the house, and drink it.

What was Yule, in the Viking Age, before Christianity came to Norway? Here we have to be careful, because almost everything we know about it was written down later, by Christians. But there's one famous passage I want you to hear about.

It comes from Heimskringla, the great history of the kings of Norway, written in Iceland around 1230, and traditionally attributed to Snorri Sturluson. In the part of it called the saga of Hákon the Good, which tells of a Christian king of Norway who ruled in the middle of the nine hundreds, a few decades after our night, Snorri explains that in heathen times, Yule was held beginning on a night called *hǫkunótt*, which he describes as midwinter night, and that it was kept for three nights. And then he tells us that King Hákon, who had been brought up as a Christian in England, made a law that Yule should begin at the same time as the Christians kept Christmas, and that every man should have a measure of ale for it, or pay a fine, and keep it holy while it lasted. I'll paraphrase that, because the wording of the law in the saga is not something I could check exactly. But the idea is clear. Even in a Christian king's law, as Snorri tells it, the old midwinter feast was not abolished. It was moved, and blessed, and kept, ale and all.

And here, as you might guess, is where the scholars disagree.

When, exactly, was the heathen Yule? Snorri says it began on midwinter night. But what did midwinter mean? In the old Norse reckoning, the year was divided into two halves, winter and summer, and midwinter might mean the middle of the winter half of the year, counted in weeks from its beginning in the autumn, which could fall in January, rather than the winter solstice in late December. The great nineteenth-century dictionary of Old Icelandic by Richard Cleasby and Gudbrand Vigfusson suggested that *hǫkunótt* fell around the time of Epiphany, in early January, and that the heathen Yule may have been celebrated some weeks later than Christmas, until King Hákon moved it. Others have argued that the feast was tied to the solstice, the turning of the sun, and that this was the very point of it. And the text of the saga itself is uncertain in places; some manuscripts may say the feast lasted three nights, and some scholars have wondered whether it once said thirteen. It's very likely, too, that different districts kept it differently. So when Thórir's household holds its Yule, a few nights from now, it may be at the solstice, or it may be weeks later. We simply don't know. In our story, I've let it fall just after the longest night. That's a choice, and I want you to know it's one.

What we do know is what Yule was for. Snorri, in another passage, describes the great heathen feasts as being held for a good year and for peace, *til árs ok friðar*, as the phrase is usually given. A good year and peace. In a place like this, at the dead point of winter, with the hay counted and the stores half gone and the sun nowhere to be seen, it's hard to think of anything a household would want more.

Let's set this beside your own winter for a moment. Many of us, in the dark weeks of December, do something not so different. We light candles and strings of lights. We bring evergreen trees into the house. We gather the family, and eat and drink more than we should, and give gifts, and wish one another a good new year. The word Yule itself survives in English, in yuletide, and in the yule log, and in the Scandinavian languages, Christmas is still called *jul*. In the darkest days of the year, people in the north have always made a feast. It's one of the oldest human answers to the dark.

Before we follow them to bed, I want to tell you a little about how this household thinks about the year itself, because it's not quite the way you or I do.

For the Norse, the year had two halves, not four seasons. There was winter, and there was summer, and each was called a *misseri*, a half-year. Winter began in the autumn, in what we'd call the middle of October, with a feast called the Winter Nights, *vetrnætr*, when the animals were brought in, the surplus beasts slaughtered, and the household settled down for the dark. And summer began in the spring, around the middle of April. There was no great fuss about the months as we know them. The months that did have names, as they were later recorded in Iceland, were named for what happened in them. There was a month for slaughtering, in the autumn. There was a month for the lambing, in the spring. And there was a month in the middle of the winter, which in later Icelandic tradition was given the wonderful name *mörsugur*, which seems to mean something like the fat-sucker, or the marrow-sucker, because by then the household was living on the fat and the stores laid down in the autumn. I can't promise that Thórir used that very name here in Lofoten in 890; the names we have come from later Icelandic sources. But the idea would have made perfect sense to him.

And the Norse counted their time not by days, as we do, but by nights. A journey might take five nights. A feast might last three. A man's age was given in winters. That habit is very old. Almost eight hundred years before our night, in about the year 98, the Roman historian Tacitus, writing a short book about the peoples of Germania, the lands north of the Roman frontier, noticed it, and was struck enough to write it down. In his Latin, he says that they do not count the number of days, as the Romans do, but of nights, and that for them, the night seems to lead the day. I'm paraphrasing his Latin there, but that's the sense of it. The night came first. The day followed it. It's an idea that sits very naturally in a place like Borg, where in December the night is nearly all there is.

There's something else that the household watches, through the long dark, and that's the moon. With no sun to mark the days, the moon is the great clock of the polar winter. Its waxing and waning give the weeks their shape. Tonight there's no moon at all. But in a week or so, as the moon grows towards full, it will rise high over the mountains and stay up for most of the day and the night together, in some winters circling the sky without setting at all, and the snow will shine under it as bright as a winter dusk. Moonlight, here, is a kind of daylight. People travel by it, and work by it, and visit their neighbours by it. A full moon in the polar night is one of the great gifts of the year.

Let's set all of this beside your own sense of time. You probably know, without thinking about it, today's date, and the day of the week, and the hour, and very likely the minute. It's written on the corner of every screen you look at. Thórir doesn't know the date in our sense at all. He doesn't need to. But he knows, to the night, how long it is until Yule, and how long until the sun comes back, and how long until the skrei come to the fjord, and how long the hay must last. His calendar isn't written down anywhere. It's in his head, and his mother's head, and in the sky.

So here, at last, is the third of our three mysteries. I asked you at the very beginning of the night: in a place where nobody has seen the sun for weeks, why is no one in this house afraid that it will never come back?

Because they count.

That's the heart of the answer. The people of the north kept the year by counting, carefully, in nights and weeks and winters. They spoke of a person's age in winters, a man of twenty winters. They counted time in nights, the way English still does in the old word fortnight, which means fourteen nights. And they knew, from generations of watching, exactly how many nights the sun stayed away, and where it would first reappear.

By my own modern calculation, at Borg, on a perfectly flat sea horizon, the rim of the sun would have disappeared in this year about a week before the solstice, and returned about a week after it. But Borg doesn't have a flat horizon. It has mountains to the south. So the real return of the sun, to the people of this house, would have come later, on whatever day the light first cleared a particular ridge, and every farm along the coast would have had its own day, and its own spot to watch for it. In northern Norway today, many towns still hold a sun day, a little celebration on the day when the sun first comes back over the mountains.

And we have an old account of exactly this. Remember Procopius, the historian from Constantinople, writing in the sixth century, who described the Sámi as hunters on skis? In the same passage about Thule, the great northern island that most scholars think was Scandinavia, he wrote that the sun there did not rise for about forty days in winter. And he described what the people did. In the English translation by H. B. Dewing, he tells how, when thirty-five days of this long night have passed, "certain men are sent to the summits of the mountains," and when they are able from there barely to see the sun, they bring back word to the people below that within five days the sun will shine upon them. And then, he says, the people "celebrate in the dark a festival which in ceremony exceeds all their others."

Procopius adds that, even so, the people of Thule feared each year that the sun might desert them entirely. Perhaps they did, a little. Or perhaps that was how it looked to a man in a warm southern city, hearing about it at second hand. But look at what he actually describes. A people who counted the days. Who knew when to send someone up the mountain. Who knew, to the day, when the light would come. And who held their greatest festival of the year, not when the sun returned, but in the dark, just before, because they knew it was coming.

And the Icelanders, a little later, took the counting even further. In the Book of the Icelanders, the *Íslendingabók*, written by the priest Ari Thorgilsson in the 1120s or early 1130s, Ari tells how the wisest men of Iceland had counted the year as fifty-two weeks, three hundred and sixty-four days, and noticed, over the years, that the summer was slowly creeping backwards towards the spring. A man named Thorsteinn Surtr proposed that a whole extra week should be added to the summer every seventh year, to put it right, and the assembly made it law, in the middle of the nine hundreds. A whole people, watching the sky, and counting, and correcting their count.

So that's our third mystery solved. Nobody in this house is afraid the sun won't come back, because they have counted its absence every winter of their lives, and it has always come back, on time, over the same ridge. The dark is long. But it has an end, and they know where it is.

Álfhild finishes counting, and folds her hands, and Ketil closes his eyes.

And the house begins, at last, to go to bed.

## Part Eleven — Around Eleven O'Clock, and on into the Small Hours: The Sleeping Benches

Here is a small, gentle question for this part of the night. Now that the stories are told and the lights in the sky have faded, where does everyone in this house lie down, and who will be the last one awake?

Let's find out, slowly.

Imagine that you are lying on the wide platform along the wall now, the one you sat on at supper. It's been made up for the night. Underneath you, a thick layer of hay and heather, which crackles softly whenever you move, and over it, a woollen blanket, and then sheepskins, fleece side up, deep and soft and smelling of sheep and smoke. Over you, a heavy coverlet of woven wool, and on top of that, for the coldest nights, a fur. At your head, a bolster stuffed with feathers. And the coverlet itself, the one Ragnhild has tucked around the children, is filled with down, the soft grey down of the eider ducks from the shore, the same feathers that Ohthere listed among the riches of the north. It's light. Almost weightless. And it's very warm.

That's the answer to our small question, or the first half of it. Everyone sleeps on the long platforms, along the walls, either side of the long fire. Close to one another, for warmth. The children together, in a row, Bera in the middle where it's warmest. Álfhild at the end nearest the hearth, where she always is. Thórir and Ragnhild a little apart, at the far end, behind a hanging of woven wool that gives them a little privacy. In some houses, the master and mistress might have had a separate small bed-closet, a little room with a door, built into the end of the hall. Grím and Oddný have their places further along the platform, near the door, each with their own blankets and skins.

The second half of the answer, who will be last awake, we'll find out together.

The long fire has been banked for the night. Thórir has drawn the embers together into the middle of the hearth, and laid slabs of turf over them, and covered them with ash. Under that blanket, the fire won't burn, exactly. It will smoulder, very slowly, all night long, giving off a gentle, steady warmth through the stones, and in the morning there'll still be live embers at its heart. A faint red glow shows here and there along the line of the hearth, through cracks in the ash. That's all the light there is now. The lamps are out. The house is dark.

Bera, of course, is first. She was asleep before anyone else even lay down, a warm, heavy little bundle in the middle of the children's row, her breath slow and soft, the wooden horse still in her fist. Nobody has managed to take it from her all evening.

Then Ketil. He lies on his back for a while, with his eyes open in the dark, thinking about the sun coming back over the notch between the two peaks, and about Yule, and about the ale, and about whether he'll be allowed to stay up for the feast. His thoughts get slower. They begin to tangle together, the sun and the ale and the lights in the sky and the gold in the postholes, into something soft that doesn't quite make sense any more. His breathing deepens. His hand slides off the edge of the coverlet.

Ketil is asleep.

Let's set this beside your own bed for a moment. Many people today fall asleep to some kind of sound, a fan, or a white-noise machine, or an app that plays the sound of rain, or of the sea, or perhaps a calm voice telling a long, slow story about a Viking house on a winter night. All of these are, in a way, trying to give us back something this household never had to think about. They have the sound of other people breathing. The soft creak of the platform. The faint, endless whisper of the ale in the vat. The tiny shifting of the ash on the hearth. And from far away at the other end of the house, through the walls, the slow, deep, contented breathing of twenty cows. These are the oldest sleep sounds there are.

Sigrid goes next. She's lying on her side, facing the wall, her hand flat against the cool planks, thinking about the names in her grandmother's story, saying them over to herself, very quietly, one after another, so as not to lose them. The man with the rams. His wife. Their sons. The one who went west. Somewhere around the fourth generation, the names begin to slip. She tries to hold onto them. And then she doesn't need to any more.

Sigrid is asleep.

I'd like to share something gentle with you now, about the name of this place, because it's a small and pleasant puzzle. The name Lofoten, for the whole chain of islands, comes, by the usual explanation, from an Old Norse name, *Lófótr*, which was originally the name of just one island, the very island Borg stands on, the one we now call Vestvågøy. And *Lófótr* is usually understood to mean the lynx's foot, from *ló*, an old word for the lynx, and *fótr*, foot. Some say the island's shape, with its long, splayed headlands, looked like a lynx's paw. Others have wondered whether the name meant something else entirely, and place-name scholars have offered other readings over the years. But I like the lynx. I like the idea of the whole great chain of islands, stretching out into the Norwegian Sea, named after the soft, wide, silent paw of a cat that walks on snow.

Out in the byre, Oddný has finished her last round. You hear her come in, very quietly, through the door at the end of the room, and the soft sounds of her unpinning her cloak, and lying down on her place on the platform, and pulling the blankets over her. She sighs, once.

Oddný is asleep.

Grím takes a little longer. He lies on his back with his hands behind his head, as you did earlier, looking up into the dark where the roof must be. He's thinking about the fishing. The skrei will come in a few weeks, and he'll be out in the boat every day it's calm, with Thórir and Eyvind, and the racks on the shore will fill. He's thinking about his own father's farm, across the fjord, and whether he'll have a farm of his own one day. And then he isn't thinking about anything. His knees, which ached so badly this afternoon, don't ache at all.

Grím is asleep.

There's another gentle puzzle I'd like to leave with you, and it's about the very word for the feast that's coming. Yule. In Old Norse, *jól*. In Old English, *geol*. It's one of the oldest words for a feast in the northern languages. And nobody knows for certain where it comes from. The old Icelandic dictionary of Cleasby and Vigfusson said simply that its etymology was not known. Over the years, scholars have proposed connections with words for a wheel, as in the turning of the year, or for merriment, or for a sacrifice, or for something else again. None of them has been proven. So the word we still use, in yuletide and the yule log, is a small mystery that has come down to us through more than a thousand winters, its meaning lost, its warmth intact.

Eyvind is still awake. He's fifteen, and at fifteen, the night is full of thoughts. He's thinking about the lights over the mountains, and about Ohthere, whose name he's heard in the stories, the man from up the coast who sailed all the way to the land of the English and spoke to their king. He's thinking that one day, perhaps, he'll go south too, on a ship with a woollen sail his sister wove, and see the markets of Hedeby, and the towns of the English, and come home with a glass beaker of his own. He lies there for a long while, in the dark, with his eyes open, travelling.

And then the ship he's sailing in drifts out of the harbour of his thoughts, into a wide, calm, dark sea, and he lets it go.

Eyvind is asleep.

Álfhild is lying on her side, as she always does, facing the hearth, watching the faint red line of light along the ash. She has watched that line every night for fifty years. She murmurs something under her breath, a few words, very old, perhaps to her husband, who is in his mound above the shore, perhaps to someone else. Her eyes close. They open again, to check the ash. They close.

There's one more verse of the Hávamál that I want you to hear before the house is quite asleep, and I'll give it in my own plain rendering, because I want to keep it simple. It's the sixty-eighth verse, and in the Old Norse, it begins with the words *Eldr er beztr*. Fire is best. In my own words, the verse says something like this: fire is best among the children of men, and the sight of the sun; and good health, if a person can keep it, and a life lived without shame. That's all. Fire, and the sun, and health, and a good name. On the longest night of the year, in a house at the edge of the Arctic, with the sun gone and the fire banked, I can't think of a better prayer.

Álfhild is asleep.

In the bed at the far end, behind the woollen hanging, Thórir has turned onto his back. He's thinking about the hay. Of course he is. He's thinking about the red cow with the white face. About the ale, and whether there'll be enough. About who will come to Yule from across the bay. And then about Ragnhild, warm beside him, and the children asleep in their row, and his mother by the fire, and the animals safe at the other end of the house, and the stores counted, and the sun only a few weeks away. He lets out a long breath, the same long, deep sigh he gave when he first sat down by the fire this afternoon.

Thórir is asleep.

And Ragnhild is the last. Of course she is. She lies there for a while longer, listening to the house, the way she does every night. She can hear them all. Bera's quick, light breathing. Ketil's. Sigrid's. Eyvind's slower, deeper breath. Grím's faint snore. Oddný's. Álfhild's, whistling a little, the way old people's breath does. Thórir's, beside her, slow and heavy. Snati, by the hearth, whimpering a little in a dream. And beyond the walls, the cows, and the horses, and the sheep, all breathing in the warm dark.

She counts them, one by one, the way Álfhild counts the nights. All here. All warm. All safe.

Imagine you are lying there with her, a guest in this house, on this one night of all the nights in history, listening too. The down coverlet is light and warm. The sheepskin is soft beneath you. The air on your face is cool, but the rest of you is warm, wrapped and held in wool and down and fleece and the warmth of other people close by. Your breathing slows. It begins to match theirs. In, and out. In, and out.

Ragnhild's hand, which was resting on the keys at her belt, laid beside her pillow, goes still.

And Ragnhild is asleep.

The whole house is sleeping now. Nine people, a dog, and twenty cows. On the hearth, under its blanket of turf and ash, the long fire sleeps too, glowing faintly, keeping the warmth of the evening folded up small and safe at its heart, waiting for the morning, the way it has done in this place for three hundred years.

Outside, the night goes on without them, in its own slow, unhurried way. The frost is still growing. On the turf of the roof, every dead stem of last summer's grass is furring over with tiny white feathers of ice, so that by morning the whole long back of the house will look as if it's been dusted with salt. Down on the shore, the boats lie on the stones, rimed with white. The sea breathes against the rocks, slow and patient, as it has all night. Out in the fjord, where the water stays open, a seal lifts its head for a moment, round and dark and glistening, and looks at the sleeping farm on the ridge, and slips back under without a ripple.

Up in the birch woods on the slope, a fox is curled in the lee of a boulder with its tail over its nose. An owl drifts low over the white fields, silent, its wings spread wide, and settles on a fence post by the hay barn, and folds itself up, and waits. The cold settles deeper into the hollows. And the faint green glow over the northern mountains comes and goes, comes and goes, softer now, like the breathing of someone asleep.

If you could rise up above the roof of the house now, and look down on the whole of the island from the height of the mountains, you would see almost nothing. No lights. No movement. Just the pale shapes of the snowfields, and the dark water of the fjords, and here and there, along the shore, the low white humps of other farms, each with its own banked fire, its own sleeping household, its own animals breathing in the dark. You'd hardly know anyone lived there at all.

And outside, very slowly, the stars turn around the Pole.

## Part Twelve — Around Half Past Nine the Next Morning: The Long Fire Wakes

The last small question of our night is the simplest of all. On a morning when the sun will not rise, how does anyone in this house know that it's morning?

Let's wait, and see.

The long night is ending, though you'd hardly know it from the dark. It's around half past nine in the morning. Outside, the stars are still out, but in the south, low along the line of the mountains, something has changed. The blackness there has thinned into a deep, dark, inky blue. It's the very first of the noon twilight, the only day there will be today, and it's creeping up from behind the peaks, slowly, the way the colour comes back into a sleeper's face.

Inside the house, it's still dark. A few people have stirred in the night, as people do, turned over, murmured, got up to see to something, and lain down again. But no one is properly awake.

The animals know first. At the far end of the house, beyond the walls, the cows have begun to shift and low, softly, one after another, and the sheep have started to call, and the horses are blowing through their noses and stamping in their stalls. They don't need the sun. They have their own clocks, in their bellies. It's time to be fed, and they want everyone to know it.

That's the answer to our last small question. In the polar night, the first clock of the morning is the byre. The animals wake the house. And then, a little after them, the faint blue in the southern sky confirms it.

Oddný is up first. She always is. She pulls on her dress and her apron and pins her brooches by feel in the dark, and wraps a shawl around her shoulders, and slips out to the byre, and in a moment you hear the hiss and hiss of milking begin again, and her voice, low, talking to the red cow with the white face.

And Ragnhild opens her eyes.

She lies still for a moment, listening, as she did last night. Everyone's breathing. Everyone's here. Then she pushes back the down coverlet, and the cold of the room touches her face, and she gets up, and pins on her keys, and goes, quick and quiet, across the cold floor to the hearth.

Imagine you are kneeling beside her now, by the long fire, in the dark. The stones of the hearth are still faintly warm under your knees, after all these hours. In front of you is the long, low mound of ash and turf, exactly as Thórir left it last night. Ragnhild takes a small iron rake, and very gently, the way you might brush snow from something precious, she draws back the turf and the ash from the middle of the mound.

And there they are.

At the heart of the hearth, still glowing, are the embers. A small heap of them, not much bigger than two cupped hands. Dull red at the edges, and in the centre, where the air reaches them for the first time in hours, slowly brightening, orange, then gold. They've been alive all night. Through the counting of the nights and the going to bed, through the deep dark of the small hours, while the stars turned around the Pole and the house slept, they've been here, breathing very slowly under their blanket of turf and ash, waiting for this.

Ragnhild takes a handful of dry grass and birch bark from a basket, and a few thin twigs of juniper. She lays the bark gently on the embers. Then she leans down close, so close that you can feel the heat on her face, and on yours, and she blows. A long, slow, steady breath.

The embers brighten. The bark curls, and smokes, a thin white thread of it. She blows again. The smoke thickens. She blows a third time. And with a soft sound, almost like a sigh, the birch bark bursts into flame. Birch bark is full of oils, and it burns like a candle, bright and quick and crackling.

She feeds it the juniper twigs, one by one, and they catch, and spit, and fill the room with a sharp, sweet, resinous smell. Then a few sticks of driftwood. Then the half-burned ends of last night's birch logs. And within a few minutes, the long fire is burning again, all along its bed of stones, just as it did at suppertime, low and orange and steady, and the light of it is moving on the walls, and on the smoke-dark posts, and on the faces of the sleepers on the platforms.

The fire is awake. And one by one, so is the house.

Álfhild first. She opens her eyes and sees the flames and smiles, the slow, creased smile of an old woman who has seen this fire wake more mornings than she can count, and has never once stopped being glad of it. Then Ketil, who sits bolt upright, and asks if it's morning, and whether the sun has come back yet. Then Sigrid, who tells him not yet, of course not, and to give her back her side of the coverlet. Then Bera, who wakes all at once, the way small children do, and climbs over everyone to get to her mother, still clutching the wooden horse. Then Eyvind, groaning. Then Grím, who stretches, and tests his knees, and finds them perfectly well. And then Thórir, who sits up, and rubs his face, and looks at the fire, and looks at Ragnhild, and nods, as if to say, there, we've done it again. The longest night. And we're all here.

Let's set this beside your own morning, one last time. Perhaps your heating comes on by itself before you wake. Perhaps your coffee machine starts brewing on a timer, and your phone wakes you with a gentle chime and the weather forecast, and the lights come on at the touch of a switch. Ragnhild's morning has none of that. But she has something we've mostly lost. She has the knowledge, in her hands, of how to keep a fire alive through the longest night of the year, and wake it again with her own breath.

The cauldron goes back on its chain over the fire, with water, and barley meal, and a little of last night's milk. There's stockfish, beaten soft, and butter, and a bowl of whey. This is the day-meal, the *dagverðr*, and it will be eaten when the first work is done, when the animals are fed and milked and watered, and the hay forked down, and the ice on the trough broken. Grím goes out to help Oddný. Eyvind goes out for wood. Ketil goes to see the calf.

The house fills up with the ordinary sounds of a winter morning. The clank of the cauldron on its chain. The hiss of milk into the pail, faint, from the far end of the house. Snati's claws on the floor as he goes to the door and waits, hopefully, to be let out. Sigrid folding the coverlets and laying them over the platform to air. Álfhild's comb, of carved antler, drawing slowly through her long grey hair by the fire before she pins her headcloth over it for the day. Bera, on her mother's hip, reaching for everything. Ketil, at the door of the byre, telling the calf, very seriously, that the sun is coming back soon, and it mustn't worry.

And the house itself is changing, as the fire grows. The smoke gathers again under the roof, in its soft blue-grey layer, finding its slow way out through the opening at the top. The warmth spreads out from the hearth, little by little, pushing the cold back into the corners. The great posts gleam faintly in the new light. Somewhere under two of them, pressed into the earth, the little gold foils lie where someone placed them long ago, holding the luck of the house, as they will go on holding it, quietly, in the dark, for more than a thousand years, until a farmer's plough turns up the darker soil above them, and someone comes to look.

Thórir goes to the door and stands there for a moment with his cloak around him, looking out over the bay. Across the water, on the other farms along the shore, thin threads of smoke are rising straight up into the still air, one after another, as other households wake their own fires. He counts them, the way a chieftain does. All there. Everyone has come through the night.

It's the view that Ohthere described to King Alfred, all those years ago, or near enough. I'll paraphrase him one last time. He told the king that the land of the Northmen was very long and very narrow, and that all of it that could be grazed or ploughed lay along the sea, and was rocky in places, and that to the east, beyond it, lay wild mountains. A thin green thread of life between the mountains and the sea. That's what Thórir is looking at now, in the blue of the morning. And every farm along that thread has kept its fire alive through the longest night.

And through the open door, you can see that the sky to the south has changed again. The deep blue has paled. Along the jagged line of the mountains there's a band of soft, clear, luminous colour, turquoise at the top, and then pale gold, and then, right along the peaks, a thin line of rose. The snow on the slopes has turned faintly pink. The sea in the bay below, which was black all night, is grey now, and still, like a sheet of pewter, with the boats drawn up on the stones beside it, white with frost. It's not sunlight. The sun won't rise today. But it's light. A long, slow, glowing dawn that will never quite become a day, and will fade again in a few hours into dusk. And today, by a few minutes, it will last a little longer than yesterday's. The longest night is over. From now on, every day, the light will grow.

Ketil stands in the doorway, looking at the band of rose along the mountains, at the notch between the two peaks his grandmother showed him, and says, out loud, to no one in particular, not long now.

Here's one last small and gentle disagreement, to leave you with, and it's about this very morning. Those of us who live in the south tend to imagine the polar night as total darkness, day after day of unbroken black. And at the very far north, closer to the Pole, that's nearer the truth. But here at Borg, sixty-eight degrees north, the polar night is not black at all in the middle of the day. It's blue. Some people who live in the north today say it's their favourite light of the whole year, and some say they can't wait for it to end. The people who live inside the Arctic Circle have always disagreed about it, gently, every winter. I suspect Thórir's household did too.

Behind Ketil, inside the house, the long fire burns.

It burns in the middle of the floor, on its long bed of stones, just as it did at dusk yesterday afternoon, and just as it will tonight, and tomorrow night, and through all the nights of this long winter, until the sun comes back over the notch in the mountains, and the skrei come into the fjord, and the grass comes up green on the slopes, and the cows are let out at last into the light. The fire that was banked and kept, and that waited through the dark. Álfhild has pulled her place on the platform up close to it, and her spindle is already turning. Sigrid is sitting at her feet, holding out her hands to the warmth. Bera is on her mother's knee. And Ragnhild is stirring the pot, with her keys clinking at her belt, while the smoke rises slowly into the roof and finds its way out through the wind-eye, into the cold blue morning, and the light of the long fire moves softly on every face in the room.

They have survived the longest night. They knew how. And now, so do you.

Thank you for spending this long Arctic night with me, and with Thórir and Ragnhild, and Álfhild, and Eyvind and Sigrid and Ketil and little Bera, and Grím and Oddný, and Snati the dog, in their long, warm, smoky house on the ridge at Borg. If this story helped you rest, or helped you drift off somewhere along the way, I'm truly glad. And if you'd like to come back for more nights like this one, you might consider liking this video and subscribing to the channel, gently, whenever you're ready. There's no hurry at all.

Next time, we'll sail west, across the sea, about four hundred years later, to the very edge of the Norse world. We'll spend a midwinter night on a small turf farm in the Western Settlement of Greenland, in the last years before it fell quiet, with a family who kept cattle and goats under the ice-bright hills, hunted seals in the fjords, and kept their own long fire burning at the end of the earth. I hope you'll join me there.

Until then, sleep well. Keep warm. And may your own fire, whatever it is, still be glowing in the morning.

## How the sources were checked

- **The household is fictional.** Thórir, Ragnhild, Álfhild, Eyvind, Sigrid, Ketil, Bera, Grím and Oddný, and the dog Snati, are invented, and the script says so in Part One. No invented person is presented as appearing in any record. The script also says that a real chieftain's house would have held more people, including enslaved people.
- **The setting is real.** The chieftain's longhouse at Borg, on Vestvågøy, in the Lofoten Islands, northern Norway (about 68°15′ N), now the site of the Lofotr Viking Museum. The night is the winter solstice of AD 890, 17–18 December by the Julian calendar. The year was chosen because Ohthere's visit to Alfred is usually placed around then; scholars allow anything from the 870s to the late 890s.
- **How quotes were checked.** In this session, page fetches were blocked by the network policy, so each item was checked through web-search result summaries that quote or summarise the pages listed below. Every verbatim quotation in the script matched at least one edition or translation in those results.
- **Search budget.** The shared web-search limit for the session (200 searches per turn, shared by all agents) ran out after about 20 searches for this script. The items in the "Background knowledge" rows below were **not** re-checked in this session. They are well-established facts or standard scholarship, but they are all given as paraphrase, and none of them is quoted. **Check these before publishing.**
- **What was cut or softened for lack of verification.** These were left out or kept in paraphrase only: Ohthere's whale and walrus figures (no numbers given); the King's Mirror's description of Greenland farming; Ibn Fadlan's exact wording on the cold, and on Rus women's dress; a "Valkyrie armour" explanation of the aurora (left out entirely, with no source found); the Landnámabók entry on a Lofoten emigrant, Óleifr *tvennumbrúni* (left out because his origin could not be confirmed); and the exact wording of King Hákon's Yule-ale law (paraphrased, and the narration says so).
- **Computed, not quoted.** These values for Borg (68.245° N, 13.79° E) in December 890 (Julian) were computed with PyEphem. Times are local solar time, about UTC + 55 minutes. The sun is about 1.8° below the horizon at noon on the solstice. Civil twilight runs about 09:28–14:34, nautical twilight about 07:56–16:06, and the solstice falls on 17 December. New moon was 15 December, and the moon stayed below the horizon through the night. On a flat sea horizon (with standard refraction), the sun's upper limb would be hidden from about 10 to 23 December, so the polar night lasts about two weeks around the solstice, and the narration says the mountains lengthen it. The midnight sun at this latitude runs roughly late May to mid-July. The narration says the moon was worked out with a modern model.
