# What Rich Victorian Women Really Did All Day | History for Sleep

## Part One — Around Half Past Seven in the Morning: Hot Water and Drawn Curtains

The sun has been up for more than three hours, but in this room it is still almost dark.

Heavy curtains hang across the tall window, lined and interlined, the colour of old claret, and only a thin blade of gold has found its way between them to lie across the carpet. The carpet is thick, patterned with roses and scrolling leaves. There is a marble washstand against the wall, a mahogany wardrobe as tall as a small house, a dressing table with a looking-glass on a swinging frame, and a little writing table under the window, with an inkstand and a blotter on it, neatly squared. In the grate, a small coal fire has been laid but not yet lit. It's May, and the mornings are mild now.

From the street below, through the glass, come the sounds of a city waking. The clop of a single horse. The rumble of iron-rimmed wheels on stone. A man calling something, long and musical, that might be milk or might be watercress. A door, somewhere along the terrace, closing.

In the wide bed, under a white counterpane, a woman is lying with her eyes open, listening.

Her name is Caroline Ashworth. She is forty years old. And this is her house: a tall, cream-painted, stucco-fronted house in Eaton Place, in Belgravia, in the West End of London. The day is Tuesday, the sixteenth of May, in the year 1865. The London Season is in full flood.

When most of us picture a woman like Caroline, we picture her through the soft focus of a costume drama. We see her drifting from sofa to sofa in a silk gown, sipping tea, embroidering a cushion, waiting for a gentleman to call, while an army of servants does everything that matters. We picture leisure. We picture a beautiful, idle life.

Not the idle lady of the costume dramas. That is not who we are going to spend the day with. The real woman lying in this bed is the manager of a household of seventeen people, the keeper of its money, its keys and its reputation, the author of a stack of letters every single morning, the planner of a dinner for twelve tonight, a mother, a mistress, a hostess and a diplomat, all at once. And she will do all of it inside a set of rules so detailed and so unforgiving that a single misstep, a card left wrongly, a letter worded carelessly, a guest seated in the wrong chair, could be talked about in a dozen drawing rooms by Thursday.

I want to be honest with you before we begin. If you were set down in this house this morning, in Caroline's place, with all your modern good sense and good manners, you would not last until dinner. Not because you're unkind or careless, but because you wouldn't know the rules. You'd say "I" in a note where you should have said "Mrs. Ashworth." You'd walk into the wrong room first. You'd stay twenty minutes on a call that should have lasted fifteen. You'd break a dozen rules before luncheon without ever knowing you'd broken them. And by evening, without meaning to, you might have quietly damaged your daughter's chances of a good marriage.

So today, we're going to learn those rules. Gently. One hour at a time.

And while we do, I'd like you to carry three small mysteries with you through the day, the way you might carry three folded notes in a pocket.

The first is this. Why would a lady sit down at her desk to write to a woman she has known for twenty years, and write to her as if she were a stranger, without using the word "I" even once?

The second. Why would a lady order her carriage, drive across London to a friend's front door, hand a small white card to a servant, and then drive away again without ever going inside, and count it as an afternoon well spent?

And the third. Why, at the very height of her own dinner party, with the candles lit and the silver shining and the conversation flowing, would the hostess suddenly rise from her chair, catch the eye of every woman at the table, and lead them all out of the room?

I'll answer each of them in its own time. The first before the morning is out. The second in the middle of the afternoon. And the third as the evening reaches its height. There's no hurry. We have the whole day.

Now, before we go any further, I must be clear about something, because it matters on this channel. The Ashworth family is invented. Caroline, her husband, her children, her servants, their names, their faces, their little habits, I have made them up. You won't find them in any record. But nearly everything around them is real. The street is real. The kind of house is real. And the books Caroline reads, the laws she lives under, the letters and diaries and household manuals and census tables that tell us how women like her spent their days, are real, and I'll give you the names and dates as we go, so that you can look them up yourself one day, if you like.

So. Let's meet her.

There's a soft knock at the door. Not a loud knock. A single, gentle tap with one knuckle, the kind of knock that's meant to be heard by someone awake and missed by someone asleep. The door opens, and a young woman comes in carrying a large copper can, with a cloth wrapped around its handle against the heat.

This is Martin, Caroline's lady's maid. She's twenty-six. Her Christian name is Louisa, but nobody in this house calls her that. A lady's maid was very often called by her surname alone, as a mark of her standing above the housemaids, who were called by their first names. Martin sets the can of hot water down on the marble washstand, beside the china basin, and covers it with a folded towel to keep the heat in. Then she crosses to the window and draws the curtains back.

Light floods the room. Caroline closes her eyes against it for a moment, and opens them again.

Here's a small question to begin with, one that we'll answer before this part of the day is done. Who, in this house, has already been awake for two hours, while Caroline slept?

Hold on to that.

Imagine, now, that you are standing beside the window where Martin has drawn back the curtains. Look down. Below you is a long, straight street of tall houses, all alike, all painted the same pale cream, with columned porches and black iron railings and steps going down to the basements below the pavement. The houses are four or five storeys high. Every one of them has the same rows of windows, the same balconies on the first floor, the same chimney stacks along the roofline, each with its little cluster of clay pots. It's a street designed to look like a palace, a single, grand, unbroken front.

And it was designed to look that way, quite recently. Belgravia was built over a few decades, starting in the 1820s, on land belonging to the Grosvenor family, and much of it was laid out and built by a remarkable builder named Thomas Cubitt. Before that, this was marshy ground and market gardens. Now it's one of the most fashionable addresses in the world. The houses are newer than many of the people living in them.

Down on the pavement, you can see a boy in an apron sweeping a doorstep. A milkman with two cans swinging from a wooden yoke across his shoulders. A housemaid, on her knees, whitening the front steps of the house opposite with a block of stone. And down below the railings, in the area, the sunken yard in front of each basement, you can see doors opening and closing, and a coal cellar under the pavement, and the coming and going of people you would hardly see if you didn't look for them.

That's where the answer to our small question lives.

Because downstairs, while Caroline slept, the house has been waking up for hours. Sarah, the head housemaid, was up before six, opening the shutters on the ground floor, raking out yesterday's ashes, laying and lighting the fires in the rooms that would be used first. Ellen, the under-housemaid, has swept the front hall and the stairs. Annie, the kitchen maid, who is only fifteen, was up even before Sarah, to light the great iron kitchen range, because nothing else in the house can happen without it. Hot water, breakfast, tea. James and Thomas, the two footmen, have cleaned the boots and brushed the coats and trimmed the lamps and polished the silver. Mrs. Bell, the cook, has been at the range since half past six. And Mr. Pryor, the butler, before he did anything else this morning, went into the front hall with a soft leather and polished one particular object, as he does every day, until it shone.

It sits on a marble-topped table just inside the front door. It's a silver salver, round, about the size of a dinner plate, with a raised and fluted rim and a little pattern of engraved leaves around its edge. It's the card tray. Before this day is over, dozens of small white cards will be laid on it, and letters, and notes, and a great deal of the business of this house will pass across its polished surface. Keep it in mind. We'll come back to it again and again, all day long, and it will be the last thing we see tonight.

Now Mr. Pryor is laying the breakfast table in the dining room at this very moment, setting out the silver toast rack and the cut-glass marmalade pot and the silver urn.

That's our answer. Almost everyone in this house has been awake since about six, some since earlier. Seventeen people sleep under this roof. Caroline and her husband, their two daughters and their young son, eleven servants who live in, and a governess. The coachman, Dobson, sleeps over the stables in the mews behind the house, with the horses. And the comfort of the five people upstairs depends, every single morning, on the early rising of the people downstairs.

But here's the thing I want you to notice. Caroline isn't lying here because she's lazy. She's lying here because, for about ten more minutes, she is planning. Tonight she's giving a dinner for twelve. The menu, the seating, the flowers, the wines, the guest who has not yet replied, the cook's temper, the price of asparagus, the daughter who must look her best, all of this is turning over in her mind, in order, like the pages of a ledger.

And there's a book on the table beside her bed that would approve.

It was published only four years ago, in October 1861, having first appeared in twenty-four monthly instalments. Its full title is enormous, but most people simply call it Mrs. Beeton's Book of Household Management. It runs to more than a thousand pages. Most of it is recipes, but it begins with a chapter called "The Mistress," and the very first sentence of that chapter is one that Caroline, like a great many women of her class, could probably recite. Here it is, as it was printed in 1861:

"As with the Commander of an Army, or the leader of any enterprise, so is it with the mistress of a house."

The commander of an army. That's how Isabella Beeton chose to describe a woman like Caroline. And she went on to say that the mistress's spirit would be seen throughout the whole establishment, and that her servants would follow her example. A few lines later, she added another sentence that Caroline has very much taken to heart:

"Early rising is one of the most essential qualities which enter into good Household Management."

And Mrs. Beeton gave her reason. When a mistress is an early riser, she wrote, it is almost certain that her house will be orderly and well-managed. Caroline isn't up at six with the housemaids. No lady of her station would be. But she's awake at half past seven, and she'll be dressed and downstairs before nine, which is earlier than some of her neighbours.

And here is the first place where historians disagree, and it's about the very picture we started with.

For much of the twentieth century, the idea of the idle Victorian lady was taken almost for granted. Part of it goes back to the American economist Thorstein Veblen, who in 1899 published a book called The Theory of the Leisure Class. Veblen argued that the wives of wealthy men performed what he called "conspicuous leisure." Their very idleness, their delicate hands and impractical clothes and their uselessness for any real work, was a way of displaying their husbands' wealth to the world. The lady, in this view, was something like an ornament.

But in 1989, the historian M. Jeanne Peterson published a book called Family, Love, and Work in the Lives of Victorian Gentlewomen, which took a close look at a network of real families of clergymen, doctors, lawyers and dons over three generations, and at what their wives and daughters actually said and did. And she rejected the old picture almost entirely. Far from idle, she argued, these women were productive members of society, who worked hard in their homes, their families and their communities, and made real contributions both in private and in public. Other historians have built on her work since, though some have noted that the women she studied were well-educated professional families, and not every household was like theirs. But the old picture of the ornamental lady has never quite recovered.

I'll lean towards Peterson today. And you can judge for yourself, by bedtime, whether Caroline was idle.

Let's think about your own morning for a moment. Perhaps an alarm on your phone woke you. Perhaps you had a hot shower, the water coming at the turn of a tap, already heated by a boiler you never see. Perhaps you made your own coffee, and checked your own messages, and chose your own clothes. In Caroline's house, every one of those things is done by a person. The hot water was carried up four flights of stairs by hand, in that copper can, from a range lit by a fifteen-year-old girl in the dark. Caroline has no tap in her bedroom. Her "shower" is a basin, a sponge and a jug.

She sits up. Martin brings her a cup of tea on a small tray. Caroline drinks it slowly, looking at the light on the carpet, and at her writing table under the window, with its squared blotter and its inkstand, waiting for her.

Then she puts the cup down, and pushes back the counterpane, and the day begins.

## Part Two — Around a Quarter to Nine: Prayers, Kidneys and the Morning Post

Here is the small question for this part of the morning. Before anyone in this house is allowed to touch a single slice of toast, everyone, from the master to the fifteen-year-old kitchen maid, will gather in one room and do one thing together. What is it?

We'll come to it in a moment. First, Caroline must be dressed, and that takes a while.

Imagine that you're sitting quietly in the corner of the bedroom, on a little upholstered chair, watching. Caroline has washed at the basin, with the hot water from the copper can and a cake of scented soap, and dried herself with a soft linen towel. Now Martin helps her into her clothes, layer by layer, in an order that never changes.

First, a fine linen chemise, loose and plain, next to the skin. Then drawers, long and white, trimmed at the knee with a little lace. Then the corset. Martin stands behind her and draws the laces in, steadily, not cruelly, the way you might tighten the strings of an instrument, until Caroline says, quietly, "That will do." Over the corset goes a camisole, and then, because this is 1865, a cage crinoline: a light frame of thin, flexible steel hoops, joined by tapes, that hangs from her waist and spreads out into a wide, graceful bell. It's lighter than you'd expect. Before the cage crinoline came in, about ten years ago, a woman had to wear layer upon layer of heavy petticoats to hold her skirt out. The cage did the work of all of them at once. Over it, a petticoat or two, and then the morning dress itself: a dark blue silk, plain and well cut, high at the neck, with long sleeves and a row of small covered buttons down the front.

Martin brushes out Caroline's hair, a hundred strokes, and twists it up into a smooth knot at the back of her head, and pins it, and sets a small lace cap over it, the kind that married women of her age often wore in the morning. A gold brooch at her throat. A ring. And at her waist, on a short chain, a little cluster of keys.

We'll come back to those keys in the next part of the morning. They matter more than almost anything else she's wearing.

It's about a quarter to nine. Somewhere below, a bell rings. Not a servant's bell, but a hand bell, rung in the hall. And Caroline, with a last glance at the glass, goes down.

The dining room is on the ground floor, at the front of the house, and it's a solemn room, all dark red walls and heavy mahogany. The long table has been covered with a white cloth and laid for breakfast. On the sideboard, along one wall, a row of silver dishes stands on little spirit lamps to keep warm, their covers gleaming. The silver urn is steaming. The light comes in through the tall front windows, softened by lace curtains, and the smell is wonderful: coffee, toast, bacon, something rich and peppery.

And standing in a line along the wall, by the door, are the servants.

Mr. Pryor, the butler, at the head of the line, grey-haired and very upright in his black coat. Mrs. Hodges, the housekeeper, in black silk, with her own bunch of keys. Mrs. Bell, the cook, red-faced from the range, who has wiped her hands and taken off her big apron for the purpose. James and Thomas, the footmen, tall young men in their morning livery. Sarah and Ellen, the housemaids, in clean print dresses and white aprons and caps. Annie, the little kitchen maid, at the very end. Nurse Webb has stayed upstairs in the nursery with Charlie, the youngest of the family, who is only six, and with the nursemaid, Polly. But Miss Lacy, the governess, has come down with Florence, who is fourteen.

At the table stand the family. Edward Ashworth is forty-eight, a barrister, and a Queen's Counsel, with chambers in Lincoln's Inn, a heavy dark beard going grey at the chin, and a deep, slow, courtroom voice. Beside him is their elder daughter, Emily, who is eighteen. This is her first Season. She was presented at Court this spring, in a white gown with a long train and three white feathers in her hair, and since then she's been out, which means that she is officially in Society, and may be invited to dances, and dinners, and may be courted. And Florence, fourteen, who is not out, and will not be for several years, stands next to her governess with her hands folded, looking at the silver dishes on the sideboard with great interest.

Caroline takes her place. Everyone is quiet.

And Edward opens a large book bound in black leather, and begins to read aloud.

That's the answer to our small question. Before breakfast, the whole household gathers for family prayers. A short reading from the Bible. A prayer, read from a printed book of family devotions, of which dozens were published in these years. Then everyone kneels, the family turning to kneel at their chairs, the servants kneeling on the carpet by the wall, and says the Lord's Prayer together. Then they rise. The servants file out, in order, back down to the basement. And the family sits down to breakfast. In many households of this kind, morning prayers were simply part of the day, as fixed as the rising bell. It was a way of saying, every morning, that this house was a single Christian household, master and servant together under God, even if, the moment prayers were over, they went back to their separate floors.

Imagine that you're kneeling there, at the end of the servants' line, beside Annie. The carpet is thick under your knees. You can smell the bacon from the sideboard and the coal smoke clinging to Annie's dress. You can hear the master's voice, slow and even, reading the words of the prayer. And if you glance sideways at Annie, you can see that her eyes are closed, and her hands are clasped tight, and that her knuckles are red and raw from the scrubbing brush and the cold water and the black-leading of the kitchen range that she did this morning before any of the family were awake.

She is fifteen. She came up from a village in Hertfordshire last year. She earns, I'd estimate, something in the region of ten pounds a year, perhaps a little more, with her board and lodging and her tea and sugar. Wages varied a great deal from house to house, so treat that as a rough figure. And she's one of an enormous army.

In the census of 1861, taken just four years before our day, the clerks counted the people of England and Wales by occupation. And among women aged twenty and over, the published tables list more than three hundred and forty-five thousand general domestic servants alone. On top of those were more than seventy-three thousand cooks, more than sixty-six thousand housemaids, and nearly sixty-four thousand charwomen, and all the other kinds of servant besides. The whole category the census called "attendance," domestic service and its relations, came to more than six hundred and eighty-eight thousand women over the age of twenty. And that doesn't count the girls, like Annie, under twenty, who were a very large part of the whole. Domestic service was by far the largest paid occupation for women in the country.

And here is a point where historians disagree, about those very numbers.

For a long time, historians took the census figures more or less at face value. But the census enumerators' books, the original handwritten sheets, are full of tricky cases. A daughter at home who helps with the housework, is she a servant? A niece living with her aunt and doing the cooking? The census sometimes called such women servants, and sometimes didn't. In 2023, the historian Xuesheng You published a study in the Economic History Review using the enumerators' books of 1851, and argued that many female relatives who did domestic work were not recorded as servants at all, so that the true number of women working in domestic service was probably even larger than the official tables suggest. Her revision suggested that domestic service probably employed more women than all the different kinds of manufacturing put together. Other historians, working on other samples, have argued about how best to count these women, and the debate is still going. But whichever way you count, the women who kept houses like this one running were very, very many.

We know a great deal about what their mornings were like from one remarkable source. Her name was Hannah Cullwick. She was a real woman, a servant, born in Shropshire in 1833, and from her mid-twenties to her mid-sixties, from the 1850s to the 1890s, she kept a diary. She kept it, in part, because a gentleman named Arthur Munby, who was fascinated by working women, and whom she would one day secretly marry, asked her to write down her work. And so she did, day after day, in long, plain lists. In one well-known entry, written while she was working as a maid-of-all-work in a household in Kilburn, she sets out her morning: opening the shutters, lighting the kitchen fire, shaking out her sooty things in the dusthole and emptying the soot there, sweeping and dusting the rooms and the hall, and on and on through the day. I'm paraphrasing those lines, because the published transcriptions differ a little in their wording, and I'd rather not put a word in her mouth that she didn't write. The standard edition of her diaries was edited by Liz Stanley and published in 1984. Scholars still argue about how far the diaries show Hannah's own voice, and how far they were shaped by what Munby wanted to read. But they are among the very few places where a Victorian servant speaks to us about her work in her own hand.

Annie, rising from her knees, goes back downstairs to the scullery, and the family sit down to breakfast.

It's a substantial meal. Breakfast in a house like this is served partly by the servants and partly by the family themselves, from the sideboard. On the silver dishes, under their covers, there are devilled kidneys, rich and peppery. There's bacon, and eggs, both boiled and poached, and a dish of broiled fish, and cold ham on the side. There's toast in the silver rack, and hot rolls wrapped in a napkin, and butter in a cool dish, and marmalade, and honey. Edward drinks coffee. Caroline drinks tea, from the urn, poured by James. Emily eats very little, because she was at a dance until half past one this morning and is pale and a little cross. Florence eats a great deal, and is told by Miss Lacy, quietly, not to.

Let's set this beside your own breakfast table. Perhaps, for you, breakfast is a bowl of cereal, a cup of coffee, a slice of toast, eaten standing up, or in the car, or at a desk. Perhaps you check your phone as you eat, reading the news of the world as it happens. Edward Ashworth has something like that too. Beside his plate, folded and ironed, so that the ink won't come off on his fingers, is today's copy of The Times.

Yes, ironed. In some grand households, so the old stories go, a footman pressed the morning newspaper with a warm iron, to dry the ink and flatten the creases. I can't point you to a document that proves it was done in a house like this one, so take it as a household legend, but it's a lovely one, and the Ashworths' footman does it.

And this spring, The Times is full of things worth reading. A little more than three weeks ago, at the end of April, the news crossed the Atlantic by steamer that President Abraham Lincoln had been shot in a theatre in Washington, in the middle of April, and had died the next morning. London has been talking of nothing else for days. Edward reads out a piece of it, and Caroline listens and says nothing, and Emily, who danced last night with a young man from the American legation, looks down at her plate.

And there's another story in the papers this spring that Caroline follows closely, though she doesn't say much about it at the table. A committee of electors in Westminster, just a mile or two from this house, has asked the philosopher John Stuart Mill to stand for Parliament at the coming election. Mill was reluctant, and when he agreed, he set out conditions that astonished people. He would not spend money on the campaign. He would not go round canvassing for votes. And he made no secret of the fact that he believed women should have the vote, too. In July of this year, he'll be elected. A year later, he'll present a petition to Parliament asking for votes for women. Edward thinks the whole thing very eccentric. Caroline, privately, isn't so sure.

But the story isn't what Caroline is waiting for. She's waiting for the post.

And here it comes. There's a step in the hall, and James enters, carrying the silver card tray from the hall table, the same round salver Mr. Pryor polished this morning. On it, today, there are no cards yet. Instead, there are letters. Perhaps a dozen. James offers the tray first to Edward, who takes three, and then to Caroline, who takes all the rest.

In London in these years, the post came not once a day but several times. The Post Office's own regulations from around this time speak of several collections and deliveries of letters every day in town, and by later in the century, in the very centre of London, it could be as many as a dozen. A note posted in the morning could be answered by the afternoon. For a woman like Caroline, the post was what the telephone, and then email, and then the message on your phone, would be to later generations. It came all day long. And it had to be answered.

Caroline looks through her letters, one by one, without opening any of them yet. A thick cream envelope with a crest, from a duchess's secretary. A thin envelope in her sister's hand, from Hampshire. Three that are clearly invitations. A bill from her dressmaker in Regent Street. A note from the vicar of St Peter's, Eaton Square, about the Ladies' Committee. A letter in an unfamiliar hand. And one small, square, stiff envelope, addressed in a neat, slanting script, that she recognises at once. Lady Halford, she thinks. At last. She's the guest who hasn't yet replied about tonight.

She doesn't open it at the table. A lady doesn't read her letters at breakfast in front of everyone, or not the important ones. She lays them in a neat pile beside her plate, under her hand. She finishes her tea.

Then Edward folds his newspaper, and rises, and kisses his wife on the forehead, and goes out to the hall, where Thomas is waiting with his hat and his gloves and his umbrella, and the brougham is already at the door to carry him to Lincoln's Inn. He'll be gone all day. That's how it is. The men of this house go out into the world. And the women stay, and run it.

Emily asks if she might go back to bed. Caroline says she may lie down until eleven, but not a minute longer, because they have calls to make this afternoon. Florence goes up with Miss Lacy to the schoolroom. And Caroline gathers her letters, and rises, and goes, not up to her room, but down. Down the back stairs. Down to the kitchen.

Because it's time to give the orders for the day.

## Part Three — Around a Quarter to Ten: The Keys, the Cook and the Orders of the Day

The small question for this hour is about that little cluster of keys at Caroline's waist. In a house with a butler, a housekeeper and a cook, all trusted servants of many years, why does the lady of the house still carry keys at all? And what, exactly, do they open?

Let's follow her down and find out.

The back stairs are a different world from the front. The front staircase of this house is wide and shallow and carpeted, with a polished mahogany rail and tall windows on the landings, made for ladies in wide skirts to sweep up and down. The back stairs are narrow, steep and bare, with a plain wooden rail, and they're lit by a single small window on each floor. These are the stairs the servants use, all day long, carrying coal and hot water and trays and slop pails and laundry, up and down, up and down, from basement to attic. A house like this was built so that the servants could do all of that almost without being seen.

At the bottom, the air changes. It's warmer. It smells of coal, and of roasting meat, and of soap, and of something sweet. There's a long stone-flagged passage, with doors opening off it: the servants' hall, where they eat; the butler's pantry, where the silver lives; the housekeeper's room; the still-room; the larder, cool and dim, with slate shelves; the scullery, with its stone sinks; and at the end, the kitchen itself.

Imagine you're standing in the kitchen doorway, behind Caroline's wide blue skirts. It's a big, high room, half below the level of the street, so that the windows high in one wall look out at the feet of people passing on the pavement. The floor is stone. The walls are whitewashed. A great iron range fills one whole wall, black and shining, with an open fire in the middle, ovens on either side, a boiler for hot water, and a row of hooks and hobs. It's already roaring hot. Rows of copper pans hang along the wall, polished until they glow like sunset. There's a long, scrubbed deal table down the middle of the room, white with years of scrubbing, and on it, already, there's a great deal going on. A heap of fresh peas being shelled. A bowl of eggs. A haunch of something under a cloth. And a tray of little moulds for jellies, waiting.

Mrs. Bell, the cook, straightens up from the range and wipes her hands on her apron. Annie, at the table, drops a pea and goes pink.

"Good morning, Mrs. Bell," says Caroline.

"Good morning, ma'am."

And now they begin.

This little ceremony, every morning, was something Mrs. Beeton described with great care. In her chapter on the mistress, she wrote that after breakfast, and I'm quoting her 1861 text here, "it will be well for the mistress to make a round of the kitchen and other offices," to see that everything is in order and that the morning's work has been done. And then, she wrote, "The orders for the day should then be given." Any questions the servants have should be answered, and any special supplies they need should be handed out.

That's what's happening now. Caroline and Mrs. Bell are going through the day, meal by meal. The servants' dinner at one o'clock, which is a plain meal of meat and potatoes and pudding. The nursery and schoolroom luncheon. The family's own luncheon. Tea at five. And then the great matter of the day, tonight's dinner, for twelve, at a quarter to eight.

They've discussed tonight's menu already, twice, last week. But Caroline goes over it again. The soups, a clear one and a thick one. The fish, a salmon, which James has been sent to collect from the fishmonger's this morning, and which had better be fresh. The entrées. The great joint, a saddle of lamb. The asparagus, if it can be got at a sensible price. The ducklings. The sweets, the jellies in their moulds, an ice. The dessert. It's an enormous amount of food, and every dish must arrive at the table at the right moment, at the right heat, on the right plate. And Mrs. Bell, with Annie and a scullery maid who comes in by the day, must do it all.

Here's where the keys come in.

Caroline unhooks the little chain at her waist and chooses one key, and goes with Mrs. Hodges, the housekeeper, along the passage to the store-room. She unlocks it. Inside, on shelves, are the dry goods of the household. Tea, in a big japanned tin. Loaf sugar, in white cones wrapped in blue paper, to be broken up with sugar nippers. Coffee beans. Rice. Candles, in boxes. Soap, in long bars, stacked to dry and harden, because soap that's dried out lasts longer. Spices. Jars of preserves. Bottles of things. Mrs. Hodges takes down what's needed for the day, and Caroline watches, and the amounts are written into a book.

That's the answer to our small question. Caroline carries the keys because in this house, the most valuable consumable goods, the tea, the sugar, the spices, the wine, the candles, are kept locked up, and the mistress is ultimately responsible for every ounce. In many houses, the housekeeper held the everyday keys, and the butler held the keys to the wine cellar and the plate. But the mistress kept her own set, and checked the books. The keys at her waist were not just a practical thing. They were a badge of office. They said, without a word, who was in charge here.

Why lock up the tea? Because tea and sugar were precious, and easily carried away. Servants in many households were given a fixed allowance of tea and sugar as part of their wages, and what went beyond that was watched closely. A careless mistress, the household manuals warned again and again, would find her stores mysteriously dwindling. A careful one kept the keys, and kept the accounts.

And the accounts are the next thing. Back in the housekeeper's room, Mrs. Hodges has laid out the books: the butcher's book, the baker's book, the grocer's, the fishmonger's, the dairy. Tradesmen in these years usually supplied great houses on credit, writing each delivery in a little book that the household kept, and the bills were settled weekly, or monthly, or quarterly. Caroline turns the pages, running her eye down the columns. The butcher's book is a little high this week. She asks about it, quietly. Mrs. Hodges explains. Caroline nods, and initials the page.

She also keeps a book of her own, upstairs at her writing desk, in which she records the household money her husband gives her, and how it's spent, and the servants' wages, which are paid quarterly, on Lady Day, Midsummer, Michaelmas and Christmas. It's a ledger, in her own hand, ruled in red ink. In a house this size, the annual budget for food, servants, light, fuel and all the rest might come to a very large sum, more than many families of the time earned in a decade. And it's Caroline, not her husband, who knows where every shilling of it goes.

Let's think about your own kitchen for a moment. When you run low on something, perhaps you add it to a list on your phone, or tap a button on a screen, and it arrives in a van the next day. Your refrigerator keeps the milk cold for a week. Your freezer keeps meat for months. Your pantry has packets with dates printed on them. In Caroline's house, there's no refrigerator. The larder is kept cool with slate shelves, and a window covered with fine wire mesh to keep out the flies, and in summer, ice delivered in blocks by an ice merchant, wrapped in flannel. The milk comes daily, perhaps twice daily. The meat must be bought for the day it's eaten. Fish must be eaten the day it's caught, or very nearly. Planning a dinner for twelve, in this world, is a feat of timing.

Now, there's something about the author of that famous book that I want to tell you very gently, because it's on Caroline's mind this morning, and because it's true.

Isabella Beeton, who wrote that the mistress of a house was like the commander of an army, was not an elderly matron with fifty years of housekeeping behind her. She was very young. She was about twenty-one when she began to write the book, and it first appeared in instalments in a magazine published by her husband, Samuel Beeton, called The Englishwoman's Domestic Magazine. When the instalments were gathered into one volume in 1861, it sold, by one account, sixty thousand copies in its first year. And then, this very year, just three months before our day, in February 1865, after the birth of her fourth child, Isabella Beeton died. She was twenty-eight years old.

Caroline read of it in the newspapers in February. She didn't know Mrs. Beeton, of course. But she has the book, and she uses it, and she thought about her a good deal.

And here is a point where historians, and food writers, disagree about the book itself.

For a long time, Mrs. Beeton was treated as a kind of national institution, the voice of the perfectly managed Victorian home, a woman who'd tested every recipe in her own kitchen. But in 2005, the historian and biographer Kathryn Hughes published a book called The Short Life and Long Times of Mrs Beeton, which looked very closely at where the book had come from. And she, and others, showed that a great many of the recipes had been taken, sometimes almost word for word, from earlier cookery writers, among them Eliza Acton, whose Modern Cookery for Private Families had come out in 1845. That was not so unusual in those days, when copyright was a looser idea. But it changed how people thought of the book. And it raised another question, too. Was Mrs. Beeton describing how real households actually ran? Or was she describing how her readers wished they ran, an ideal to aim at, written for women a step or two below the grand houses of Belgravia, who hoped to manage their two or three servants the way a duchess managed thirty? Hughes's own answer leaned towards the second. Other writers have defended Beeton as a brilliant compiler and organiser, whose real gift was to put everything a household needed into one clear, well-ordered book. Both views can be true at once.

What isn't in doubt is that Caroline's morning round, with the keys and the books, is exactly what Mrs. Beeton recommended.

There's another woman whose letters show us the other side of all this, the side that didn't go according to the book. Her name was Jane Welsh Carlyle, and she was the wife of the writer Thomas Carlyle. They lived, from 1834 until her death in 1866, in a house in Cheyne Row in Chelsea, not two miles from here. Jane was one of the great letter-writers of the century, witty and sharp and often very funny, and a great many of her letters are about the difficulties of running a house. She did a good deal of the hard work herself: nailing down carpets, cleaning, battling bedbugs. And she went through a remarkable number of servants. In her book The Carlyles at Home, published in 1965, the writer Thea Holme drew up a list of the servants who passed through the house in Cheyne Row over the years, and it came to thirty-four maids. Jane's letters about them are a long, comic, exasperated chronicle of girls who drank, girls who broke things, girls who left without notice, and the occasional treasure who stayed. I won't quote her directly here, because I haven't been able to check the exact wording of her lines against an edition, but if you ever want a few evenings of real Victorian domestic life told with wit and feeling, her collected letters are a wonderful place to start.

Caroline has been luckier than Jane Carlyle. Mrs. Bell has been with her for eleven years. Mr. Pryor for nine. Mrs. Hodges was her own mother's housekeeper once. These are not people she thinks of as friends, exactly, in the way you or I would use the word. The gulf between upstairs and downstairs is too wide for that. But they are people she depends on completely, and she knows it, and they know she knows it.

Before she leaves the kitchen, she does one more small thing. She stops by the table where Annie is shelling peas, and asks her whether her mother's chest is any better. Annie, scarlet, says yes, ma'am, thank you, ma'am, a little better. Caroline nods, and says she's glad, and that if Annie wants to write home, she may have a sheet of paper and a stamp from the housekeeper. A penny stamp, since the Penny Post began in 1840, will carry a letter anywhere in the country.

It's a small thing. But in a house like this, small things are noticed, and remembered.

Then Caroline goes back along the stone passage, and up the narrow back stairs, through the door at the top that swings shut behind her with a soft thud, and suddenly she's back in the front of the house, with its carpets and its gilt mirrors and its silence. She passes through the hall. The silver card tray is on its marble table by the front door, empty again now that the letters have been taken up, polished, shining, waiting for the afternoon.

She goes on up the front stairs, to the first floor, to the small room at the back of the drawing room, which she calls her morning room.

There's a writing desk waiting for her there, and on it, the pile of letters from breakfast. And among them, that one small, square, stiff envelope, in Lady Halford's slanting hand.

## Part Four — Around Half Past Ten: The Writing Desk

Here is this hour's small question. A woman in Caroline's position, in the middle of the London Season, sits down at her desk every morning. How many letters and notes will she write before luncheon? And how many different kinds of letter are there?

The morning room is small and pretty, at the back of the house, looking out over a narrow strip of garden with a plane tree in it and the backs of the mews beyond. It's papered in a pale green trellis pattern. There's a little sofa, two chairs, a work table with an unfinished piece of embroidery on it, a bookcase, and, under the window, where the light is best, the writing desk.

It's a handsome thing, of walnut, with a sloping top covered in green leather, and a row of little pigeonholes and drawers along the back. In the pigeonholes there are papers sorted into bundles and tied with tape: bills, receipts, letters to be answered, letters answered and kept. In the drawers there are sheets of thick, cream notepaper with the address, Eaton Place, stamped at the top in small, raised letters. There are envelopes to match. There's a stick of red sealing wax and a small brass seal. A silver inkstand, with two glass wells, one of black ink and one of red. A pen tray, holding a wooden penholder with a steel nib. A blotter. A little brass box of penny stamps, printed in red, with the Queen's profile on them.

Caroline sits down, arranges her skirts, and opens Lady Halford's envelope first.

It's a single sheet, folded once. And this is what it says, in its neat, slanting hand, more or less: Lady Halford has much pleasure in accepting Mrs. Ashworth's kind invitation to dinner on Tuesday the sixteenth, at a quarter to eight o'clock.

That's all. No "Dear Caroline." No "I." No signature, even, at the bottom. Just a single, smooth sentence, written as though by some invisible secretary, about two ladies who are referred to only by their titles and their surnames.

And yet Lady Halford and Caroline have known each other since they were girls. They were bridesmaids at each other's weddings. They've sat up together, through the night, with each other's sick children.

So here, at last, is the first of the three mysteries I asked you to carry this morning. Why would a lady write to a woman she has known for twenty years as if she were a stranger, without using the word "I" even once?

The answer is that this isn't really a letter at all. It's a note of a particular kind, and it follows a particular form, and that form was, by these years, almost a law of polite society. Notes of invitation, and the replies to them, were written in the third person.

The etiquette manuals of the period are quite clear about it. One of the most widely used, Routledge's Manual of Etiquette, from later in this same era, sets it down plainly: notes of invitation and acceptance are written in the third person and in the simplest style. It even tells its readers that the old-fashioned opening, "Mrs. So-and-so presents her compliments to Mrs. So-and-so," was being dropped by the most elegant letter-writers, in favour of something shorter. Another guide, The Habits of Good Society, published anonymously in London in 1859, belongs to the same busy family of books, all of them explaining to anxious readers exactly how such things were done. I'm paraphrasing the manuals here, rather than quoting them at length, because the wording shifted from edition to edition. But the rule itself is not in doubt.

Why? There are several reasons, and they're all true at once.

The first is that an invitation to dinner isn't a private matter between two friends. It's a matter between two households. It's the Ashworths, as a family, inviting Sir George and Lady Halford, as a couple, to dine. The third person makes that clear. It takes the personal feeling out, and leaves the social fact.

The second is that the form protects everyone. A note in the third person can decline an invitation without wounding anyone. "Lady Halford regrets that a prior engagement prevents her from accepting." No explanation is needed, and none is expected. Nobody has to say, "I don't want to come." The form does the work.

And the third reason is that the form itself is a kind of password. To write a correct third-person note, on the right paper, in the right words, within a day of receiving the invitation, was to show that you knew the rules. To get it wrong, to write "Dear Mrs. Ashworth, I should love to come," was to show, in a single line, that you didn't belong.

So when Lady Halford writes to her old friend as if to a stranger, she isn't being cold. She's being correct. And, if you'll forgive me, Caroline would have been a little worried if she hadn't been. And I should add, so that you don't imagine their friendship as all starch and ceremony, that inside the same envelope, folded small, there's a second note, in the first person, from Lady Halford, which says, "Dearest C., forgive my slowness, George has had the gout, we shall come if I have to carry him. Ever your loving M." That one Caroline reads twice, and smiles, and puts in her pocket.

That's our first mystery solved. The formal note was a form. And the real friendship lived in the second sheet of paper, folded inside it.

Now she begins to write.

Imagine that you're sitting at this desk yourself, in Caroline's place. You take up the wooden penholder. The steel nib is sharp and springy, and you dip it into the inkwell, not too deep, and tap it gently on the rim to shake off the drop. You begin to write, and at once you discover that a steel nib is a temperamental thing. Press too hard, and it splutters and blots. Press too lightly, and the line fades to nothing. Go too fast, and the ink runs dry halfway through a word. You must dip, write a few words, dip again, write a few more. And every so often, you must stop and press the blotter over what you've written, so that your sleeve doesn't smudge it. Caroline does all this without thinking. You, in her chair, would be ink to the wrist within a minute.

And she has a great many letters to write.

First, the replies to the three invitations that came this morning. One to a ball in Grosvenor Square on the twenty-fifth, accepted, in the third person, for herself, her husband, and her daughter. One to a dinner in Lowndes Square, declined, in the third person, with regrets, because they're already engaged that evening. One to an afternoon concert, accepted for herself and Emily.

Then a note to the dressmaker, about the bill, which is high, and a second fitting for Emily's new ball gown, which is urgent.

Then a reply to the vicar of St Peter's, about the Ladies' Committee for the parish visiting, which meets on Thursday, and for which she's agreed to read the accounts.

Then a letter to her sister in Hampshire, which is long, and warm, and in the first person, and full of news about the children and the Season, and which she'll finish later, because it's the one she actually wants to write.

Then a note to the florist in Covent Garden, confirming the flowers for tonight's table.

Then a note to a friend who's been unwell, which must be just the right length: long enough to show real concern, short enough not to tire her.

Then a note of thanks to a hostess from last week, for a dinner at which the soup was cold and the conversation dull. That one takes her longer than all the others.

Then a reply to a lady she hardly knows, who's written to ask whether a certain young woman, who has applied for a position as a lady's maid, was honest and sober in her previous place. This is called a character, and it's one of the most serious letters a mistress writes, because a servant without a good character might never find another place. Caroline writes it carefully, and kindly, and truthfully.

That's ten. By the time she's done, there'll be eleven or twelve. And that's the answer to our small question. In a morning, in the Season, a woman like Caroline might write a dozen notes and letters, of half a dozen different kinds, each with its own rules: third-person notes, first-person letters, letters of business, letters of condolence, letters of thanks, letters of character. Every one of them is a small act of management. Every one of them keeps a thread of the family's life from coming loose.

Let's set this beside your own life for a moment. You probably write more messages in a day than Caroline does. Emails, texts, a note in a group chat, a reply to a comment. You can write them in seconds, with your thumbs, on a train, and send them across the world before you've finished your coffee. But I think you'd recognise the feeling of Caroline's morning. The inbox that's never empty. The message that must be worded just so. The reply you've been putting off. The sense that the whole web of your life is held together by these small, constant acts of writing back.

And you'd recognise something else, too. The interruptions.

Because in the hour Caroline spends at her desk, she's interrupted five times. Mrs. Hodges comes up to ask about the extra leaf for the dining table. Nurse Webb sends Polly down to say that Charlie has a slight cold and shall he go out in the Square garden or not. Emily appears, in her wrapper, an hour before she was told she might, to ask whether she may wear the pink or the white tonight. Mr. Pryor sends up to ask about the claret. And Martin comes to say the dressmaker's girl is downstairs with the trimmings.

There's a passage about exactly this, written by a woman who knew this world from the inside and found it almost unbearable. In the early 1850s, Florence Nightingale, before she was famous, before the Crimea, wrote an essay called Cassandra. It was a cry of frustration about the lives of women of her class. Scholars still argue about exactly when she wrote it, with dates from about 1850 to 1852 suggested, and she later had it printed privately, around 1860. In one of its most quoted lines, she complains that women never have half an hour in all their lives, except before or after anybody is up in the house, that they can call their own, without fear of offending or hurting someone. And she went on to say, and I'm paraphrasing here, that a woman was never supposed to have any occupation so important that it couldn't be interrupted.

Never half an hour. Except before or after anybody is up in the house. Remember that phrase. We'll come back to it at the very end of the day.

And here is where historians disagree, sharply, about what the life at this desk meant.

In 1987, the historians Leonore Davidoff and Catherine Hall published a great book called Family Fortunes, a study of middle-class families in Birmingham and in Essex and Suffolk between about 1780 and 1850. They argued that in this period, the middle classes came to believe in what historians have called "separate spheres": the idea that men belonged to the public world of work, money and politics, and women to the private world of the home, the family and the moral life. Men went out. Women stayed in. And that division, they argued, was central to how the English middle class came to see itself.

But in 1993, the historian Amanda Vickery published an influential article in the Historical Journal, called "Golden Age to Separate Spheres?," which questioned the whole framework. She argued, among other things, that the timeline didn't hold, that historians of earlier centuries had found women confined to the home long before industrialisation, and that the language of separate spheres described what moralists said women ought to do, rather than what women actually did. Real women, she suggested, moved between public and private all the time. And to some, her article seemed to discredit the simple model of public men and private women. Davidoff and Hall, in a new edition of their book in 2002, answered their critics and broadened their argument. The debate has gone on ever since.

Look at Caroline's desk and you can see why. Is a letter to the vicar about the parish visiting committee a private thing, or a public one? Is a character reference for a lady's maid, which may decide whether a young woman finds work, domestic business or public business? Is a dinner invitation to a judge's wife, which may help a barrister's career, a matter of the home, or of the world? Caroline would probably say she has never once left her proper sphere. And yet, from this little walnut desk, she's reaching out across half of London.

She signs the last note, blots it, folds it, and slips it into its envelope. She addresses it in her clear, upright hand. She presses a red penny stamp in the corner. She rings the bell.

James comes. She hands him the bundle of letters. He'll carry them down and lay them for a moment on the silver card tray in the hall, sorted, while Mr. Pryor looks them over, and then the ones going by hand will go by hand, delivered by a footman or a boy to houses in the next square, and the rest will go to the pillar box at the corner, or to the receiving office. And if you're picturing that pillar box as bright red, I should tell you that in these years, as far as I can tell, London's pillar boxes were mostly painted a dark green. The famous red came in only in the following decade. Some of them, posted this morning, will be read in other Belgravia houses before luncheon.

It's a quarter to twelve. Caroline sits back in her chair for just a moment, alone. She puts her hand into her pocket and touches the folded, first-person note from Lady Halford. Then she rises, and goes upstairs. It's time to see her son.

## Part Five — Around Noon: The Nursery and the Schoolroom

The small question for this part of the day is a tender one. In a house like this, with a nurse and a nursemaid and a governess, how much of the day does a mother actually spend with her children? And what does she do when she's with them?

To find out, we have to climb.

The nursery is at the top of the house, on the third floor, under the servants' attics. That's where nurseries usually were in a tall London house: high up, away from the noise of the street and the comings and goings of visitors, and, it must be said, away from the drawing room, where a crying child or a clattering toy wouldn't disturb the grown-ups. The stairs grow narrower as you climb. The carpet gets plainer. The ceilings get lower. And by the time Caroline reaches the top landing, a little out of breath in her corset and crinoline, she's in another country altogether, a small, warm, self-contained kingdom with its own rules, its own meals, and its own queen.

The queen is Nurse Webb. She's fifty-two, stout, starched and unshakeable, with a white apron and a white cap and a voice that can quiet a room of children without ever rising. She came to this house when Emily was born, eighteen years ago, and she has brought up all three of the Ashworth children since. She curtseys when Caroline comes in. But it's a small curtsey. In this room, the two women both know, Nurse Webb is very nearly in charge.

Imagine that you're standing in the nursery doorway now. It's a bright room, with two windows, each guarded by iron bars to stop a child from falling. There's a high brass fireguard around the grate, and on its rail, a row of small white garments drying. There's a big scrubbed table with a red cloth on it, and a rocking horse, dappled grey, with a real horsehair mane and tail, worn thin where small hands have gripped it. There's a dolls' house that was Emily's once, and then Florence's, and is now mostly ignored. A shelf of picture books. A box of wooden bricks. A Noah's Ark with its animals lined up in pairs on the windowsill, the elephants at the front. And a regiment of painted tin soldiers, in red coats, drawn up for battle on the hearthrug.

And sitting in the middle of the hearthrug, in a holland smock, commanding the soldiers, is Charlie.

He's six. He has his father's dark eyes and his mother's chin, and a slight cold, which is why his nose is pink and why Nurse Webb has decided, after some thought, that he will not be going out to the garden in the Square this afternoon. When he sees his mother, he jumps up and runs to her, and then remembers himself, and stops, and says, "Good morning, Mamma," and holds up his face to be kissed.

Caroline kneels down, with a great rustle of silk and a creak of steel hoops, onto the nursery hearthrug, which she would never do in her own drawing room. And Charlie shows her the battle.

It's the Battle of Waterloo, of course. The French are losing, as they always do on this hearthrug. Caroline asks which one is the Duke of Wellington, and Charlie shows her, a small tin figure on a horse with a chip of paint missing from its hat. She asks what happens next. He tells her, at length. She listens. Polly, the nursemaid, who is seventeen, is mending a stocking by the window and pretending not to listen too.

This visit will last about twenty minutes. Then Caroline will go down to the schoolroom. And later this afternoon, at about five o'clock, if there's time, Charlie will be washed and brushed and put into a clean velvet suit and brought down by Nurse Webb to the drawing room, to spend half an hour with his mother before his nursery supper and his bed.

That's the answer to our small question, at least for a house like this, in the Season. A mother of Caroline's class might see her younger children at fixed times: a visit to the nursery in the morning, and the children brought down to her in the late afternoon, washed and dressed, for an hour or so. Much of the rest of their day, their meals, their walks, their baths, their bedtimes, their small illnesses and their tears, belonged to the nurse. On a quiet day in the country, it might be very different, with long hours together. But this is London, in May, and Caroline's day is spoken for.

And yet, notice what she does with those twenty minutes. She kneels on the floor. She learns the names of the tin soldiers. She feels Charlie's forehead with the back of her hand, without seeming to. She asks Nurse Webb, very quietly, by the window, whether he slept, and whether he's eating, and whether she should send for Dr. Lambert. She notices that his boots are getting tight. She'll write it in her book tonight.

Let's think about this beside a modern family. Many parents today, working long hours, rely on nurseries, childminders, after-school clubs and grandparents, and spend precious, tired hours with their children in the evenings and at weekends. Some of them worry, sometimes, that it isn't enough. In that sense, Caroline isn't as far from you as she might seem. The great difference is that for her, a certain distance from her children wasn't a compromise she made reluctantly. It was, in her world, simply the proper order of things. The nurse raised the children. The mother oversaw them.

Before she goes down, Caroline looks in on the night nursery next door, a smaller room with Charlie's little iron bed, with its white counterpane and its brass knobs, and Nurse Webb's bed against the far wall. Under Charlie's pillow, she can see the corner of something blue. It's his rabbit. She leaves it there.

The schoolroom is a floor below, on the second floor, and it's a different kind of room again. Plainer. There's a long table covered in green baize, a pair of globes, one of the earth and one of the heavens, a map of Europe on the wall, a bookcase full of schoolbooks, a blackboard, and an upright piano, a little out of tune. The windows look out over the street.

At the table sit Florence and Miss Lacy.

Miss Lacy is thirty-one. She's the daughter of a clergyman in Norfolk who died with very little money, and she's been a governess for eleven years, in four different families. She speaks good French, some German and a little Italian. She plays the piano well, and draws well, and is very quiet. She earns, in this house, a salary that's larger than a housemaid's but not as large as the butler's. And she lives in a strange position, somewhere between upstairs and downstairs, belonging fully to neither. She eats her meals in the schoolroom, with Florence. The servants think her proud. The family think her a servant. Very few people in the house ever talk to her about anything except Florence.

Her situation was a very common one, and it worried a good many Victorians. In 1843, a charity called the Governesses' Benevolent Institution was founded in London to help governesses who were old, or ill, or out of work. And a few years later, in 1848, the Institution's circle helped to found Queen's College, in Harley Street, to give young women who would become governesses a proper education of their own. It was one of the first steps towards higher education for women in England.

This morning, Florence has done her French, and her history, and her sums. Now she's at the piano, practising scales. Up and down, up and down, the same five notes, while Miss Lacy counts quietly, one and two and three and four. After luncheon, there will be drawing, and the use of the globes, and an hour of needlework, and a walk in the Square garden with Miss Lacy if it stays fine.

The history Florence learns is mostly a list of kings and dates and battles. Generations of English schoolgirls learned it from a book called Historical and Miscellaneous Questions, by a schoolmistress named Richmal Mangnall, first published at the very end of the eighteenth century and reprinted again and again. It worked by question and answer. A question was printed, and then the answer, and the girl learned the answer by heart, and recited it. Florence knows a great many answers. She can tell you the date of the Battle of Hastings and the name of Henry the Eighth's third wife. She's not sure she could tell you why any of it happened.

Caroline sits for a while in the window seat and listens to the scales, and then asks Florence to play the piece she's been learning. Florence plays it, a little too fast, with two wrong notes, and her mother praises her for the middle part and asks her to play the beginning again, more slowly. She does. It's better.

And now I want to bring in a book that sat on the shelves of a great many homes like this one, because it shows us how a woman like Caroline was supposed to think about all of this.

In 1839, a writer named Sarah Stickney Ellis published a book called The Women of England, Their Social Duties and Domestic Habits. It was the first in a series of hugely successful advice books for women, followed by The Daughters of England, The Wives of England and The Mothers of England. Ellis wrote, she said, for the women of the middle classes, especially those connected with trade and manufactures, who had a good education but no claim to high rank. And her central argument, which I'll paraphrase, was that the true power of English women lay in their moral influence at home. A woman, in Ellis's view, should make her home a place of order, comfort and goodness, should give her time and attention unselfishly to the needs of others, and should shape the characters of her husband and children by her quiet example. Her influence was not loud. It didn't appear in Parliament or the newspapers. But it was, Ellis believed, the foundation of the nation's virtue.

Caroline read Mrs. Ellis as a young wife, as many women did. She found some of it comforting, and some of it rather tiresome. She still agrees with a good deal of it. She'd certainly agree that what happens in this schoolroom and that nursery matters a great deal.

And here is where historians disagree, about books like this one.

For a long time, books by writers like Ellis were read almost as descriptions, as if they told us how middle-class women actually lived. But many scholars now see them quite differently. They describe Ellis's work as prescriptive: books that set out an ideal of how women ought to behave, rather than reporting how they did. One scholar has called her picture of middle-class domesticity a kind of fantasy. And yet the literary historians Karen Chase and Michael Levenson, in their book The Spectacle of Intimacy, published in 2000, placed Ellis alongside Charles Dickens as one of the deep designers of the mid-century family imagination. Her ideal, in other words, may not have described real homes, but it helped to shape how people dreamed about them, and judged themselves against them. Both of those views can be held at once, and many historians hold both.

There's one more true thing about mothers in this world that I'll tell you, very gently, because it's a quiet shadow over Caroline's love for her children. Until 1839, the same year Mrs. Ellis's book came out, an English mother had almost no legal right to her own children if her marriage broke down. The father had custody, and that was all. A campaign led by a remarkable writer named Caroline Norton, whose own husband had taken their sons from her, helped to pass the Custody of Infants Act of 1839, which for the first time allowed a separated mother to apply to the courts for custody of her children under the age of seven, and for access to older ones. It was a small, cautious law, hedged with conditions. But it was a beginning. Caroline Ashworth's marriage is a happy one, and she has never had to think about such things. But she knows the name of Caroline Norton. Everybody does.

Florence finishes her piece. Caroline kisses her, and tells Miss Lacy that Florence may come down to the drawing room at five, with Charlie, if her lessons are done. Miss Lacy says, "Yes, Mrs. Ashworth." And, after a pause, Caroline asks her whether she's heard from her mother in Norfolk lately. Miss Lacy, a little surprised, says that she has. Caroline says she's glad.

Then she goes down, through the house, past the closed door of Emily's room, where Emily is supposed to be resting and is in fact reading a novel. Past the drawing room. Down the wide front stairs. Through the hall, where the silver card tray sits on its marble table, polished and empty, catching the light from the fanlight over the front door.

A gong is sounding somewhere below. It's one o'clock. Time for luncheon.

## Part Six — Around One O'Clock: Luncheon, and a Basket for Pimlico

Here is the small question for this part of the day. After luncheon, before the afternoon calls, Caroline will step into her carriage with a covered basket on her lap and drive, not to a grander street, but to a poorer one. What's in the basket? And why is she, of all people, taking it there herself?

First, luncheon.

It's a quieter, smaller meal than breakfast, and in a house like this it's very much a ladies' meal, because the men are out. Edward is at Lincoln's Inn, and will eat a chop at his club or in the hall of his Inn. So the table in the dining room is laid for two: Caroline, and Emily, who has risen at last, rather pale, in a lilac morning dress. Florence and Miss Lacy will eat in the schoolroom, and Charlie and Nurse Webb in the nursery. And the servants are having their own dinner, their main meal of the day, at the long table in the servants' hall below, at one o'clock sharp, with Mr. Pryor at the head and Mrs. Hodges at the foot, and everyone seated in strict order of rank between them.

On the dining room table, there are cold things mostly, with one or two hot. Cold chicken. A tongue. A dish of cutlets. A salad. Bread and butter. Some stewed rhubarb with cream, because it's May. A glass of sherry for Caroline, and water for Emily. It's a sensible, thrifty meal, much of it made from what was left over from yesterday, so as not to waste anything, and so as not to burden the cook in the middle of a day when she has a dinner for twelve to think about.

They talk, mother and daughter, mostly about this afternoon and this evening. Who they will call on. What Emily will wear. Who will be at dinner. Emily wants to know whether Mr. Fenwick is coming tonight. Caroline says he is. Emily says nothing, and eats a very small piece of chicken, and her ears go pink. Caroline notices, and also says nothing.

We'll come back to Mr. Fenwick this evening.

After luncheon, Emily goes up to rest again before the calls, and Caroline puts on her bonnet and her mantle and her gloves, and Martin hands her a basket, and she goes down to the hall, where the carriage is waiting.

It isn't the big carriage, the one with the coat of arms on the door, which they keep for the evening. It's the brougham, a small, neat, closed carriage with one horse, driven by Dobson, the coachman, in his dark blue coat and his tall hat with a cockade. Thomas, the second footman, opens the door for her and lets down the step and hands her in, and closes the door, and climbs up beside Dobson on the box. And off they go, along Eaton Place, and then south.

The brougham is a fairly new kind of carriage, and it carries a famous name. It was designed around 1838 for the lawyer and politician Lord Brougham, a former Lord Chancellor, who wanted a light, closed carriage for town that could be pulled by a single horse. It caught on at once, and by the 1860s, the little black brougham was everywhere in the West End, the carriage of doctors, barristers and ladies who had calls to make. Edward likes to point out, a little too often, that it was invented by a lawyer. Caroline likes it because it can turn in a narrow street, and because, with only one horse, it costs a great deal less to keep than the big carriage.

Imagine you're sitting opposite her, in the little closed carriage, knee to knee. It's surprisingly quiet in here, with the windows up. The seats are padded in dark blue cloth, buttoned. There's a small looking-glass, and a little pocket for cards. And through the window, you can watch the streets changing as you go. The great cream terraces of Belgravia give way, within a few minutes, to smaller houses, plainer, older, in grey brick. Then the brick is darker and sootier. The streets are narrower. There are shops now, and public houses, and barrows in the road. Children without shoes. A woman with a baby on her hip, leaning in a doorway. The smell coming through the window changes too: horse dung, coal smoke, frying fish, drains. This is Pimlico, or the poorer parts of it, and the streets around the river and the railway lines towards Westminster. And it's barely a mile from Eaton Place.

That closeness was one of the great facts of Victorian London. The very rich and the very poor lived, very often, within a short walk of each other, though they might go a lifetime without meeting.

The carriage stops at the end of a narrow court, too narrow for it to enter. Thomas opens the door. Caroline gets down, with her basket, and walks, with Thomas a step behind her, along the court, between two rows of crowded little houses with washing hanging from the windows, to a door near the end. She knocks.

The woman who opens it is called Mrs. Doyle. She's a widow, Irish, about thirty-five, though she looks older. Her husband was a labourer on the railways, and he died last winter of a fever. She has four children, the eldest twelve and already at work, and she takes in washing. She knows Caroline, because Caroline has been here before. She's a name on the list of the parish visiting committee, and this street is Caroline's district.

And so, here's our answer. In the basket, under a white cloth, there's a cold roast chicken, left over from yesterday, wrapped in paper. A loaf. A pot of beef tea, made in Mrs. Bell's kitchen this morning, for the youngest child, who has been ill. A pound of tea and a pound of sugar, from the store-room Caroline unlocked at ten o'clock. A flannel petticoat, sewn by the ladies of the parish working party. And a small packet of tickets: a coal ticket, and a ticket for the dispensary, which will let Mrs. Doyle see a doctor for her child without paying.

And why is she taking it herself? Because in this world, for a woman of Caroline's class, charity of this kind, personal, local, organised by the parish, carried out by visiting, was one of the most respectable and expected things she could do. District visiting, it was called. The ladies of a parish divided its poorer streets into districts, and each lady took one, and visited the families in it, regularly, bringing help, and advice, and tracts, and keeping a record of who was sick, who was out of work, who had children who should be in school. In many parishes across England, in these years, the poor were visited this way by a great army of comfortable women.

Caroline goes in. The room is small, and dark, and very clean, and very damp. There's a smell of soap and wet linen, because there's washing in a tub by the fire. The youngest child, a little girl of three, is in a box bed by the wall, coughing. Caroline sits on the one good chair and asks how she does, and listens, and gives the basket, and writes something in a small notebook. She stays for a quarter of an hour. When she leaves, Mrs. Doyle thanks her, and means it. And when the door closes, Mrs. Doyle sits down by her washtub and is, perhaps, a little relieved that the lady has gone.

Both of those things are true.

I want to tell you now about a real woman who, in this very year, was beginning something new in exactly this kind of street. Her name was Octavia Hill. In 1865, she was twenty-six years old, an artist and a teacher, the granddaughter of a famous sanitary reformer. She had become convinced that the worst thing about the lives of the London poor was their housing: damp, crowded, filthy rooms, let by landlords who never repaired them. And she persuaded the great art critic John Ruskin, for whom she'd worked copying paintings, to put up the money for an experiment. In 1865, using money from Ruskin, the leases of three small houses, six rooms each, were bought for seven hundred and fifty pounds, in a street called Paradise Place, in Marylebone, about two miles north of here. Sources differ slightly on the date of the purchase, with some placing it at the very end of 1864, but English Heritage, which has a blue plaque for her there, dates the start of her first housing scheme to 1865. The street has since been renamed Garbutt Place. Ruskin asked for a return of five per cent on his money from the rents, and the rest was put back into the houses. Hill repaired them, cleaned them, and let them to poor families, and she, and later a team of women she trained, collected the rents in person, every week, and got to know every tenant. A year later, Ruskin bought five more houses for her to manage in nearby Freshwater Place.

Octavia Hill would go on to become one of the most influential housing reformers of the century, and later, one of the three founders of the National Trust. She wrote about her methods in a book called Homes of the London Poor, published in 1875. Her approach was stern, in some ways. She believed in self-reliance, and she was firm with tenants who didn't pay. But it was also personal, and patient, and based on the idea that a lady visiting a poor family should be something like a friend.

Caroline hasn't heard of Octavia Hill yet. Few people have, in May 1865. But in ten years, she will have.

And here is where historians disagree about everything we've just seen.

For a long time, many historians looked at Victorian ladies' charity with a rather cool eye. Some saw it as a kind of social control: an attempt by the comfortable classes to watch over the poor, to teach them thrift and sobriety and religion, and to keep them in their place, rather than to change the conditions that made them poor. The historian Gareth Stedman Jones, in his influential book Outcast London, published in 1971, looked closely at how the better-off of London saw the poor in this period, and how fear and moral judgement were mixed into their charity. But other historians have pushed back. Frank Prochaska, in his book Women and Philanthropy in Nineteenth-Century England, published in 1980, argued that charitable work was one of the great fields in which Victorian women of the comfortable classes gained real experience, real responsibility, and real public influence, long before they had the vote. Visiting, committees, fund-raising, bazaars, accounts: this was where many women learned to organise, and to speak, and to manage, outside their own homes. In his view, philanthropy was not only something done to the poor, but also something that changed the women who did it. Most historians today would say both things are true: that the visiting could be intrusive and judgemental, and that it was also, for many women, a genuine form of work, and a door to a wider world.

Let's think about this beside your own life. Today, when you want to help someone in need, perhaps you give to a charity by direct debit, or tap a card at a collection box, or donate to a food bank at the supermarket. The giving is often anonymous, and the people who receive it never see your face. In Caroline's world, it was almost the opposite. Charity was personal. You went yourself. You knew the family's names, and their children's names, and the state of their chimney. That had its kindness, and it had its burdens, for both sides of the door.

Caroline gets back into the brougham. Thomas closes the door. Dobson turns the horse, and they go back, up through the narrow streets, the brick getting cleaner and paler with every turn, until they're among the cream stucco again, as if nothing they'd seen had been real.

She sits back in the carriage and takes out her notebook and writes a line in it. The child's cough. The coal ticket. A note to ask the vicar about the eldest boy. Then she closes it, and puts it away, and looks out of the window.

When she gets home, she has just half an hour. She goes up, and Martin takes off her bonnet and mantle, and brushes her dress, and helps her into a different one, of grey silk with a deep trim of black lace, more suitable for the afternoon. A different bonnet, with ribbons. Fresh gloves, pale lavender kid, buttoned at the wrist. And from the drawer of her dressing table, a small, flat, engraved silver case, about the size of a playing card.

Inside the case, in a neat stack, are her visiting cards.

Down in the hall, on the marble table, the silver card tray is still empty. But not for long. Because in houses all over Belgravia and Mayfair, at this very moment, other ladies are putting on their afternoon bonnets too, and slipping their own card cases into their pockets.

It's nearly three o'clock. The calling hour has come.

## Part Seven — Around Three O'Clock: Calls, Cards and the Art of Not Going In

Here is the small question for this hour. When a lady pays a call on another lady in the afternoon, sitting in her drawing room, making conversation, how long is she supposed to stay? And how does she know when to go?

The big carriage is at the door now. It's an open carriage, since the afternoon is fine and dry, with a folding hood, painted dark green and black, with the Ashworth crest, a small one, on the door panel, and two bay horses in silver-mounted harness, tossing their heads. Dobson is up on the box in his best coat. James and Thomas, both of them this time, stand at the back in their full afternoon livery, with white gloves and silk stockings, and gold braid on their coats. In the very grandest houses, footmen still powdered their hair for occasions like this. The Ashworths don't go quite that far. A carriage like this, in the London Season, isn't just a way of getting about. It's a statement. It says, to everyone in the street, this family is here, and this is who they are.

Caroline and Emily come down the front steps together. Emily, rested and restored, is in pale blue, with a white bonnet trimmed with forget-me-nots. Caroline is in her grey silk and black lace. They settle into the carriage, side by side, and arrange their skirts, which takes some time, because together they fill it entirely. Caroline has her silver card case in her hand, and a small list on a slip of paper, written this morning at her desk. Eight names. Eight houses. Two hours.

Off they go.

The calling hours in London society were the afternoon, after luncheon and before the drive in the Park. In this world, and confusingly for us, these were still often called "morning calls," because in the older sense of the word, "morning" meant the whole part of the day before dinner. Mrs. Beeton, in her chapter on the mistress, wrote that after luncheon, morning calls and visits may be made and received, and she divided them under three heads. In her words, they are "those of ceremony, friendship, and congratulation or condolence." Each kind had its own rules. And the most important of those rules, for a visit of ceremony, was that it should be short.

The first house is in Chester Square. The carriage draws up. And here's where it begins.

Imagine that you're sitting in the carriage beside Caroline, under the folded hood, looking up at the house. It's much like her own: tall, cream, with a columned porch. Caroline doesn't get out. Instead, James jumps down from the back, goes up the steps, and knocks. It's a loud, confident knock, several raps in quick succession. Old accounts of London life say you could tell who was at a door by the knock alone, the postman's double rap, the tradesman's single humble one, and the footman's long, showy flourish for a lady in a carriage. I can't vouch for every detail of that, but it's how the story was told. The door opens. A footman appears. James asks whether Mrs. Calthorpe is at home.

The other footman says, very gravely, that Mrs. Calthorpe is not at home.

And here's the curious thing. Mrs. Calthorpe is at home. She's upstairs in her drawing room right now. Both footmen know it. Caroline knows it. In fact, everyone knows it. "Not at home," in this world, didn't mean that the lady was out. It meant that she wasn't receiving visitors this afternoon. It was a polite fiction, understood by everyone, that allowed a lady to decline a visit without anyone being offended. Very like the third-person note, in fact. A formula that does the awkward work, so that no one has to say "I'd rather not see you."

So Caroline opens her card case.

Inside are two kinds of card. Her own, which are about three inches wide, of thin, fine white card, engraved in a plain copperplate script with just her name, Mrs. Edward Ashworth, and, in the corner, in smaller letters, the address. And her husband's, which are smaller and narrower, and which say Mr. Ashworth, Q.C., and his club. She takes out one of her own, and two of her husband's, and hands them to James, who takes them up the steps and gives them to the Calthorpes' footman, who will carry them in and lay them on his own family's silver card tray in the hall.

Why one of hers and two of his? Because, according to the custom of the time, a lady left one of her own cards for the lady of the house, and her husband's cards for both the lady and the gentleman of the house, since a lady didn't call on a gentleman, but her husband's card could. If there were a grown-up daughter in the house, or another lady living there, more cards might be needed. One compilation of etiquette advice from this period puts it simply: on an ordinary visit, you leave a single card, but if there be living in the family a married daughter, an unmarried sister, a guest, or anyone else in a separate position from the mistress of the house, you leave two cards, one for each.

And today, because Emily is with her, and Emily is out this Season, Caroline does one more thing. With her gloved fingers, she bends down one corner of the top card, just a little.

And the carriage drives on.

That's it. The visit is over. It lasted about forty seconds, and Caroline never left her seat.

So here, at last, is the second of our three mysteries. Why would a lady order her carriage, drive across London to a friend's front door, hand a small white card to a servant, and then drive away again without ever going inside, and count it as an afternoon well spent?

Because in this world, the card was the call. Leaving a card, in person, at someone's door, was a complete social act in itself. It said: I have come to your house. I acknowledge you. Our acquaintance continues. And it put the other lady under an obligation, gently, to return it, with her own card, or with a visit, within a reasonable time. A lady's whole circle of acquaintance was kept alive in this way, week after week, through hundreds of small white cards passing from hall to hall, across the silver trays of London. If Caroline stopped leaving cards at a house, the acquaintance would quietly wither, and everyone would understand that it was meant to. If she began leaving them at a new house, she was opening a door.

And the turned-down corner? That's where it gets complicated, and I'll be honest with you about it. The manuals of this period don't fully agree. In some accounts, folding down a corner showed that you had called in person, rather than sending your card by a servant. In others, the meaning depended on which corner. And one compilation of the time describes ladies pinching down one corner of a card to show that the visit was meant for only one of two ladies in the house, and calls that a "transient mode," a passing fashion. By later in the century, some families even had words printed in the corners, so that nobody could misread them. So when Caroline bends that corner, she knows exactly what she means by it, and so does Mrs. Calthorpe. But I can't promise you that every lady in London meant the same thing.

The next house is in Upper Belgrave Street. This time, the footman says that Lady Ormsby is at home.

So Caroline and Emily get out. They're led up the stairs to a large first-floor drawing room, much like their own. Lady Ormsby rises to greet them. There are two other ladies already there, also on calls, in their bonnets. Nobody takes off her bonnet on a call. Nobody takes off her gloves either, or not usually. Nobody is offered anything to eat. Caroline and Emily sit down, on chairs a little apart, and the conversation begins: the weather, the Royal Academy's exhibition, which opened at the beginning of the month and which everyone has been to see, a wedding at St George's, Hanover Square, the dreadful news from America, and, very lightly, the Derby, which is two weeks away.

And that's the answer to our small question. How long does a lady stay? For a visit of ceremony like this one, the guides of the period are clear that it should be short. Many people of this world spoke of a quarter of an hour as about right. Long enough to be civil. Short enough not to be a burden. And how does she know when to go? When another visitor arrives, very often. That was the signal. A new caller comes in, the earlier caller finishes her sentence, rises, takes her leave, and goes. And so the drawing rooms of London, all afternoon long, filled and emptied like the chambers of a heart.

Twelve minutes after they arrived, a new lady is announced, and Caroline rises, and they go.

Six more houses. At two of them, the lady is not at home, and cards are left. At one, the family is in mourning, for a grandfather who died in March, and so this is a visit of condolence, and Caroline only leaves cards, with a few kind words written on the back of one, because a house in deep mourning doesn't receive. At another, an elderly cousin of Edward's is at home, and very deaf, and very pleased to see them, and they stay twenty minutes, which is more than they should, because she's lonely. At another, a young bride, married in April, receives them for the first time in her new house, in Hill Street, and that's a visit of congratulation.

And at the last, a house in Grosvenor Crescent, the lady at home is the mother of Mr. Fenwick.

Caroline stays exactly fifteen minutes. She's charming. Emily says very little, and blushes at the right moment, and admires a watercolour. Mrs. Fenwick watches Emily very carefully, with a small, polite smile, and says she understands they are to meet again this evening, at Eaton Place. Caroline says she hopes so. They leave.

In the carriage, neither of them says anything about it at all, for a full minute. Then Emily says, "Do you think she liked me?" And Caroline says, "I think she found you perfectly charming, my dear, which is exactly what you were." And Emily looks out of the window, smiling.

Let's set all of this beside your own life. Think of the small signals you send and receive every day. A message read. A like on a photograph. A reply to a group chat, or the absence of one. A friend who always remembers your birthday, and one who's started to forget. A small, quick gesture that says, I see you, we're still connected. Caroline's cards did very much the same work. The great difference is that her signals were physical, made of card, carried by footmen, delivered to a door, and laid on a silver tray in a hall, where anyone passing could see who had called.

And here is where historians disagree about what all this meant.

In 1973, the historian Leonore Davidoff, who would later write Family Fortunes, published a book called The Best Circles, about Society, etiquette and the London Season in the nineteenth century. She argued that all this calling and card-leaving was far from trivial. It was a system, run very largely by women, for deciding who was in Society and who was out. The hostess, in her view, was a kind of gatekeeper, controlling access to the dinners and balls and drawing rooms where marriages were made and careers were advanced. Her cards and calls were the machinery of a whole class, keeping its boundaries in place. Other historians, looking at the flood of etiquette manuals that poured out in these decades, have suggested a different emphasis. Many of those manuals, they point out, were written for newcomers: families who had made money in trade or industry and were anxious to learn the rules. The very number of the books may tell us less about how the old families behaved, and more about how nervous everyone else was. Both views can be held together. The rules mattered because people were watching. And people were watching because the rules mattered.

It's nearly five o'clock now. The list on the slip of paper has a pencil line through every name. Caroline's card case is lighter by about twenty cards.

And when the carriage draws up at last at their own front door in Eaton Place, and James hands them down, and Mr. Pryor opens the door, Caroline glances, as she always does, at the marble table in the hall.

The silver card tray is no longer empty. While they were out, other ladies' carriages have stopped at this door too. And on the polished silver now lies a small, neat drift of white cards. Five. Six. Seven. Caroline picks them up, one by one, and reads the names. One of them has a corner turned down.

She smiles, and lays them back on the tray, and goes up. It's time for tea.

## Part Eight — Around Five O'Clock: Tea, Two Children and the Drive in the Park

Here's the small question for this hour of the afternoon. In a house with a butler and two footmen to do everything, there is one small task, at five o'clock, that the lady of the house almost always does with her own hands. What is it?

The drawing room is on the first floor, at the front, and it's the grandest room in the house. It runs the whole depth of the building, two rooms really, joined by a wide arch, with tall windows opening onto the balcony over the street. The walls are hung with a pale gold paper. There's a great gilt looking-glass over the white marble chimneypiece, and on the chimneypiece, a gilt clock under a glass dome, flanked by a pair of tall vases. There are sofas and chairs, more than seem possible, covered in rose-coloured silk damask, and little tables everywhere, each with something on it: a lamp, a vase of flowers, a box of mother-of-pearl, an album of photographs. There's a grand piano, of rosewood, near the back windows. There's a harp, which nobody plays. And everywhere, on the walls, the tables, the chimneypiece, there are things: pictures, miniatures, china figures, ferns under glass, shells, a stuffed bird under a dome. A drawing room of 1865 was meant to be full.

Caroline sits on a low chair by the fire, which has been lit, because even in May the evenings can be cool. Emily sits on the sofa opposite, with her bonnet off at last.

The door opens, and James carries in the tea tray. He sets it on a small table at Caroline's side. On it there's a silver teapot, a silver kettle on a little stand with a spirit lamp under it, a silver sugar basin and tongs, a cream jug, and a set of fine china cups and saucers, white, with a band of gold. On a second tray, a plate of thin bread and butter, cut almost transparent, and a few small cakes. James sets it all down, and bows very slightly, and goes out, and closes the door.

And Caroline pours the tea.

That's the answer to our small question. At afternoon tea in a house like this, the servant brought the tray, and then withdrew, and the lady of the house made and poured the tea herself. She warmed the pot from the silver kettle, measured the leaves from a caddy, poured on the boiling water, waited, and poured out the cups with her own hands, asking each person how they liked it. It was one of the very few times in the day when a lady in a great house actually handled food and drink for others. And that was part of the charm of it. Five o'clock tea was a small, informal, intimate ritual, more relaxed than any other meal of the day, and it belonged to the women.

Imagine you're sitting on the sofa beside Emily now, and Caroline turns to you with a cup and saucer in her hand. "Milk? Sugar?" You take it. The china is so thin that you can see the shadow of the tea through it when you hold it up to the window. The tea is hot, and strong, and fragrant, and it tastes, after the long afternoon of calls, quite wonderful. You take a slice of the transparent bread and butter. It dissolves on your tongue. And for a moment, in the soft light of the drawing room, with the noise of the street muffled by the tall windows, everything feels very calm.

Now, you've almost certainly heard the story of where this custom came from. And here is our point of disagreement for this part of the day, because it's a lovely story that historians have come to doubt.

The story goes like this. In the early 1840s, Anna, Duchess of Bedford, one of the Queen's ladies, found that she grew hungry in the long gap between luncheon and the dinner hour, which was getting later and later in fashionable houses. So she began to have tea and a little something to eat brought to her room in the late afternoon. She invited friends to join her. And the custom spread, and became the English afternoon tea.

It's told everywhere, in books and on menus and on the backs of tea packets. But historians and food writers have pointed out some problems. For one thing, people in England had been drinking tea in the afternoon long before the Duchess was born. There are advertisements and complaints about afternoon tea-drinking going back to the 1750s. And for another, the best-known piece of evidence linking the Duchess to the custom comes from someone else entirely. The actress Fanny Kemble, writing many years later in her memoirs, published in 1882, recalled a visit to Belvoir Castle in March 1842, at which she was invited, rather secretly, to the Duchess of Bedford's private room for tea in the late afternoon. And Kemble wrote that she didn't believe the custom of five o'clock tea went back any further than that "very private and I think rather shame-faced practice," as she called it. I've given you only that short phrase directly, because I haven't been able to check the rest of her wording against the book. But you can see what's happened. Kemble was describing something that seemed new to her, in her own circle. Later writers turned it into an invention. Most historians today would say that the Duchess didn't invent afternoon tea, but that she, and women like her, helped to make the late-afternoon tea a fashionable, regular, ladylike custom, at just the moment when dinner was moving later into the evening.

Caroline knows the story, in its usual form, and believes it. Everybody does.

There's a knock at the door, and Nurse Webb comes in with Charlie, and Miss Lacy with Florence.

Charlie has been scrubbed. His face shines. His hair has been brushed flat with water and has already begun to spring up again at the crown. He's wearing a dark blue velvet suit with a wide white lace collar, which he hates, and white socks, and black buttoned shoes. He walks across the room very carefully, as he's been told to, and says good afternoon to his mother, and to his sister, and is allowed to sit on a footstool by his mother's chair and have a small piece of cake. Florence sits by Emily, and is given a cup of very weak tea, mostly milk. Nurse Webb and Miss Lacy withdraw to the far end of the room, by the window, to wait.

This is the children's hour. It's not quite an hour today. But for the next forty minutes, Charlie and Florence belong entirely to their mother. Caroline asks Florence about her drawing lesson, and admires a sketch of the plane tree in the garden. She asks Charlie about the Battle of Waterloo, and is told that the French lost again, in the afternoon, very badly. Charlie asks if he can see the clock. Caroline lifts him up to look at the gilt clock on the chimneypiece, under its glass dome, with its little figures on top, and its pendulum swinging behind a tiny window, and he watches it for a long time, absolutely silent, the way children sometimes watch things that grown-ups have stopped seeing.

Let's set this beside modern life. Many people today take a break in the afternoon too: a coffee at a desk, a cup of tea in the staff kitchen, a snack in the car on the school run, a pause to scroll through a phone. It's often hurried, and often alone. Caroline's tea hour is neither. It's slow, and it's shared, and for a little while, the work of the day is set aside. I think that's one reason the custom has lasted so long. It isn't really about the tea at all.

At a quarter to six, Nurse Webb comes forward, and Charlie knows what that means. He kisses his mother, and his sisters, and is led away, upstairs, to his nursery supper of bread and milk, and his bath in front of the nursery fire, and his bed.

He'll be the first in the house to fall asleep tonight.

Florence goes up with Miss Lacy to finish her lessons. And Caroline and Emily, who still have an hour before they must begin to dress for dinner, go down to the carriage again. It's the hour for the Park.

In the London Season, between about five and seven in the evening, the place to be seen was Hyde Park, a short drive north from Belgravia. The carriages of fashionable London drove slowly round the Ring and along the Drive, in a long, glittering procession, the ladies sitting back under their parasols, bowing to their acquaintances as they passed. Alongside, on the sandy track called Rotten Row, gentlemen and some ladies rode on horseback, in top hats and long riding habits, up and down, up and down. It wasn't really about getting anywhere. It was about being seen, and seeing. Who was in town. Who was with whom. Who had a new carriage, a new horse, a new bonnet. Who had stopped to speak to whom.

The name Rotten Row has puzzled people for a long time. You'll often read that it comes from the French route du roi, the King's road, because the track was once part of a royal road from the palace at Westminster towards Kensington. It's a charming explanation, and you'll see it repeated with great confidence. But people who study the history of English words are much more cautious about it. Other Rotten Rows exist in other English towns, far from any king, and the word "rotten" has older meanings to do with soft, loose ground, which would suit a sandy riding track very well. So, as with so many lovely stories, the honest answer is that nobody is quite sure. The Serpentine, beside it, is less mysterious. It was made in the 1730s, for Queen Caroline, the wife of King George the Second, by damming a small river, and its long curving shape gave it its name.

Caroline, who shares that Queen's name, has always been rather fond of it.

Caroline and Emily drive twice round. They bow to Lady Ormsby. They bow to the Calthorpes, who were not at home this afternoon, and who are now very much at home, in an open carriage by the Serpentine. And on the Row, riding a tall chestnut horse, with his hat in his hand as the carriage passes, is Mr. Fenwick. He doesn't stop. That would be too much. But he bows, and smiles, and Emily bows back, and for the next five minutes of the drive, she can't stop smiling at all.

There's someone who is not in the Park this afternoon, and whose absence everyone in this procession can feel. The Queen.

Since the death of her husband, Prince Albert, in December 1861, Queen Victoria has withdrawn almost completely from the public life of London. She's still in deep mourning, three and a half years on. She lives mostly at Windsor, at Osborne on the Isle of Wight, and at Balmoral in Scotland, and she very rarely appears in the capital. The Queen's Drawing Rooms at St James's Palace, the great formal receptions at which ladies were presented at Court, go on without her a good deal of the time. A newspaper court report from the end of this very month tells us that one Drawing Room, announced to be held on the Queen's behalf by her daughter Princess Alice, now Princess Louis of Hesse, has been put off until the twenty-seventh of June. And a week from now, the same reports say, the Queen will arrive at Balmoral, on the twenty-third of May, and spend her birthday there the next day, the twenty-fourth, when the servants of the Balmoral estate and their families will come up to the castle in the morning to offer her their congratulations. The court news, in these years, is full of such small, quiet, faraway details. London society has learned to carry on its Season without her.

Caroline doesn't mind as much as some people do. She was presented to the Queen herself, as a girl of seventeen, in 1842, the year of the Duchess of Bedford's tea at Belvoir, as it happens. She remembers the Queen as very small, and very young, and very bright-eyed. She thinks of her now, sometimes, at Windsor, in her black dress, with a kind of sympathy.

The carriage turns for home. Back along the Drive. Out at Hyde Park Corner, past Apsley House, the great house of the late Duke of Wellington, whom Charlie defeated the French with this afternoon. Down Grosvenor Place, into Belgravia, and along Eaton Place, and stops at the door.

In the hall, the silver card tray has two more cards on it, left in the last hour. And beside it, a telegram, in its buff envelope. Caroline's heart turns over for a second, as everyone's did in these years when a telegram came. Then she sees it's addressed to her husband, and knows that it's probably from his clerk.

It's half past six. Edward is home. She can hear his voice in the library, and the slow, deep laugh she has known for twenty years. In an hour and a quarter, the first guests will arrive.

And she isn't dressed.

## Part Nine — Around Half Past Six: Silk, Steel and Diamonds

The small question for this hour is a practical one. How long does it take a lady of 1865 to change from her afternoon dress into her evening dress, and how many people does it take to do it?

Let's go up and see.

Caroline's bedroom has changed since the morning. The bed has been made, the counterpane smooth and white. The curtains are open, and the evening light is long and golden, coming in low across the roofs of the mews. A fresh can of hot water stands steaming on the washstand. The fire is lit. And laid out across the bed, as carefully as if it were a sleeping person, is the evening dress.

It's silk, a deep, shimmering moiré, the colour of claret, with a watered pattern in the weave that moves like ripples on a pond when the light catches it. The bodice is cut low and wide across the shoulders, as evening dresses were in these years, with short sleeves and a fall of fine black lace around the neckline. The skirt is vast, many yards of silk, made to spread over the crinoline in a smooth, gleaming dome, with a train that will sweep behind her on the carpet. Beside it on the bed lie a pair of long white kid gloves, a lace handkerchief, a fan of carved ivory and black silk, and a pair of satin evening slippers.

Martin is waiting. And this time, she has help.

Imagine you're standing by the fireplace, out of the way, watching. First Caroline must wash again, at the basin, and Martin unhooks the grey afternoon dress, which closes at the back, and lifts it carefully away over her head, and lays it aside. Then the afternoon petticoats come off. The day crinoline stays, or is changed for a slightly larger evening one, which has been hanging in the wardrobe like a great birdcage. The corset is unlaced a little, and then laced again, more firmly, for the evening bodice, which is cut very close. Then a fresh petticoat. Then the evening skirt, lowered over her head by Martin and by Ellen, the under-housemaid, who has been called up to help, because one person cannot manage so much silk without crushing it. Then the bodice, which is a separate piece, hooked up the back with a long row of tiny hooks and eyes, perhaps two dozen of them, that Caroline couldn't possibly reach herself.

Then her hair. The morning knot comes down. Martin brushes it out again, and arranges it for the evening, in a more elaborate style: smooth over the ears, coiled and plaited at the back, held in a net of fine black silk, with a little ornament of jet and gold pinned into it. That alone takes twenty minutes.

And so, here's the answer to our small question. Changing for dinner, for a lady of Caroline's standing, on an evening when she's giving a dinner party, might take the best part of an hour. And it took at least one other person, her maid, and on a night like this, two. A lady of 1865 simply could not dress herself for the evening without help. The hooks were at the back. The laces were at the back. The skirt was too large to lift alone. Her clothes, in a sense, were designed on the assumption that someone else would be there.

Let's think about this beside your own evening. When you get ready to go out, perhaps it takes ten minutes, or twenty, or, on a very special occasion, an hour, with a shower and a hairdryer and a mirror. You can almost certainly do every bit of it yourself. Zips, buttons at the front, stretch fabrics, clothes that slip on over your head. Most of that is quite recent. For Caroline, getting dressed was a collaboration.

Now, there's one more thing to go on. Martin brings out a flat, square case of dark red leather from the locked drawer of the dressing table, and opens it. Inside, on a bed of white velvet, lies a necklace of diamonds, a single row of stones graduating in size towards the centre, with a larger drop hanging at the front. There are earrings to match. Martin fastens the necklace at the back of Caroline's neck, and the stones catch the firelight and the evening sun all at once, and throw tiny sparks of colour across the white of her shoulders.

It was Edward's gift, on their tenth wedding anniversary. And here's a strange, true thing about that necklace, which I want to tell you carefully.

Legally, in 1865, it is not entirely Caroline's.

Under the English common law of this time, a married woman lived under what lawyers called coverture. When she married, her legal identity was, in a sense, covered by her husband's. Most of the personal property she brought into the marriage, and most of what she earned or was given afterwards, belonged in law to him. She couldn't, in general, make a contract in her own name, or sue, or be sued, without him. The law did recognise certain personal things, her clothes and some ornaments, as a special category, but even these were hedged with limits. Wealthy families got around much of this through marriage settlements, legal agreements drawn up before the wedding, which put some property in trust for the wife's use. Caroline's father, a careful man, insisted on one. But the basic rule was clear. A married woman, in law, owned very little in her own right.

That was beginning to change, slowly. In 1857, the Matrimonial Causes Act set up a new divorce court in London, and gave some protection to the earnings and property of a wife who had been deserted by her husband. And then, five years after our day, came a law that would have interested Caroline very much. The Married Women's Property Act of 1870, which received the royal assent on the ninth of August that year, allowed a married woman, among other things, to keep her own earnings as her own separate property. It was a limited law, and it left a great deal unchanged. But twelve years later, the Married Women's Property Act of 1882, which received the royal assent on the eighteenth of August 1882, and came into force on the first of January 1883, went much further. It allowed a married woman in England, Wales and Ireland to own and control property in her own right, just as if she were single. Scotland had passed its own law a year earlier.

So when Caroline looks at the diamonds in the glass tonight, she's looking at something that, for nearly another twenty years, the law would treat more as her husband's than her own. She doesn't think about it. Not tonight. But the law was there, all the same, like the steel hoops under the silk.

And speaking of steel hoops, here's our point of disagreement for this part of the day.

For a long time, the corset and the crinoline were held up as the very symbols of how Victorian women were confined. The tight-laced corset, critics said, crushed women's ribs and organs and made them faint. The crinoline, many yards wide, made it impossible to move freely, to work, to climb stairs easily, or even to sit in comfort. Dress reformers of the time said so, loudly, and many historians and writers of the twentieth century agreed. The clothes, in this view, were a cage in a quite literal sense.

But other historians of fashion have argued that the picture was more complicated. The fashion historian Valerie Steele, in her book The Corset: A Cultural History, published in 2001, argued that the corset was not simply an instrument of oppression forced on women by men. Many women chose to wear it, and valued it, as part of looking respectable, elegant, and grown up, and the most extreme tight-lacing was probably much rarer than the horrified reports of the time suggested. And the cage crinoline itself, when it arrived in the 1850s, was greeted by many women as a liberation: one light frame of steel instead of six or seven heavy, hot petticoats. It let the legs move freely underneath. Historians still argue about how much these clothes restricted women, and how much they expressed something women wanted. Probably, again, both are true.

The crinoline did have one real danger, and I'll mention it only briefly. Its wide, light skirts, if they swung too near an open fire or a candle, could catch alight, and there were accidents in these years, some of them terrible. Martin, like every careful lady's maid, has a habit of standing between her mistress and the fire whenever Caroline turns round in her evening dress. She does it now, without a word, as Caroline rises from the dressing table.

It's half past seven. Caroline stands in front of the long glass, and looks at herself, the claret silk, the black lace, the diamonds. She draws on the long white gloves, and Martin buttons them at the wrist with a buttonhook. Caroline takes the fan, and the handkerchief.

You may have heard that Victorian ladies had a secret language of the fan: that a fan held open in the left hand meant one thing, a fan drawn across the cheek meant another, and a fan snapped shut meant something else again, so that a lady could flirt across a crowded ballroom without saying a word. Little printed guides to this language really were published in the nineteenth century, and they were great fun. But historians who've looked into it suspect that they were mostly a novelty, invented and sold by fan-makers and publishers, and that very few women ever actually used such a code. Caroline's fan has a simpler purpose. Dining rooms lit by candles and gas, with twelve people and a hot dinner in them, became very warm indeed. A fan was a small, private breeze.

"Thank you, Martin. That will do very well."

Across the landing, in her own room, Emily is being dressed too, by Ellen and by Sarah, the head housemaid, who has a quick hand with hair. Emily is in white tulle, as was usual for a girl in her first Season, with a few pale pink rosebuds at her waist and in her hair, and a single string of small pearls. She is pale with nerves and very lovely. When her mother comes in to look at her, Caroline says only, "Yes," and straightens one rosebud. That's all. Emily understands.

And down the corridor, Edward has dressed himself, more or less, with the help of James, in a black tailcoat and trousers, a white waistcoat, a white shirt with a stiff front, and a white tie, which James ties for him. A gentleman's evening dress in 1865 was already very close to what it would remain, for formal occasions, for the next hundred years and more.

Let me tell you about the house at this moment, because every part of it is working at full pitch.

Up at the very top, it's quiet. Charlie is asleep. He fell asleep at a quarter past seven, in his little iron bed, with his rabbit under his cheek and Nurse Webb's hand on his back. He's the first of the household to fall asleep tonight. Nurse Webb is sitting by the night-nursery fire, darning, with the door open.

On the second floor, Florence is having her supper on a tray in the schoolroom with Miss Lacy, bread and butter and a boiled egg and a glass of milk, and is very cross that she's not allowed to come down to dinner, and is allowed, by special favour, to sit on the stairs later and watch the guests arrive.

But below, in the basement, the kitchen is a furnace. Mrs. Bell has been at the range since four. The soups are ready, in their great pans. The salmon, which came this morning on a slab of ice, is poaching in its long fish kettle. The saddle of lamb is turning on the spit in front of the fire. Annie is beating cream. The scullery maid who comes in by the day is washing pans as fast as they're used. Two extra men, waiters hired for the evening, as many households hired them for big dinners, have come in from an agency and are being given their orders by Mr. Pryor in the pantry. The dining room table has been extended with two extra leaves, and laid, and laid again, until it's perfect.

And in the front hall, by the door, the silver card tray has been cleared of the afternoon's cards, and set back in its place on the marble table, shining. Mr. Pryor has lit the gas. The hall is golden.

Caroline goes down the stairs, slowly, one hand on the banister, the train of her claret silk sliding behind her on the carpet. She stops on the half-landing and looks down into the bright hall, at the door, at the tray, at Mr. Pryor waiting in his black coat and white gloves.

It's twenty to eight. And there, at the door, is the first knock.

## Part Ten — Around a Quarter to Eight: Dinner for Twelve

Here is the small question for the beginning of dinner. When dinner is announced, and twelve people go down the stairs from the drawing room to the dining room, two by two, who goes first? And why is the hostess, in her own house, the very last to go in?

The guests have been arriving for a quarter of an hour. Each one comes in at the front door, past the silver card tray gleaming on its marble table under the gas, is relieved of hat and cloak and wrap in the hall, and is led up the stairs by James, who announces them in a clear, carrying voice at the drawing-room door. Caroline stands just inside, near the fire, to receive them, with Edward beside her. She gives each one her gloved hand and a word of welcome. Florence, in her nightgown and a shawl, is sitting at the very top of the stairs, three floors up, peering down through the banisters at the bonnets and bald heads below, as she was promised she might.

Let me tell you who has come, and I should say first that every one of them, like the Ashworths, is invented.

Sir George Halford, who is a judge, and gouty, and very good company, with Lady Halford, Caroline's oldest friend, who is laughing about his foot. Lord and Lady Ormsby, whom Caroline and Emily called on this afternoon, and who are the grandest guests of the evening, because he is a peer. Mr. and Mrs. Calthorpe, who were not at home at three o'clock and are very much at home now. Mr. Dunstan, a young Member of Parliament, unmarried, clever and rather pleased with himself, who will be useful for talking to everyone. Mrs. Fenwick, a widow, in grey silk and amethysts. And her son, Mr. Arthur Fenwick, twenty-six, tall, quiet, a junior barrister in Edward's own chambers, who has been talking to Emily by the piano for six minutes now, which everyone in the room has noticed.

Twelve, counting the family.

At a quarter to eight, Mr. Pryor appears at the drawing-room door, and says, quietly but very clearly, "Dinner is served."

And now comes the procession.

Edward offers his arm to Lady Ormsby, because she's the lady of highest rank in the room, the wife of a peer. They go first. Behind them, in an order Caroline has worked out with great care at her desk this morning, the other couples follow: Sir George, limping, with Mrs. Fenwick; Mr. Calthorpe with Lady Halford; Mr. Dunstan with Mrs. Calthorpe; and Mr. Arthur Fenwick, by a happy arrangement that no one will mention, with Miss Ashworth, Emily. And last of all, at the very end, Caroline herself, on the arm of Lord Ormsby, the gentleman of highest rank.

That's the answer to our small question. In a dinner party of this kind, the host led the way with the most important lady guest, and the hostess came last with the most important gentleman. Precedence, the order of rank, decided who took whom in, and the books of etiquette and the printed tables of precedence in reference books set it out in great detail: a duke's daughter before an earl's, a bishop before a baron, a judge's wife according to her husband's office. Get it wrong, and someone would notice, and remember. The hostess went last because the whole procession was, in a sense, hers. She was sending her guests ahead of her, into her own dining room, and following behind, to see them all safely in.

Imagine you're walking down the stairs in that procession, perhaps on the arm of Mr. Dunstan, the young MP, who's telling you about a speech he made last week. The staircase is lit by gas, and the light glows on the silk of the dresses ahead of you, the claret, the grey, the white, the blue, and on the black backs of the men's coats, and on the bare shoulders of the women, and on the jewels. Your skirt brushes the banisters on one side and the wall on the other. You can hear the soft swish of silk on the stair carpet, and Sir George's stick, tap, tap, ahead of you. And then, at the foot of the stairs, the dining room doors are open, and the light pouring out of them is warm and golden, and you step in.

The dining room has been transformed. The long mahogany table, extended to its full length, is covered with a white damask cloth so heavily starched that it gleams. Down the middle runs a line of tall silver and glass stands, piled with fruit, grapes and peaches and early strawberries, and between them, low arrangements of flowers, roses and ferns, from the florist in Covent Garden. There are candles in tall silver candlesticks, and the gas has been turned low, so the room glows. At each place there's a row of glasses, for sherry, for hock, for champagne, for claret, and a little regiment of silver knives and forks and spoons, and a folded napkin, and a small card with a name on it in Caroline's hand. And behind the chairs, standing against the walls, in their best livery, are Mr. Pryor, James, Thomas and the two hired waiters, waiting.

Everyone sits. Edward at the head, with Lady Ormsby on his right. Caroline at the foot, with Lord Ormsby on hers. And the dinner begins.

Now, there's a quiet revolution happening on this table, and I want you to notice it. In the older English style of dining, called service à la française, the French style, all the dishes of each course were placed on the table at once, in a careful pattern, and the guests helped themselves and each other, and the host carved the joint at the table. It was splendid, but the food got cold, and the table was crowded with dishes. In the newer style, called service à la russe, the Russian style, the table was decorated with fruit and flowers instead, and the food was carved at the sideboard and handed round to each guest, dish by dish, by the servants. It came into fashion in England in the middle decades of the century, and by the 1860s, fashionable hostesses were adopting it. Cookery books of these years, Mrs. Beeton's among them, give menus for dinners in this new Russian way. Caroline has adopted it this Season, for the first time, and she's slightly nervous about it, and very proud.

So the dishes come. A clear soup, and a thick one. The salmon, pink and perfect, with cucumber and a lobster sauce. Two entrées, small and elegant, little cutlets and a vol-au-vent. Then the saddle of lamb, carved by Mr. Pryor at the sideboard, with mint sauce, and new potatoes, and the asparagus, which was got at a sensible price after all. Then the ducklings, with green peas. Then the sweets: jellies trembling in their moulds, a cream, a tart. Then an ice. And at the end, the dessert, the fruit from the centre of the table, with small dishes of sweetmeats.

Let's set this beside a modern dinner party. Perhaps, when you have friends round, you cook one main dish, or two, and a pudding, and put it all on the table and let people help themselves. Perhaps you order food in. Perhaps the conversation runs late round the kitchen table. Caroline's dinner, by contrast, involves perhaps fifteen people working for twelve hours, and something like a dozen different dishes, and it will last nearly two hours. It's not really a meal. It's a performance.

And it's also, in its way, a piece of work. Every word of the conversation is being steered. Caroline, at her end of the table, keeps an eye on everything. She notices that Mrs. Calthorpe is stuck with Mr. Dunstan's speech, and draws her into the talk about the Royal Academy. She notices that Sir George's foot is paining him, and has a footstool brought, discreetly. She notices that Mr. Fenwick and Emily have been talking about poetry for twenty minutes, and decides to let them go on. She notices that a hired waiter has served the claret on the wrong side, and that Mr. Pryor has noticed too, and will deal with it. She keeps Lord Ormsby amused, which isn't easy. She does all of this while eating, very lightly, and laughing at the right moments, and never once looking anxious.

And here is where historians disagree, about what these dinners meant for women like Caroline.

For a long time, the political and social history of Victorian Britain was written almost entirely as the history of men: the men in Parliament, the men in the clubs, the men who made the speeches and signed the treaties. The women at the dinner table were treated as decoration, if they were mentioned at all. But in 1986, the historian Pat Jalland published a book called Women, Marriage and Politics, 1860 to 1914, based on the private papers of political families. And she showed that the wives and daughters of politicians played a real part in political life: as hostesses, as advisers, as letter-writers and go-betweens, as managers of their husbands' careers. Dinners like this one were where alliances were made, and introductions given, and favours asked. Other historians have been more cautious, pointing out that this influence was informal, indirect, and depended entirely on the men involved, and that it was no substitute for a vote or a voice of one's own. Both views tell us something. Caroline has no vote, and will never have one. But Mr. Dunstan, the young MP, will leave this house tonight with an introduction to Lord Ormsby that Caroline arranged, and he knows it.

Halfway through the lamb, the talk at Edward's end of the table turns, as it does at every dinner table in London this month, to the Derby. It will be run at Epsom in two weeks' time, on Wednesday the thirty-first of May, and the whole of London will empty out onto the Downs to see it. Parliament itself used to take the day off for it. And this year, there's a particular reason for excitement. One of the favourites is a French horse, a big, powerful colt called Gladiateur, bred in France and owned by a French count. No horse bred outside Britain has ever won the Derby. Lord Ormsby says it's impossible. Mr. Dunstan says he's seen the horse gallop, and it's not impossible at all. Sir George says he's put a sovereign on him, which shocks Mrs. Fenwick.

I can tell you, as nobody at this table can know yet, how it ends. On the thirty-first of May, Gladiateur will win, the first foreign-bred horse ever to win the Derby, and in France they'll call him the Avenger of Waterloo. Charlie, upstairs, asleep, with his tin Duke of Wellington on the nursery hearthrug, would be outraged.

At Caroline's end of the table, the talk is gentler. Lord Ormsby, who has been rather heavy going through the fish, turns out to love roses, and to grow them, and to have opinions about them, and Caroline, who knows exactly nothing about roses, asks him one question after another, and listens to every answer as if it were the most interesting thing she has heard all Season. By the ducklings, he's quite pink with pleasure. That, too, is part of the work. A hostess who could find the one subject that would make a dull man shine was worth more, in this world, than one who could sing.

The dessert has been served. The fruit has been taken. The wine has gone round, a glass or two of it, sherry and claret, and Edward is looking pleased with his claret, and Sir George is telling a story about a barrister who once fell asleep in court.

And Caroline, at the foot of the table, catches Lady Ormsby's eye.

It's the smallest of looks. A slight lift of the eyebrows. And Lady Ormsby, who has done this a thousand times, lays down her napkin. And Caroline rises. And at once, all down the table, every woman rises too, with a great soft rustle of silk, and every man rises with them. And Caroline leads the ladies out of the room, Lady Ormsby first, then the others in order, Emily last, with one glance back over her shoulder that she shouldn't have made. The gentlemen stand, until the last lady is gone and the door has closed behind her. And then they sit down again, and draw their chairs closer together, at Edward's end of the table, and the decanters begin to go round.

So here, at last, is the third of our three mysteries. Why, at the very height of her own dinner party, would the hostess suddenly rise from her chair and lead every woman out of the room?

Because this was one of the fixed customs of the English dinner, and it was the hostess's own particular duty to set it in motion. Mrs. Beeton described it exactly, in the instructions for dinner in her book of 1861. When the fruit has been taken, she wrote, and a glass or two of wine passed round, "the time will have arrived when the hostess will rise, and thus give the signal for the ladies to leave the gentlemen." Only the hostess could give that signal. Nobody else could end the dinner. And the moment she rose, the dinner was over for the women, and they withdrew upstairs to the drawing room, while the men stayed behind with the port and the claret, for half an hour or more, to talk of politics and business and the law, and to tell each other the kind of stories that were not told in front of ladies. Then the men would come upstairs, and join the ladies, and the evening would go on.

That's why the room upstairs is called a drawing room at all. It's short for a withdrawing room, the room to which the ladies withdrew.

Some of the ladies, it must be said, were glad of the half hour. Caroline is one of them.

## Part Eleven — Around Half Past Nine: Coffee, Music and the Carriages

Here is the small question for this part of the evening. At the end of a dinner party like this one, there's no clock on the wall telling the guests that it's time to go, and no host yawning by the door. So how does everyone know when the evening is over?

Let's go up with the ladies, and find out.

The drawing room is softly lit now. The gas has been turned up in the tall glass globes on either side of the chimneypiece, and there are candles on the piano, and the fire has been made up, so that the whole double room glows warm and golden, and the gilt of the looking-glass and the picture frames shines in the light. The curtains have been drawn across the tall windows. From the street below, very faintly, comes the sound of a carriage going by, and then another. On the chimneypiece, the gilt clock under its glass dome ticks quietly to itself.

The ladies settle themselves, in a great soft spreading of silk. Lady Ormsby on the sofa by the fire, as the guest of honour. Lady Halford beside her. Mrs. Fenwick in a low chair, with her amethysts glinting. Mrs. Calthorpe by the lamp. Emily on a footstool at her mother's feet. And Caroline, in her claret silk, moving gently among them all, making sure that each one has a cushion and a footstool and a cup of coffee, which James has brought up on a silver tray.

The talk is easier now. Without the gentlemen, the ladies loosen, a little. They talk about children, and illnesses, and servants, and the dressmaker in Regent Street, who is wonderful and impossibly slow. They talk about the wedding at St George's. Lady Halford makes everybody laugh, with a story about George and his gout. And Mrs. Fenwick, very quietly, takes the chair beside Caroline's and tells her, without saying anything directly at all, that her son speaks very highly of Miss Ashworth. Caroline says, without saying anything directly either, that Miss Ashworth has said nothing whatever on the subject, which is a very good sign. The two women smile at each other. It's a conversation that will be continued, in its own careful way, over many weeks.

Imagine you're sitting quietly in the corner of the room, in a little chair by the piano. It's warm in here, and peaceful, and the voices of the women are soft and low, rising and falling, like the sound of a stream. Someone laughs. A cup is set down on its saucer with a tiny chime. The fire crackles. And the clock on the chimneypiece goes on ticking, steady and unhurried, under its glass dome.

Upstairs, the house is beginning to go to sleep.

Florence has been put to bed at last. She sat on the top stair until the last guest had gone in to dinner, and then was carried off by Miss Lacy, protesting, and was asleep, Miss Lacy reports, within five minutes of her head touching the pillow. Charlie has been asleep for more than two hours, deep and warm, with his rabbit. Nurse Webb has finished her darning, and folded it, and gone to her own bed in the night nursery, leaving the door ajar so that she'll hear him if he wakes. And Miss Lacy, having seen Florence settled, has gone to her own small room on the second floor, and is sitting by her candle, writing a letter to her mother in Norfolk, and will be asleep herself before eleven.

And down in the basement, the great machine of the dinner is slowing at last. The kitchen is still hot, but the range has been allowed to sink, and Mrs. Bell has sat down, for the first time since four o'clock, in the wooden armchair by the fire, with her feet on the fender and a cup of strong tea in her hand. She's not finished. There's the servants' supper still to see to, and the stock for tomorrow to set on the back of the range. But the worst is over, and the salmon was perfect, and Mr. Pryor came down especially to tell her so. In the scullery, Annie and the scullery maid are washing the plates and the glasses and the silver, a mountain of it, in big tubs of hot water, with soda, and drying it, piece by piece, with soft cloths, and stacking it on the long wooden racks. It will take them until nearly midnight. Annie's hands are red to the wrist. But she's humming to herself, a tune she heard Miss Emily sing through the floor above, and she doesn't mind very much.

After about half an hour, there are voices on the stairs, and the gentlemen come up.

They come in a little flushed, smelling faintly of cigar smoke and port, in a cheerful body, still finishing the conversation they were having downstairs, about the Derby, and the French horse. Edward comes in last, looking pleased. Sir George makes straight for the sofa beside his wife, and puts his foot up on a stool, with a sigh. Mr. Dunstan makes straight for Lord Ormsby. And Mr. Arthur Fenwick, with no hurry at all, but no hesitation either, makes his way across the room to the footstool where Emily is sitting, and asks her whether she will play.

Emily looks at her mother. Caroline nods.

And so Emily goes to the piano. She sits, and arranges her white tulle skirts, and lays her music on the stand. And Mr. Fenwick stands at her shoulder, to turn the pages.

She plays first a piece by Mendelssohn, one of his Songs Without Words, very sweetly, and only a little too fast. And then, after a little persuading, she sings. A ballad, in English, simple and old-fashioned, about a girl and a garden and a summer long ago. Her voice isn't strong, but it's true, and clear, and the drawing room goes quiet to listen. Even Lord Ormsby stops talking about roses.

That's another thing a young lady in this world was expected to be able to do. To play the piano, and sing, well enough to give pleasure in a drawing room after dinner. Not as a performer. Never as a performer. That would have been almost improper. But as an accomplishment, a part of her education that she could offer her family and their guests, as Emily is doing now. Thousands upon thousands of girls in houses like this one learned to play exactly so, with patient governesses, in schoolrooms with slightly out-of-tune pianos, practising scales, like Florence this morning, up and down, up and down.

Let's set this beside your own evenings. If you want music tonight, you can have almost any piece ever recorded, played by the finest musicians in the world, through a small speaker or a pair of earphones, in a moment, at the touch of a screen. In 1865, there were no recordings at all. Not one. The phonograph would not be invented for another twelve years. If you wanted music in your home, someone in the room had to make it. A sister at the piano. A friend who sang. That's all there was. And perhaps that's why, when Emily finishes her song, the applause in this drawing room is so warm.

Mr. Fenwick turns the last page, a little too late, and Emily laughs, and he laughs, and then they both remember where they are, and stop.

And now, at about ten o'clock, tea comes up. Not the afternoon tea of five o'clock, but the tea that, in many English houses, was served in the drawing room in the later part of a dinner party, from a great silver urn, with a little bread and butter or a few biscuits, as a gentle sign that the evening was beginning, very slowly, to draw towards its end. Caroline pours it, as she did at five, with her own hands.

And here is a quiet place where historians disagree, about a woman who isn't in this room, but whose name has come up twice tonight, once at dinner and once over the coffee.

Mr. Dunstan, the young MP, has been telling Lord Ormsby about Mr. John Stuart Mill, and his campaign in Westminster, and his views about votes for women, and Lord Ormsby has been saying, at length, what nonsense it all is. And Lady Halford, overhearing, has said, rather tartly, that the country seems to have managed perfectly well with a woman at the head of it for very nearly twenty-eight years. Everyone laughs. But it's an interesting point, and historians have thought about it a good deal.

Queen Victoria herself, as it happens, was no friend to the cause. Five years after this evening, in a letter written on the twenty-ninth of May 1870, to her friend Theodore Martin, she would call the campaign for women's rights a "mad, wicked folly," in a phrase that the printed versions of her letter all agree on, though they differ a little in the words around it. And yet, in her book about the Queen and the women's movement, the historian Arianne Chernock has argued that Victoria's reign mattered to the women who campaigned for the vote all the same, whatever the Queen herself thought. Simply by being a woman on the throne, by doing a sovereign's work, reading state papers, meeting ministers, ruling an empire, she gave those campaigners an example they could point to again and again. Other historians would say the Queen's own conservatism did the cause no good at all. Lady Halford, I think, would side with Chernock. And so, I suspect, would Caroline.

The talk drifts on, to gentler things. The weather. The roses. Whether the Ormsbys will go down to the country at the end of July, when the Season ends. Whether Lady Halford will take the waters at Bath for George's foot. And then, at about half past ten, comes the answer to our small question.

Lady Ormsby, on the sofa by the fire, sets down her teacup, and looks at her husband. And he, with a small sigh, rises.

That's how everyone knows the evening is over. In a dinner party of this kind, the guest of highest rank was expected to be the first to leave, and the others did not, as a rule, leave before them. So when Lady Ormsby rose, everyone in the room understood. The evening had reached its natural end. Nobody needed a clock. And, in the hall below, the carriages had already been ordered to come back at about this time, so that they were waiting in the street.

James goes down the stairs, and out of the front door, and calls into the soft dark street, "Lord Ormsby's carriage!" And somewhere a little way along Eaton Place, a coachman who has been waiting on his box, with a rug over his knees, gathers up the reins, and the carriage comes clattering up to the door, its lamps glowing.

Outside, in the soft May dark, the street is quiet and still. The gas lamps along the pavement, lit at dusk by the lamplighter with his long pole, burn in their glass lanterns at regular intervals down Eaton Place, each one making a little pool of yellow light on the stones. Above the rooftops the sky isn't quite black, even now, because the sun set less than four hours ago and this is May. There's still a faint, deep blue in the north-west. The coachmen of the guests have been waiting for an hour or more, some in the street and some round the corner in the mews, talking quietly together, and smoking their pipes, and feeding their horses from nosebags, and stamping their feet a little. It's a part of the evening nobody upstairs ever sees. For every dinner in Belgravia tonight, there's a little gathering of patient men and horses in the street outside, waiting for a name to be called.

One by one, they go. Lord and Lady Ormsby first, with many thanks. Then the Calthorpes. Then Mr. Dunstan, on foot, to his club, very pleased with his evening and his introduction. Then Sir George and Lady Halford, with Sir George leaning on James's arm on the front steps, and Lady Halford turning back at the door to squeeze Caroline's hand and whisper, "Perfect, my dear. Absolutely perfect." And last of all, Mrs. Fenwick, and her son, who bows over Emily's hand in the hall, and says he hopes he may have the honour of calling, and is told by Caroline, very graciously, that they're at home on Thursdays.

The front door closes. Mr. Pryor slides the bolts.

In the hall, under the gas, the silver card tray sits on its marble table, as it has all day. It's empty now. But on Thursday afternoon, Caroline thinks, it may hold a card she has been rather hoping to see.

It's a quarter past eleven. The guests have gone. And the house, very slowly, begins to go to bed.

## Part Twelve — Around a Quarter Past Eleven: Half an Hour of Her Own

The last small question of our day is the simplest. In a house of seventeen people, all of whom have worked hard since morning, who will be the very last one awake tonight?

Let's watch them go, one by one, and see.

Emily goes first, of the family who are still up. She kisses her father good night in the drawing room, and he holds her at arm's length for a moment and looks at her, and says, gruffly, that she sang very nicely. Then she kisses her mother, and Caroline holds her a moment longer than usual, and says nothing at all, and Emily says nothing either. She goes upstairs, slowly, trailing her white tulle, a rosebud coming loose from her hair. Sarah is waiting in her room to undo the hooks and the laces and take down her hair. Within half an hour, Emily is in bed, in the dark, lying on her back, looking at the ceiling, and smiling. She thinks she won't sleep at all tonight. She's asleep by midnight.

Down in the basement, the work is ending too. The last of the silver has been dried and counted, every fork and spoon, and carried into the butler's pantry, where Mr. Pryor locks it away in the iron safe for the night. The hired waiters have been paid and have gone off into the dark, towards the King's Road, with their coats over their arms. Annie and the scullery maid hang up their wet aprons and their cloths to dry by the range. The scullery maid goes home, to her mother's rooms in Pimlico, not far from Mrs. Doyle's court. And Annie, so tired that she's almost walking in her sleep, climbs the back stairs, all five flights, from the basement to the attic, with a stub of candle in a tin holder, to the little room under the roof that she shares with Ellen.

Imagine you're climbing behind her. The back stairs are dark and narrow, and they creak. Your candle throws a small, wobbling circle of light on the bare wooden steps and the whitewashed wall. Above you, Annie's shadow is huge on the wall, and then small, and then huge again. At the top, there's a short passage under the slope of the roof, with three plain doors. Annie opens one. The room is small, with a sloping ceiling, a skylight, two narrow iron beds, a washstand with a jug and basin, a chair, a row of pegs for their dresses, and a little square of carpet. It's warm up here, after the day's sun on the slates. Ellen is already asleep in one bed, her cap still on. Annie unpins her own cap, and unlaces her boots, and takes off her print dress, and says her prayers, kneeling by the bed, very fast, and climbs in. She's asleep before you can blow out the candle.

That's Annie. Fifteen years old. Up before six. In bed at a quarter to twelve. And tomorrow she'll do it all again.

Then the others. Mrs. Bell, slow on the stairs, her knees aching, to a room of her own on the attic floor, which is one of the privileges of a cook. Mrs. Hodges, with her keys, after a last walk through the store-room to see that it's locked. James and Thomas, who in many houses like this slept down in the basement, near the pantry and the plate, to guard it, and who tonight are asleep within minutes of lying down, their livery coats hung up, their stockings rolled. Sarah, after Emily is settled, up to the attic. Polly, the nursemaid, in the little room next to the night nursery.

And Mr. Pryor, last of the servants, makes his rounds.

He does it every night, in the same order. The area door, bolted and barred. The kitchen door. The windows on the ground floor, every one of them, the shutters closed and the iron bars dropped into their sockets. The front door, the bolts at top and bottom, the chain. Then he goes through the ground floor rooms, the dining room, the library, the morning room, with his candle, turning down the gas in each one until the little blue flame dies. He comes at last to the hall. He looks at the silver card tray on its marble table, as he did at six o'clock this morning, and straightens it by half an inch. He turns the hall gas down, but not quite out, leaving the faintest blue glimmer, because the master and mistress are still up. And then he goes down to his own room by the pantry, and his day, which began at six, is over.

Edward goes up next. He and Caroline have sat together for a quarter of an hour by the dying fire in the drawing room, talking quietly about the evening. Edward says young Fenwick is a good fellow, and a sound lawyer, and has no money to speak of yet, but will have, in time. Caroline says yes. Edward says, a little helplessly, that he supposes there's nothing to be done about it, and that she'll arrange it all, as she arranges everything. Caroline says that there's nothing to arrange, nothing at all, and that he's to go to bed. He kisses her, and goes. In ten minutes he's asleep, and snoring gently, in his dressing room across the landing.

Then Martin.

Martin has been waiting up, as a lady's maid always did. She has been sitting in her mistress's bedroom since eleven, by the fire, mending a torn flounce. When Caroline comes in, Martin rises, and begins, without a word, to undo the day.

The gloves first, unbuttoned at the wrist, and drawn off, finger by finger. The fan and the handkerchief, laid on the dressing table. Then the diamonds, unclasped at the back of her neck, and laid back in their red leather case, on the white velvet, and the case locked, and the key given to Caroline. Then the two dozen tiny hooks of the bodice, one by one, from the top down. Then the great claret skirt, lifted away by Martin, alone this time, carefully, carefully, and laid over a chair. The crinoline, unhooked and stepped out of, and hung in the wardrobe like a great empty birdcage. The petticoats. The corset, unlaced at last, so that Caroline takes a long, deep breath, the first of its kind since half past seven. A nightgown of fine white cotton, with a frill at the neck. And her hair, unpinned, the net and the jet ornament taken out, the coils unwound, brushed out long and loose down her back, a hundred strokes again, as it was this morning.

"Thank you, Martin. That will do. Go to bed."

"Good night, ma'am."

And Martin goes, up the stairs, to her own small room, and she's asleep, perhaps, a quarter of an hour later.

And that's the answer to our last small question. Of everyone in this house of seventeen people, the last one awake tonight is the lady of the house herself. Caroline.

She doesn't go to bed. Not yet.

She sits down at the little writing table under her bedroom window, which has been waiting there all day, with its blotter squared and its inkstand filled, and she lights a candle on it, and she opens her account book, and writes in the expenses of the day in her neat, upright hand. The salmon. The asparagus. The hired waiters. The florist. Then, on a fresh line, she writes: Charlie's boots, too small. And: Ask Mr. Hall about the Doyle boy. Then she closes the book.

Then she takes out the letter to her sister in Hampshire, the long, warm, first-person letter she began at half past ten this morning, and finishes it. She writes about the dinner. She writes about the salmon, and Lord Ormsby's roses, and George Halford's foot. And then, very carefully, in a way that only a sister would understand, she writes a little about Mr. Arthur Fenwick. She signs it, "Your loving C." She blots it, and folds it, and seals it, and addresses it, and presses a red penny stamp in the corner.

And then she sits, for a while, doing nothing at all.

The house is completely silent. Beyond the window, Eaton Place is empty, its gas lamps burning in their little pools of light along the pavement. Far away, somewhere towards Victoria, a single cab goes by, and the sound of its wheels fades. And Caroline sits by her candle, with her hands in her lap, and her hair loose on her shoulders, and for the first time today, nobody needs her. Nobody is knocking. Nobody is waiting for an answer. No bell is ringing.

Do you remember what Florence Nightingale wrote, in her essay Cassandra, the line I promised we'd come back to at the very end of the day? That women never have half an hour in all their lives, except before or after anybody is up in the house, that they can call their own.

Before or after anybody is up in the house. This is that half hour. Caroline has found it, as so many women of her world must have found it, at the very end of the day, when everyone else is asleep. Scholars still gently disagree about Cassandra, about exactly when it was written, whether in 1850, or 1852, or later, and about whether it describes the common lot of women like Caroline or the special anguish of one extraordinary woman who wanted far more than her world would give her. Perhaps it's both. But I think Caroline would recognise this half hour, if you described it to her. I think she'd know exactly what it was worth.

Let's set this beside your own life, one last time. Perhaps, at the end of your own day, when everyone else is asleep, you find a little time that's yours. A book. A cup of something warm. A few minutes looking at the dark outside the window. Perhaps you spend it with a phone in your hand, its light on your face, the whole world still talking to you. Caroline has no phone. Her world has gone quiet. And in that quiet, I think, she's resting in a way we sometimes forget how to.

At last she rises, and takes her letter, and takes her candle, and opens her bedroom door.

Imagine you're following her now, very quietly, down the wide front staircase, in the dark. She's in her nightgown and a soft woollen wrapper, with her hair down her back, and her bare feet in slippers. Her candle makes a small golden circle around her, on the stairs, on the banister rail, on the pictures on the wall, and the dark closes in again behind. You pass the drawing room door, closed now, with the clock ticking softly inside. You go on down. The house creaks gently around you, settling as old houses do at night, though this one isn't old at all.

And then you're in the hall.

It's very still. The faintest blue glimmer of the hall gas, turned low by Mr. Pryor, and Caroline's candle. The black and white marble of the floor. The tall front door, bolted and chained. The umbrella stand. The hall chairs. And there, on its marble-topped table, just inside the door, where it has been all day long, the silver card tray.

It's empty. All the cards of the afternoon have been taken up and sorted and put away in the drawer of the hall table, to be returned, or answered, or simply kept. The tray is clean and bare, and shining softly in the candlelight, its fluted rim gleaming, its little band of engraved leaves catching the flame. In the morning, Mr. Pryor will polish it again. And all day tomorrow, as on every day of the Season, the small white cards and letters of London will drift across it, one by one, like leaves.

Caroline lays her letter to her sister beside the tray, on the marble, where James will find it at seven and carry it to the post.

She stands there for a moment longer, looking at the tray, at the light on the silver. Then she turns, and goes back up the stairs, slowly, the small golden circle of her candle climbing with her, until it disappears round the turn of the landing.

In her room, she sets the candle down beside the bed, and slips under the cool white counterpane, and lies back. Outside, beyond the curtains, if she could see it, a waning moon, a little more than half full, is just beginning to rise over the rooftops of London, a few minutes after midnight. I worked that out, by the way, with a modern astronomical program, because nobody wrote down the moon over Eaton Place on this particular night. It will lay a pale, quiet light on the cream stucco and the empty street, and on all the sleeping houses.

Caroline leans over, and blows out the candle.

And the whole house is asleep.

Thank you for spending this long day with me, and with Caroline and Edward Ashworth, and Emily and Florence and little Charlie, and with Mr. Pryor and Mrs. Bell and Martin and Annie and all the rest of the household, in their tall cream house in Eaton Place. If this story helped you rest, or helped you drift off somewhere along the way, I'm truly glad. And if you'd like to come back for more days and nights like this one, you might consider liking this video and subscribing to the channel, gently, whenever you're ready. There's no hurry at all.

Next time, we'll go back to this same kind of house, but we'll go in by the area steps instead of the front door. We'll spend a whole day, from five in the morning until long after midnight, with a young Victorian housemaid, and we'll find out what it was really like to be the person who lit the fires, carried the water and polished the silver, and who was awake long before the lady of the house, and climbed the attic stairs at night with every bone aching. I hope you'll join me there.

Until then, sleep well. And may there be, at the end of your own day, a little half hour that is all your own.
