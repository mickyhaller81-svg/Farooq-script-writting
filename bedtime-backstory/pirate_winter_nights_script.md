## Part One — Around Three O'Clock in the Afternoon: The Last Blue Light

It is January, in the year 1720, off the coast of New England, and a small sloop is creeping into the shelter of a spruce-dark island as the afternoon fades. The wind comes off the land like a blade. It smells of snow and pine and salt. Spray freezes where it lands, in thin white crusts along the rail. And below decks, in the dim hold where the crew will sleep tonight, a tin lantern with windows of thin horn hangs from a hook on a beam, cold and dark, swaying gently, waiting for its candle.

Not the pirates of the picture books, with their parrots and their buried maps and their planks. Just one ordinary crew, on one ordinary winter night, under one small wooden deck.

And without a few old tricks, you would not have lasted until morning, not in a wet wool shirt, in an open hold, on a January sea.

So tonight I would like you to carry three small questions with you. The first is this. Why did pirates, of all people, vote on nearly everything they did? The second. Why was every light on board ordered out at eight o'clock at night, in the dark of a northern winter? And the third. How did men sleep, warm enough to wake again, in wet clothes, at sea, in January?

These are not three separate questions. They are three hatches into the same small hold, on the same long night, and we will lift each of them in its own time.

So let your head grow heavy now. Let your shoulders loosen and drop. Your eyes may close whenever they like, and the lantern will be lit without you. Let us go back, gently, to a gray winter afternoon, and to a little ship coming in from the cold. So let us begin.

The sloop is called the Delight. She is not large. From the tip of her long bowsprit to her square stern she measures perhaps sixty feet, and she has a single tall mast, raked a little backward, with one great sail behind it and smaller sails before it. She was built in Bermuda, of red cedar, which grows on that island and which does not easily rot. She was built to be fast. And she was, until last October, a trading sloop called the Mary and Sarah, carrying salt and sugar and timber between the islands.

Then she met the men who now sail her.

They took her near the Bahamas on a bright October morning, and not a shot was fired. Her master and his four hands were put aboard a passing ship bound for Charles Town, unharmed and rather relieved, and the sloop was given a new name by vote. Delight. It was the youngest man aboard who suggested it, and everyone laughed, and then everyone raised a hand.

Now it is January, and the Delight is a very long way from the Bahamas.

She is creeping, under shortened sail, into the lee of a small island in the outer reaches of Casco Bay, on the coast of a country called the District of Maine. In 1720, Maine was not a separate colony at all. It was part of the Province of Massachusetts Bay, governed from Boston, and along much of this coast, after long years of war, the settlements were few and far apart. There is a farmhouse on a point to the north, three miles off, with a thin line of smoke above it. There is nothing else. Only dark spruce, and gray granite ledges, and snow lying in the hollows of the rock, and the sea turning from gray to slate as the sky behind the island begins to fade.

So why is a pirate sloop here at all, in the coldest month of a New England winter?

Most pirates of these years did not choose the cold. The great pirate cruising grounds of the time were warm ones: the Bahamas, the Caribbean islands, the coasts of Carolina and Virginia, and the long hot shore of West Africa. When pirate ships did come north, they usually came in summer, to the fishing harbors of Newfoundland and Nova Scotia, where they could find fresh food, spare sails, and fishermen who might be talked or forced into joining them.

The Delight is here by bad luck and a broken mast.

In the middle of December, off the coast of Carolina, she was caught in a long winter gale that blew for three days from the southwest and carried her far out to sea, and far to the north. Her topmast cracked. Her mainsail tore along a seam. Her water casks ran low. And when the gale finally blew itself out, the man who knew these northern waters best stood at the rail and looked at the color of the sea, and said, quietly, that he knew a place.

That man is the quartermaster, and his name is Nathaniel Cobb. He is forty-one years old, broad and slow-spoken, with a gray-flecked beard and hands like oak roots. He was born in Marblehead, Massachusetts, the fishing town north of Boston, and he fished these cold banks for twenty years before he went south. He knows which coves have fresh water, and which islands have dry wood, and which farmers on lonely points will sell a barrel of meal to strangers and ask no questions.

So the crew voted, and the Delight came north, to mend her mast, and fill her casks, and wait for a fair wind south again.

There are nine of them aboard.

There is the captain, John Darby, who is thirty-four. He was born in Bristol, in the west of England, and he was once the mate of a merchant ship in the Jamaica trade, until a hard master and a long voyage made him think again about the shape of his life. He is lean and quiet, and he reads, which is not common. He keeps the ship's log in a neat, slanting hand, and he has never once raised his voice to a man aboard.

There is the quartermaster, Nathaniel Cobb, whom you have met.

There is the boatswain, Matthew Searle, who is forty-five, from Bideford in Devon. He has charge of the rigging, the anchors, and the cables, and he has the loudest voice on the ship, which he almost never uses.

There is the gunner, Daniel Royce, who is thirty-eight, from Wapping, on the river below London. The Delight carries only four small cannon, and Daniel looks after them, and after the powder, as if they were his children.

There is the cook, Henry Ash, who is fifty-two, the oldest man aboard. He has a stiff left knee from an old fall in an old war, and he walks with a roll even on dry land.

There is James Gold, who is thirty, a free seaman born in Barbados, the best helmsman on the ship, with the sharpest eyes.

There is the carpenter, Thomas Wills, who is twenty-seven, from Plymouth, with sawdust always somewhere in his hair.

There is the fiddler, Owen Price, who is nineteen, from Pembrokeshire, in the far west of Wales. He is the youngest man who has signed the ship's articles.

And there is Benjamin Gale, who is twenty-two. He is a fisherman from Salem, Massachusetts. In October, far to the south, he was taken off a Salem ketch that was carrying dried cod to the islands, because he could hand and reef and steer, and the crew of the Delight was short of hands. He has never signed the articles. He is not quite a prisoner and not quite a pirate. And tonight, very near his home, he is hoping for something he has not dared to say out loud.

This crew never quite existed under one name, but every detail of their night is real.

Here is something that surprises most people. All the pirates of these years, sailing at once, would not have filled a single small town. Historians who have counted ships, crews and reports believe that in the busiest years, around 1716 to 1718, there were perhaps eighteen hundred to twenty-four hundred pirates at sea at any one time, across the whole Atlantic. By the early 1720s, the number was falling fast. The whole famous age of pirates, the age of the stories, lasted only about ten years, from around 1716 to around 1726.

Where did they come from? Many of them had been privateers in the long war that ended in 1713, sailors licensed by their governments to capture enemy ships. When the war ended, thousands of seamen were suddenly out of work, in ports full of hungry men and cheap ships. Some found honest berths. Some did not. And a royal proclamation of September 1717, which offered a pardon to any pirate who surrendered, brought many of them in, while others took the pardon and went back out again.

Almost everything most people know about these men comes, in the end, from one book. It was published in London in 1724, and it was called A General History of the Robberies and Murders of the most notorious Pyrates. Its author signed himself Captain Charles Johnson. It told the lives of famous pirate captains, often in vivid detail, and it printed, word for word, some of the written rules that pirate crews agreed to live by. Those rules will keep us company tonight. One of them, from the crew of a Welsh captain named Bartholomew Roberts, will answer one of our three questions. Captain Johnson's book was a bestseller, and it has been reprinted again and again ever since.

Historians do not agree on who Captain Charles Johnson really was. For much of the twentieth century, many scholars believed that he was Daniel Defoe, the author of Robinson Crusoe, writing under a false name. Others have argued, more recently, that the case for Defoe is weak, and that the book more likely came from the circle of a London printer and journalist named Nathaniel Mist, who had himself been a sailor. What both sides agree on is that the author knew a great deal, had read trial records and official reports, and sometimes improved a story when the truth was thin.

Now come up on deck with me, into the last of the light.

You step out of the low hatch into the wind, and it takes your breath. The deck under your feet is wet and gritty with frozen spray, and every rope you touch is stiff and cold as iron. Ahead of you, the bowsprit points at a dark line of spruce trees, black against a sky the color of a pigeon's wing. Snow lies white along the granite at the water's edge. The water itself is very still in the lee of the island, dark green and glassy, and when you look over the side, you can see thin plates of ice drifting past, turning slowly, clinking softly against the cedar hull.

You smell tar, and wet wool, and wood smoke from the cook's fire forward. Somewhere a gull calls once and is silent. And from the bow comes a long, rattling roar as the anchor goes down, the cable running out through the hawse hole, and then a deep, settling quiet as the Delight swings round on her cable and comes to rest.

Today, a sailor in these waters would know days ahead that a gale was coming, from a forecast on a screen in a warm wheelhouse. In 1720, the only forecast was the sky, and the feel of the wind on your cheek, and the memory of older men.

Matthew Searle stands at the bow, watching the cable, his breath smoking in the cold. "She holds," he calls at last, not loudly. "She has bitten well."

John Darby nods. He stands by the tiller with his hands in the pockets of a long blue coat that once belonged to the master of the Mary and Sarah, who left it behind in his hurry. "Then we will lie here tonight," he says. "Mr. Cobb, see to the water party at first light."

Nathaniel Cobb is already looking at the shore, the way a man looks at a face he knows. Somewhere along this coast, a few days' sail south, is the town where he was born. He has not been home in eleven years.

Forward, by the fire, Henry Ash is bent over a black iron pot, with his stiff leg out to one side. Owen Price is beside him, feeding the fire with broken pieces of an old cask, and blowing on his fingers between each piece. James Gold is coiling a rope in the waist of the ship, very neatly, the way he does everything. Thomas Wills is looking up at the cracked topmast, measuring it with his eye, and whistling softly between his teeth.

And Benjamin Gale stands alone at the rail, looking north, at that thin line of smoke above the farmhouse on the point.

The light is going quickly now. The island turns from green to black. The sky turns from gray to blue, and from blue to a deep, cold violet. Below decks, in the dim hold, the horn lantern swings softly on its hook, still unlit, waiting for the cook to bring it a flame.

## Part Two — Around Ten Minutes to Five: Sunset, and the Lantern Is Lit

The sun goes down behind the island at nine minutes before five o'clock, though nobody aboard the Delight sees it go. There is only a slow dimming behind the spruce trees, a last band of pale yellow low in the west, and then the cold blue dusk coming down over the water like a lid.

How do nine men on a small ship know what time it is, with no clock on board?

The answer hangs beside the tiller, in a little wooden frame. It is a sandglass, two bulbs of blown glass joined at the waist, with fine sand inside, and it takes exactly half an hour for the sand to run from the top bulb to the bottom. When it runs out, the man on watch turns it over, and strikes the ship's bell. One stroke after the first half hour. Two strokes after the second. And so on, until eight strokes, eight bells, mark the end of a four-hour watch, and the count begins again.

There is one more tool beside the glass, and it is a lovely old thing. It is a round board of wood, painted with the thirty-two points of the compass, with eight little holes drilled along each point, and small wooden pegs hanging from it on strings. It is called a traverse board. Every half hour, when the glass is turned, the helmsman pushes a peg into the hole for the course he has been steering. At the end of the watch, the mate can read the whole four hours at a glance, in pegs, and work out roughly where the ship has gone, even if the man at the helm could neither read nor write. Many could not. The board did not need them to.

So the crew of a ship lived, day and night, by watches, not hours. The day was cut into watches of four hours each, with two short dog watches in the early evening, so that the men would not stand the same hours every day. On a merchant ship or a warship, the crew was split into two halves, the starboard watch and the larboard watch, and they took turns, four hours on deck and four hours below, all through the voyage. A seaman rarely slept more than four hours at a time, for months on end.

Tonight, at anchor, in a quiet lee, the Delight does not need half her crew on deck. She needs only an anchor watch, two men at a time, to watch the cable, the wind, the ice, and the shore. The rest may go below, and eat, and sleep. And so, tonight, the bell will be struck every half hour by whoever is awake to strike it, soft and clear across the water, all through the dark.

There is one more small puzzle about the date. If you could look at the captain's log tonight, you might be surprised by what it says. In 1720, England and her colonies still used the old Julian calendar, which by now had fallen eleven days behind the calendar that most of Europe used. And the English counted the start of the new year not from the first of January, but from the twenty-fifth of March. So tonight, by our calendar, is Saturday the twentieth of January, 1720. But in John Darby's log, it is Saturday the ninth of January, and the year is still written as 1719. Careful clerks of the time often wrote it as both, with a little slash, 1719 over 1720.

Down below, the lantern is about to be lit.

It is Henry Ash's task, every evening at sunset, because the cook is the one man aboard who always has a fire. He takes a single tallow candle from the box the quartermaster keeps. He lights a long pine splinter at his cooking fire, and shields it with his hand. And he limps to the hatch, and goes carefully down the steep ladder into the hold, with the little flame cupped in his palm.

Now follow him down, and feel how the hold closes around you.

You come down the ladder backward, the rungs cold and slick under your hands, and the air changes at once. It is still cold, very cold, but the knife edge of the wind is gone. It is close and dark and still. You can smell it all at once, the smell of a ship's hold in winter: bilge water and tar, damp oak and wet wool, salt pork in its barrels, old rope, tobacco, and the sour ghost of rum. Somewhere close to your ear, the hull creaks softly as the ship swings on her cable.

Then the splinter flares, and you see the lantern.

It is a plain thing, made of tin, about the height of your forearm, with a round top pierced with holes for the smoke, and a little ring to hang it by. Its sides are not glass. They are horn, the horn of an ox, heated and split and scraped so thin that the light comes through it in a soft, warm, honey-colored glow. Henry opens its small door, sets the candle in its socket, touches the splinter to the wick, and shuts the door again, and the hold fills with a gentle amber light, not bright at all, but enough. Enough to see the curve of the hull, and the barrels lashed against it, and the low beams overhead, with the hammocks, still rolled, tied up against them.

Henry hangs the lantern back on its hook, beside the foot of the ladder, where anyone coming down can see it. He watches it for a moment. Then he nods to it, as if to an old friend, and climbs back up into the cold.

Here is the surprising thing. On a pirate ship, even this one small light was a matter of written law. We will come to why a little later tonight.

Not every pirate crew who came north to these cold waters was as lucky as the crew of the Delight.

In the spring of 1717, a pirate captain named Samuel Bellamy brought his ship, the Whydah, north along this very coast. She was a big, fast, three-masted ship, which had been built to carry captive Africans across the Atlantic, and which Bellamy's crew had taken in the Caribbean that February. By most accounts, she was heavy with the plunder of more than fifty ships. On the night of the twenty-sixth of April, 1717, a violent northeast gale, the kind New Englanders call a nor'easter, caught her off the outer shore of Cape Cod, and drove her onto a sandbar near Wellfleet in the dark. Of the men aboard her, only two came ashore alive. One was the ship's carpenter, a young man named Thomas Davis. The other was a young pilot named John Julian, a Miskito man from the coast of Central America.

For more than two hundred and sixty years, the wreck of the Whydah lay lost under the shifting sand. Then, in 1984, an underwater explorer named Barry Clifford found her. And in the autumn of 1985, his divers brought up her bell, crusted and green, and when the crust was cleaned away, there were words cast in the bronze around its rim: THE WHYDAH GALLY 1716. By most accounts, it was the first pirate shipwreck anywhere in the world to be named beyond doubt.

Historians do not agree on why Bellamy came north that spring. A much-loved Cape Cod legend says he was sailing back to a young woman he had met in Eastham, the year before, and that she watched for his sails from the cliffs. Most historians treat that story as a later invention, charming but without a single document behind it. They point instead to the plain needs of a pirate crew in spring: a quiet northern coast, out of the way of warships, where a fleet could meet, clean the weed from its hulls, mend its gear, and buy food from people who asked few questions. In that, at least, Bellamy and the crew of the Delight would have understood each other well.

Today, a captain in these waters would know his position to within a few feet, from a small screen glowing beside the wheel, and his cabin would be lit at the touch of a switch. Aboard the Delight, there is one candle in one horn lantern, and a captain who knows where he is because Nathaniel Cobb remembers the shape of an island.

Up on deck, the blue dusk deepens. The first stars are coming out over the open sea to the east, very sharp and white in the cold, clear air.

Daniel Royce, the gunner, is making his evening rounds of the four small cannon, two on each side. He runs his hand along each one, checks that the canvas covers are tight over the touchholes, and that the tompions, the wooden plugs in the muzzles, are pushed firmly home against the spray. He does this every evening, whether there is need or not. Then he goes forward to look at the little powder room, deep in the hold, behind a thick wooden bulkhead lined with felt, and comes back up, satisfied, rubbing his cold hands together.

Thomas Wills is still up at the mast, or rather, he is sitting astride the crosstrees, forty feet above the deck, with one arm round the cracked topmast, peering at the split in the last of the light. He comes down at last, sliding the final few feet on a backstay, and lands lightly on the deck, blowing on his hands. He tells the captain that the spar can be fished, which is a carpenter's word for splinting it, with two strips of oak and a long lashing of tarred rope, as a doctor would splint a broken arm. It will take him most of tomorrow, he says, and a good dry spruce pole from the island if he can find one. John Darby listens, and nods, and says that tomorrow is the Sabbath. Thomas grins, and says that the spar does not know it.

Owen Price, meanwhile, has crept down into the hold to warm his fingers near the lantern, which gives almost no heat at all, and he knows it, but it feels warmer there, and that is enough.

James Gold has taken the first anchor watch, with Matthew Searle. They stand together at the bow, wrapped in their coats, watching the cable where it runs down into the black water. Every so often, Matthew leans over and puts his bare hand on the cable, to feel whether the anchor is dragging. A dragging anchor trembles. A holding anchor is still.

"Still," he says, every time.

At the stern, by the tiller, Nathaniel Cobb is talking quietly with Benjamin Gale. They are the only two New Englanders aboard, and they have fallen, over the long weeks, into a kind of friendship, the older man and the younger. They do not talk about the sea. They talk about Marblehead and Salem, which are only a few miles apart, and about the people they both knew there, and about the taste of fresh cod fried in salt pork fat, and about whose mother made the better johnnycake.

"Your people will be at supper now," Nathaniel says.

Benjamin nods, and looks away, toward that one small farmhouse light on the point.

Stay up here a moment longer, in the dusk, and take a turn at the glass yourself.

You stand by the tiller, which is a long curved bar of oak, lashed amidships now that the ship is at anchor. The little sandglass hangs at the height of your eyes, in its frame, and in the failing light you have to lean close to see it. The last few grains are running down, very fast at the end, a thin bright thread of sand. And then the top bulb is empty. You take it down, turn it over, and hang it up again, and the thread begins to run once more. Then you reach for the short rope on the bell, and you give it one clean stroke, and then a second, because a full hour of the first dog watch has gone by, and the evening has truly begun.

The sound goes out across the still water, round and bright and lonely, and comes back to you, very faint, from the granite of the island. On the point to the north, the farmhouse window glows a dull orange. Your hands are already aching with the cold, so you push them deep under your arms, and stamp your feet on the frozen planks, and feel the ship move under you, very slightly, as she breathes on her cable. Above the mast, the sky is the deep blue of a mussel shell. You can see your breath. You can see the first star. And you can hear, from below, the low murmur of voices, and the clink of the cook's iron spoon against his pot, and know that supper is not far away.

"Tonight we will put it to the company," Nathaniel says, so quietly that only Benjamin hears. "Your going home. I will speak for it."

Benjamin does not answer. He cannot, quite. His throat has gone tight, and his eyes sting, and he tells himself that it is the wind. He only grips the cold rail a little harder, and keeps looking at the light.

Below them, through the open hatch, a soft amber glow rises up out of the hold, and the horn lantern burns steadily on its hook, one small warm light in all that wide dark bay.

## Part Three — Around Half Past Five: The Fire in the Sandbox

By half past five it is fully dark, and a smell is drifting aft along the deck that makes every man aboard lift his head. Salt pork, and onions, and pease, and something frying.

How do you keep a cooking fire burning on a ship made of wood, tar and rope, without setting her alight?

The answer is sand, and iron, and care. On a big warship of the time, the galley stove was a great iron or brick hearth, set on its own platform of bricks and lead. On a small sloop like the Delight, it is much simpler. Just forward of the mast, on the open deck, stands a squat box of bricks, about the size of a sea chest, with an iron grate inside it and a little iron hood above it to keep off the spray. And the whole box sits on a thick bed of sand, held in a shallow wooden frame, so that no coal or spark that falls out of the fire can ever touch the planks. Beside it, always, stands a leather bucket of seawater.

The fire is fed with whatever wood the ship can find. Tonight it is the staves of an old rum cask, broken up by Owen, and a little driftwood, and some split spruce that Nathaniel cut on a different island a week ago. It is a small fire, never a large one. And when supper is done, it will be put out, raked and drowned, and the sand stirred over it, every single night. Of all the dangers that a wooden ship carried with her across the sea, the one that many sailors feared most was not the storm, and not the enemy. It was fire.

And there is the matter of what a crew like this one eats, on a winter night, far from any market.

Most of what a ship carried was meant to keep, for months, in barrels. There was salt beef and salt pork, packed in brine, so hard and salty that it had to be soaked overnight in a cask of seawater, called a steep tub, before it could be cooked at all. There were dried peas, which became the thick, comforting porridge that sailors called pease. There was oatmeal, and cheese, and butter that grew rancid as the weeks went on. And there was the bread of the sea, ship's biscuit, baked hard and dry ashore, twice over, so that it would last a year in a cask. It was so hard that men soaked it in their soup, or broke it with the butt of a knife, and it was famously home to small black weevils, which sailors learned to knock out by tapping the biscuit sharply on the table before each bite.

Here is a number that may surprise you. In these years, a sailor in the Royal Navy was allowed, by the regulations of the time, a pound of biscuit every day, and a gallon of beer. A gallon, every day, for every man. The beer was weak, and it was drunk instead of water, because water stored for weeks in wooden casks went green and foul, while beer, at least for a while, stayed sweet.

Pirates had no navy rations, and no purser, and no regulations from London. They had whatever they had taken, and whatever they could buy, and whatever they could catch. That meant that for a pirate crew, life ran from feast to famine and back again. After a rich capture, they might have wine, fine cheese, live hens, sugar, chocolate and spices, more than they could eat. After a long cruise with no luck, they might have nothing but biscuit and water for weeks.

That was why the sharing of food mattered so much to them.

Captain Johnson's book of 1724 printed the written articles of the crew of Bartholomew Roberts, a Welshman who became, by many reckonings, the most successful pirate captain of these years. The very first article on Roberts' list says that every man has "a vote in affairs of moment." And it goes on, in the same breath, to give every man an equal right to any fresh food or strong drink the crew captured, to use as he pleased, unless supplies ran so short that the company had to vote, for the good of everyone, to cut the ration.

Think about what that meant. On an ordinary merchant ship, the captain and the officers ate the best of the food, in the cabin, and the men ate what was left, in the forecastle. On Roberts' ship, the captain had no more right to a fresh chicken than the youngest hand. Roberts' articles were written down a little after our night, in 1720 and 1721. But articles very like them were sworn on many pirate ships in these years, and the crew of the Delight swore to a set of their own last October, with their hands on a small black Bible that the master of the Mary and Sarah had left behind in his cabin. Not every crew had a Bible to swear on. Captain Johnson tells of one pirate crew, a few years later, who swore to their articles on a hatchet instead, for want of one.

Now come below, and have your supper with them.

You climb down the ladder into the hold, and the warmth of nine bodies and a pot of hot food meets you halfway, a thick, steamy warmth, smelling of pork fat and onion and wet wool. The horn lantern glows on its beam. Under it, the crew sit on sea chests and upturned buckets and coils of rope, in a rough ring, with their knees almost touching, and their wooden bowls in their laps. Their faces are red with cold and gold with lantern light.

Someone hands you a bowl. It is a wooden bowl, worn smooth, with a crack mended with a little strip of copper. Into it Henry Ash ladles a thick, yellow-green porridge of pease, with chunks of salt pork in it, and beside it, on the rim, he lays a piece of fried fish, white and flaking, crisp at the edges. Somebody passes you a biscuit, and you do as the others do, and tap it twice, sharply, on the edge of a barrel. Nothing falls out. You are lucky. Then you dip the biscuit into the hot pease, and wait until it softens, and bite.

It is salty and rich and very hot, and it is the best thing you have ever eaten. You feel the heat of it go down into your chest and spread outward, into your cold arms and your numb feet. The fish is sweet and fresh, so fresh that it was swimming an hour ago. Around you, the men eat quietly, the way hungry men eat, and for a long while there is no sound but spoons on wood, and the soft creak of the ship, and the hiss of the lantern's small flame.

Today, a sailor's supper can come out of a freezer or a can, and be hot in minutes. In 1720, many a meal at sea began the day before, with a lump of salt meat soaking in a tub.

Historians do not agree on how well pirates actually ate. Some point to the accounts of captured sea captains, who described pirate crews feasting and drinking for days after a rich prize, throwing away food that an honest sailor would have lived on for a month, and argue that pirates ate far better than the men of the navy or the merchant fleet. Others point to the long, lean weeks in between, the reports of crews reduced to a few biscuits a day, and the many pirate voyages that ended in hunger, and argue that the feasts were remembered precisely because they were rare. Very likely both are true, on the same ship, a few weeks apart.

Henry Ash, who cooked that supper, came to his trade in an old and very common way.

In the navies of these years, the post of ship's cook was very often given to a seaman who had been hurt in the service and could no longer climb the rigging. It was a kind of pension, paid in work. And the navy had a real pension, too, of sorts. Since the year after the Armada, English seamen had paid sixpence a month out of their wages into a fund called the Chatham Chest, so named because the money was kept, at first, in a great iron-bound chest at the dockyard at Chatham, and it paid out small sums to men who had been injured at sea.

Pirate crews kept their own version of the same idea. Many of their articles promised a fixed sum from the common stock to any man who lost a limb in the company's service. Roberts' articles set it at eight hundred dollars, which was a small fortune for a sailor. Henry's knee was hurt long ago, in the Queen's navy, in the last war, and the Chatham Chest gave him very little for it. When the crew of the Delight wrote their own articles in October, it was Henry who asked for a line about injured men, and it was carried without a single hand against it.

That fish, by the way, was Benjamin's.

He caught it at dusk, with a hand line over the side, while the others were stowing the sails. It is a codfish, a fine big one, nearly as long as his arm. In these years, the cod came close in to the coast of Maine in the winter, into the cold dark water of the bays, and a fisherman from Salem knew exactly where to drop a line for them. When he hauled it up over the rail, gleaming and heavy, the whole crew had cheered, and Henry Ash had taken it from him with both hands, like a gift.

Now Nathaniel Cobb is sharing it out.

That is the quartermaster's task, on a pirate ship. The quartermaster keeps the stores, and the plunder, and the peace. He shares out everything, the food, the drink, the clothes, the money, according to the articles, and he does it in front of everyone, so that every man can see it is fair. So he cuts the fish into nine pieces with his knife, as evenly as he can, and holds up each piece in the lantern light before it goes into a bowl. The smallest piece goes to himself. That is his custom. Nobody has ever asked him to do it, and nobody has ever commented on it, and every man aboard has noticed.

One of the nine pieces goes to Benjamin, who has never signed the articles, and who strictly need not have been given any. Nobody objects. It was his fish. Henry Ash gives him a wink across the ring. Benjamin looks down at his bowl, and eats.

Then comes the rum, which is the moment every man has been waiting for, though nobody would admit it. There is a single small keg left from the Mary and Sarah, and Nathaniel draws off a measure for each man into a tin cup, mixed with hot water from the kettle and a spoonful of brown sugar. It is not much. The company voted a week ago, by Roberts' kind of rule, to make the keg last until they reach the islands again. So it is a small cup, but it is hot, and it is sweet, and it is shared, and the men hold the tin in both hands to warm their fingers, and drink it slowly. When the cup comes to you, you hold it the same way, both hands round the hot tin, and you breathe in the steam before you drink. It smells of burnt sugar and something dark and sweet, like molasses, and when you sip it, it is hot enough to make your eyes water. You feel it travel all the way down, and settle, and glow. You do not hurry. Nobody here hurries over the rum.

The pot is nearly empty now. The spoons go quiet. Somebody sighs, very contentedly.

Owen Price is sitting on the ladder, the lowest rung, with his bowl on his knees, and he is so close to the lantern that he can feel its faint warmth on the side of his face. He scrapes the last of the pease out with his finger.

"Mr. Ash," he says, "is there more?"

Henry looks at the pot, and then at Nathaniel, and Nathaniel nods. There is a little more, at the bottom, and it goes round the circle a second time, a spoonful each, and the last scraping goes to Owen, because he is nineteen, and still growing, and everyone knows it.

James Gold and Matthew Searle come down from the anchor watch, stamping and blowing, their coats white with frost, and their supper has been kept for them in the pot, warm, beside the dying fire. Daniel Royce and Thomas Wills climb up to take their place, grumbling cheerfully, with their bowls still in their hands.

And when the bowls are empty, John Darby sets his own bowl down on a barrel head, and looks round the ring of faces in the lantern light, and says, in his quiet way, that there is a matter to put to the company tonight.

Every face turns to him. Then, one by one, they turn to Benjamin.

Above them all, on its beam, the horn lantern burns on, small and steady, and its soft light shines on every face alike.

## Part Four — Around a Quarter Past Six: A Show of Hands

By a quarter past six, the bowls are stacked in a bucket, the cook's fire on deck has been raked out and drowned, and a little cloud of steam is still rising from the wet sand where it burned. Down in the hold, the crew of the Delight settle themselves in a ring around the lantern.

How do nine men hold a proper vote, on a dark ship, with no paper and no ballot box?

The answer is very simple, and very old. The matter is put to the company, in plain words, by the captain or the quartermaster. Anyone who wishes to speak may speak, and nobody may interrupt him. And then every man who has signed the articles raises his hand, or keeps it down, and the quartermaster counts the hands aloud, in front of everyone. The greater number carries. That is all. What is decided by the hands is binding on every man, including the captain, until the company votes again.

The articles themselves are the heart of it all. Before a pirate crew set out on a cruise, they drew up a written agreement, a set of rules, usually somewhere between eight and a dozen of them. Every man who joined the crew signed it, and those who could not write their names made their mark, a cross, or a pair of initials, beside a name written for them. The articles set out who would get what share of any plunder, what would happen to a man who stole from his shipmates or deserted in a fight, what would be paid to a man who was injured, and how disputes would be settled. Then they were sworn to, solemnly, and sometimes nailed up somewhere in the ship, where anyone could read them.

The shares are worth hearing, because they are so different from the world these men had left. On Roberts' ship, the captain and the quartermaster each received two shares of a prize. The master, the boatswain and the gunner each received one share and a half. Other officers received one and a quarter. And every other man aboard received one full share. On a merchant ship of the time, a captain might earn many times what an ordinary seaman earned, and the owners, who never left the shore, took the greatest part of all.

On a pirate ship, there were no owners. The ship belonged to the crew. Every plank of her. Every rope, every barrel, every candle.

And the captain was chosen by the crew, too, by vote, from among themselves. He was usually picked for his seamanship, his nerve, and his luck. He had full command in a chase or a fight, when there was no time for talk, and his orders then had to be obeyed at once. But at every other time, the company was above him. He ate the same food. And, as Captain Johnson tells it, any man in the crew might walk into the captain's cabin whenever he pleased. And if the crew came to think that he had chosen badly, they could vote him out, as simply as they had voted him in.

Here is a famous case of exactly that. In November 1718, the pirate captain Charles Vane, sailing in the waters between Cuba and Hispaniola, came upon a ship that turned out to be a French man-of-war, and he chose to run from her rather than fight. Most of his crew thought he had been timid. So, as Captain Johnson tells it, the next day they put it to the vote, and Vane was voted out of his command, and his quartermaster, a man named John Rackham, was voted captain in his place. Vane and the few men who had sided with him were given a small sloop and sent off on their way. There was no fight about it. The hands had been counted.

So why did pirates, of all people, vote on nearly everything?

Part of the answer lies in the ships these men had come from. Almost every pirate of these years had been an ordinary sailor first, on a merchant ship or a navy ship, and on those ships the captain's power was very nearly absolute. He could cut a man's rations, stop his wages, or have him flogged, and there was very little a seaman could do about it. Many sailors of the time had served under captains who were cruel, or dishonest, or both. When those same men took a ship for themselves, the last thing they wanted was another master. Captain Johnson put it neatly. In his telling, pirates allowed a man to be their captain only on the understanding that they could be captain over him.

But part of the answer is much more practical. A pirate crew had no courts, no owners, no government, and no one at all to complain to. If they quarreled over the plunder, there was nobody to settle it but themselves, on a small ship, at sea, with every man armed. Voting, and written rules, and a quartermaster who shared things out in front of everyone, were the only way to keep a crew of tough, suspicious men from tearing itself apart. So the vote was not only a matter of fairness. It was how they survived each other.

That is the answer to our first question. They voted on nearly everything because most of them had once lived under masters who answered to no one, and because, with no law to protect them, the company's own word was the only law they had.

We have one witness who saw all this from close up. His name was William Snelgrave, and he was the captain of a merchant ship that was taken by pirates at the mouth of the Sierra Leone River, on the coast of West Africa, in April 1719. He spent some weeks as their prisoner, and many years later, in 1734, he published an account of it in London, in a book about the coast of Guinea. Snelgrave watched the pirates very closely, partly because his life depended on understanding them. And one thing that struck him was the power of the quartermaster. When some of the pirate captains helped themselves to fine clothes from Snelgrave's cargo, without first asking the quartermaster's leave, Snelgrave noticed that the ordinary crew were deeply offended by it. Even a captain, it seemed, could not simply take what he wanted. The plunder belonged to all of them.

Historians do not agree on what these pirate votes really meant. Some see in them something close to a rough, early democracy, a rebellion by ordinary working sailors against the harsh discipline of the merchant fleet and the navy, a world turned upside down at sea, where the men at the bottom made the rules for once. Others, especially some economists who have studied the pirate articles, argue that there was nothing very idealistic about it. To them, the votes and the shares were simply good business, the cleverest way for a band of thieves to cooperate without cheating each other. The truth may well be that the men themselves never stopped to ask which it was.

It is worth saying that these written articles were drawn up more than sixty years before the Constitution of the United States.

Now take your place in the ring, in the warm lantern light, as the matter is put.

You are sitting on a coil of rope, with your back against a cold barrel and your knees drawn up, and the men around you are so close that you can feel their warmth through your clothes. Their faces glow amber and shadow. Their breath hangs in the air above the lantern in a faint, shining haze. You can hear the water slapping softly against the hull, just on the other side of the planks, an inch or two from your shoulder. Someone's wet coat is steaming gently. Someone shifts, and a sea chest creaks.

Just above your head, nailed to the beam beside the lantern, is the crew's own copy of their articles. It is a single sheet of thick paper, a little stained and curled at the corners, held flat by four copper tacks. You lean back and look up at it in the amber light. The writing is John Darby's, neat and slanting. There are eleven articles, and below them, two columns of names. Some are signed in a good clear hand. Daniel Royce has written his in large, careful capitals, pressing hard. James Gold's signature is small and elegant, with a flourish under it. Henry Ash could not write, so beside his name, written for him by the captain, there is a firm black cross. And beside the name of Owen Price, who can write perfectly well, there is a tiny drawing in ink, of a fiddle, which he added himself, the night they signed, to make the others laugh.

There is no name for Benjamin Gale. There is only a space, at the bottom, which has stayed empty since October.

John Darby speaks first, quietly. He says that they lie tonight a few miles from a farm where a boat might land. He says that Benjamin Gale was taken against his will, in October, because they were short of hands, and that he has worked as hard as any man aboard since, and complained less than most. And he proposes that tomorrow, when the water party goes ashore, Benjamin should go with them, and not come back. That he should be given his warm coat, and food for three days, and a letter in the captain's own hand, stating plainly that he was taken by force, and never signed.

Then he sits down, and there is a silence. You can hear the candle hiss.

Daniel Royce speaks next. He is not unkind about it. He only says what someone has to say. That a man set ashore will talk, and that a Massachusetts man will talk to Massachusetts magistrates, and that within a week every sloop and shallop on this coast will know that pirates were lying in Casco Bay.

Nathaniel Cobb answers him, slowly, as he says everything. He says that by the time Benjamin has walked to the nearest town through the snow, the Delight will be three days south, with her topmast mended and her casks full. And he says something else, more quietly, looking at the deck. That every man in this ring was once a sailor with no say in where he went. And that they swore, in October, that this ship would be different.

Nobody speaks for a while. The candle hisses again. Somewhere forward, a drop of water falls into the bilge with a tiny, hollow sound. Henry Ash rubs his knee. Owen looks from face to face, and holds his breath.

Then the hands go up.

You watch them rise, one after another, into the lantern light. Nathaniel's first, broad and scarred. James Gold's, long and steady. Matthew Searle's, Thomas Wills', Henry Ash's, Owen's, quick and eager, and then John Darby's own. Seven. And then, after a long moment, with a small rueful smile and a shrug, Daniel Royce raises his hand as well. Eight hands. Not one against.

Benjamin cannot vote. He has never signed. He sits very still on his sea chest, with his hands clasped hard between his knees, and his face, in the amber light, looks suddenly much younger than twenty-two.

Nathaniel reaches over and puts one heavy hand on his shoulder, and leaves it there. Nobody says anything. Nobody needs to.

A second matter is put, and carried just as quickly. The Delight will lie here through tomorrow, while Thomas mends the topmast and the water casks are filled, and then, with the first fair northwest wind, she will run south for the warm islands again. Eight hands rise for that, too, and there is a murmur of approval round the ring, and someone laughs, and the spell of seriousness breaks.

Benjamin stands up, then, because he feels he ought to say something, and finds that he does not know what. He looks round at them all, at the eight faces in the lantern light, men he had feared, in October, more than he had ever feared anything. He says thank you, twice. He says that he will tell the magistrates nothing that would harm them, and that if anyone asks him, he will say that he was used well. Daniel Royce, of all people, tells him to sit down before he falls down, and everyone laughs again, and Benjamin sits.

Henry Ash limps over to his own sea chest, and rummages in it, and comes back with a small cloth bag. He unties it, and inside are dried apples, wrinkled and brown, taken off a Boston brig in the autumn and saved, a handful at a time, for an occasion. He has decided that this is the occasion. The bag goes round the ring, and every man takes one slice, and chews it slowly, and it tastes of a far-off orchard on a summer afternoon.

You take one too, when the bag comes to you. It is tough and sweet and faintly sharp, and it smells of the autumn. You hold it in your mouth a long time, and let it soften.

And above them all, on its beam, the horn lantern burns on, steady and warm, as if it, too, had been counted.

## Part Five — Around Seven O'Clock: Wool, Tar and a Monmouth Cap

At seven o'clock, the bell on deck rings out, slow and clear, and a moment later two pairs of boots come thumping down the ladder. Daniel Royce and Thomas Wills have been relieved early, for a while, so that they can thaw. They stand under the lantern beating their arms against their sides, with frost in their eyebrows, and the whole hold seems to grow a degree colder just from looking at them.

What did a sailor wear against a New England winter, three hundred years ago?

Not what most of us would guess. Seamen of these years dressed differently from men ashore, so differently that you could pick out a sailor in a crowded street by his clothes alone. Ashore, a working man wore knee breeches and long stockings, and a long coat that reached well down his legs. At sea, those long skirts and tight breeches were a nuisance, and a danger, when a man had to climb the rigging in a wind. So sailors wore short jackets, cut off at the waist, so that nothing could catch. And instead of breeches, many of them wore loose, wide-legged trousers of canvas or linen, or very wide, short breeches that flapped about the knee like a skirt, which were called petticoat breeches.

Much of this clothing was bought ready-made, which in 1720 was unusual. Most people of the time had their clothes made for them, by a tailor or by someone in their own family. But sailors bought what were called slops, cheap ready-made clothes, sold in sea ports and on board ship. The word survived for centuries afterward, in the name of the slop chest, the little store of clothing that a ship's purser sold to the crew.

Under the jacket went a shirt of linen, often of a checked blue-and-white pattern, and over the shirt a waistcoat of wool, sometimes two. On the legs went knitted woolen stockings, and on the feet went low leather shoes with buckles, although in warm seas many sailors went barefoot, and on deck in rain or spray they might go barefoot even in the cold, because wet leather slipped and bare toes gripped. Around the neck went a cloth, a kerchief, which could be pulled up over the ears and mouth.

And over everything, in foul weather, went tar.

Sailors had long known that canvas soaked in tar would turn the rain. So they made jackets of old canvas, painted them with tar, or with a mixture of tar and oil, and let them dry stiff and black and shining. They did the same with their hats. The coats were stiff, and heavy, and they smelled overpoweringly of pine and smoke, but they kept the worst of the water out. Sailors wore them so commonly that by the middle of the 1600s a seaman was known in English as a tarpaulin, and later, simply, as a tar.

Washing all this was a problem of its own. Fresh water at sea was far too precious to wash clothes in, so sailors washed them in seawater, scrubbing them on deck, and hung them in the rigging to dry. But clothes washed in seawater seldom dry properly. The salt stays in the cloth, and salt draws damp out of the air, so a shirt washed in the sea will feel clammy again within an hour, even on a fine day. Sailors knew this well. When a good shower of rain came over the ship, men would rush to spread their clothes on deck, or even stand in the rain in them, to rinse the salt away.

And there was a strange, small luxury in the pirate articles that had to do with exactly this. Roberts' articles, as Captain Johnson printed them, said that when the crew took a prize, the men would go aboard her in turn, by a list, so that it was fair, because on those occasions every man was allowed, over and above his share, a change of clothes. A shift, they called it. A clean, dry shirt, from somebody else's sea chest. For a man who had been wearing the same salt-stiff shirt for a month, that was no small thing. Most of what the Delight's crew are wearing tonight came to them in exactly that way.

There is one small piece of clothing that had a whole law to itself.

On his head, a sailor of these years very often wore a knitted cap of thick brown or gray wool, close-fitting, round, with a small button or loop on top. These were called Monmouth caps, after the town on the border of Wales where many of them were knitted. They were warm, cheap, and nearly indestructible, and soldiers and sailors wore them all over the English world. And here is the surprising part. In 1571, the English Parliament passed a law to protect the cap-makers' trade, which ordered that ordinary people, apart from the nobility and some others, must wear a woolen cap, made and finished in England, on Sundays and holy days. The law was repealed in 1597. But the caps went on.

They even turn up in Shakespeare. In his play Henry the Fifth, first performed around 1599, the Welsh captain Fluellen reminds the king of an old battle, in which, he says, the Welshmen did good service, "wearing leeks in their Monmouth caps." Shakespeare could use the phrase without explaining it. Everyone in his audience knew what a Monmouth cap was.

Now, nearly every pirate in the picture books wears silk and lace, and a coat with gold buttons. There is a grain of truth in that. Pirates did take fine clothes from their prizes, and some of them wore those clothes. Captain Johnson tells us that on the last morning of his life, off the coast of Africa in February 1722, Bartholomew Roberts dressed himself in a rich crimson damask waistcoat and breeches, with a red feather in his hat, and a gold chain round his neck with a diamond cross hanging from it.

Historians do not agree on how typical that was. Some argue that pirates really did love to dress well, that fine clothes from a captured ship were one of the great pleasures of the life, and that trial records and the accounts of their captives describe pirates in silk shirts and velvet coats. Others point out that silk and velvet did not last long in salt spray and tar, that most pirates, most of the time, must have worn the same rough seaman's clothes they had always worn, and that the swashbuckling costume of the picture books owes a great deal to illustrators of the late 1800s and early 1900s. Roberts in his crimson waistcoat may have been memorable precisely because he was unusual.

Now get yourself ready for a night watch, and feel what all this is like to wear.

You start with the shirt you already have on, the checked linen one, damp at the collar. Over it you pull a wool waistcoat, and then a second, older one, its buttons mismatched. You are already warmer. Over those you shrug on your short blue jacket of thick, felted wool, so heavy that it hangs on your shoulders like a hand. Then the stockings, a second pair over the first, pulled up over your knees. Then the long kerchief, wound twice round your neck and tucked into your jacket. And then, last of all, the tarred canvas coat. It is cold and stiff, and it crackles as you pull it on, and it smells like the inside of a pine forest after a fire. It will not keep you warm. That is the job of the wool. Its job is to keep the wind and the wet away from the wool.

And then your cap. You pull it down over your ears, and your whole head goes soft and warm and quiet, the noises of the hold suddenly muffled, the air on your face suddenly sharper by contrast. You have perhaps five layers on now, and you can barely bend your arms. You stand there, stiff as a scarecrow. Owen grins at you. You feel like a bundle of washing. You also feel, for the first time since sunset, that you might just be able to face that wind.

Today, a sailor heading out on a winter deck would pull on a thin fleece and a light waterproof shell, made from plastics, and be warmer in them than any man in 1720 could have been in five layers of wool and tar.

Down in the hold, there is a line strung between two beams, well away from the lantern, and it is hung with wet things: stockings, kerchiefs, a pair of mittens, a shirt. They drip slowly onto the planks. They will not dry tonight, not in this cold. But they will be a little less wet by morning, and that is something. Every so often, someone reaches up and squeezes a stocking, and a few cold drops fall, and the line sways, and settles again.

Owen Price is sitting on the ladder again, his favorite place, mending a hole in the thumb of his mitten with a needle and a length of wool. His cap is a Monmouth cap, a real one, brown and close-knit, and his mother knitted it for him in Pembrokeshire, the winter before he went to sea. He has worn it every day since, through two summers in the islands and now this winter in the north, and it has been washed by rain and spray, and dried by the sun, and is beginning to go thin at the crown. He loves it more than anything he owns, apart from his fiddle. When it finally wears through, he says, he will darn it. And when the darns wear through, he will darn the darns.

Benjamin Gale, across the ring, is looking at his own coat.

It is the coat the company voted him, a good one, of thick blue wool, lined with flannel, that came off a Boston brig in the autumn. He has worn it for weeks, but tonight, for the first time, it is his. He keeps touching the buttons. Nathaniel Cobb, beside him, is rummaging in his own sea chest, and comes up with a pair of gray stockings, thick and new, knitted in Marblehead, he says, by a woman who was very fond of him once, a long time ago.

"You will want dry feet for the walk," he says. "Take them."

Benjamin starts to say that he cannot, and Nathaniel simply drops them in his lap, and turns away to something else, and the matter is closed.

Henry Ash has his stiff leg out in front of him, close to the warmth of the men, and he rubs the knee slowly with both hands. Cold always finds it first. James Gold, sitting beside him, wordlessly takes off his own scarf, a long one of red wool, and folds it twice, and lays it over Henry's knee. Henry pats it, and says nothing, and leaves it there. It is a small thing. It is the kind of thing that happens on this ship.

John Darby's long blue coat is the finest garment on the ship, and he is faintly embarrassed by it. It came from the master of the Mary and Sarah, who left it hanging in his cabin when he went over the side into the boat, and when the company shared out the sloop's goods, it was voted to the captain, by a show of hands, because, as Matthew Searle put it, the captain ought to look like a captain if they ever had to speak to anyone. John wears it on deck in the cold, and takes it off below, and folds it carefully over a barrel, as though it might still be asked for back.

Thomas Wills, in the ring, has taken off his tarred coat and laid it beside him, and the lantern light runs along it in long black gleams. Under it he wears a carpenter's leather apron, scarred and soft, with a pocket on the front for nails and a loop for his hammer, and he will not take it off, not even to sleep. He says the cold gets in through the front of a man first, and the leather keeps it out. Nobody has ever been able to argue him out of it.

You look down at your own hands, in the lantern light. They are red and raw across the knuckles, and the skin has cracked a little at the fingertips, as every sailor's hands do in winter, from cold and salt and wet rope. Henry Ash, seeing you look, leans over and passes you a small tin. Inside is a lump of yellow mutton tallow, the same stuff the candle is made of, softened with a little oil. You rub a fingertip of it into your knuckles, and it stings for a moment, and then it soothes. It smells of the lantern. Your hands feel better at once, and you flex them slowly.

Daniel and Thomas, warmer now, pull their caps down, and their tarred coats on, and climb up the ladder again into the dark, letting in a gust of air so cold it makes the lantern flame bow and shiver in its horn case. Then the hatch closes, and the flame stands up straight once more, and burns on, small and golden, over the dripping line of stockings.

## Part Six — Around Half Past Seven: A Fiddle on the Eve of the Sabbath

By half past seven, Owen Price has taken his fiddle out of its canvas bag. It is a plain brown fiddle, scuffed at the edges, with one peg that does not match the others, and he holds it under his coat for a few minutes before he plays, to warm the strings, the way you might warm your hands.

There are more lights in the hold than usual tonight. Besides the horn lantern on its beam, two short candle-ends have been lit for the music and the cards, and stuck upright in their own melted tallow on the tops of two barrels, where their small bare flames lean and tremble every time somebody moves. Up on deck, a tiny lamp glows in the binnacle, the wooden box that houses the compass beside the tiller. It is a rare, extravagant evening, by the standards of this ship, and everyone knows that it cannot last long.

Why would a crew of pirates write a rule about their musicians?

It sounds like a joke, but it is real. The eleventh of Bartholomew Roberts' articles, as Captain Johnson printed them in 1724, says that the musicians shall have rest on the Sabbath day, but that on the other six days and nights, none shall have rest without special favor. In other words, the musicians had one night off a week. On every other night, if the company wanted music, the musicians played.

That tells you how much music mattered to these crews. Ships of the time often carried a fiddler, or a man with a pipe or a drum, and on pirate ships, where every man was a volunteer and there was no officer with a whip, music did a great deal of work. It set the rhythm for heavy labor, for hauling on ropes and turning the capstan to raise the anchor. It filled long empty evenings, at anchor or in a calm. It kept up men's spirits in cold and wet and boredom. And in a chase, it could be used to make a pirate ship seem larger and more frightening than she was. When Roberts' ships sailed into the harbor of Trepassey, in Newfoundland, in June 1720, five months after our night, one report says that his musicians were playing as he came, and the crews of more than twenty ships at anchor there took to their boats and fled ashore.

Musicians who were taken from captured ships were sometimes forced to join a pirate crew, simply because the pirates wanted their music. A good fiddler was worth having. Owen came willingly, but he knows his worth.

And tonight is a special night for him, because tomorrow is Sunday. Tomorrow, under the articles of the Delight, which copy that very rule, he may rest. So tonight he will play.

There is just one small problem, and Nathaniel Cobb raises it, with a perfectly straight face. In Massachusetts, where he grew up, the Sabbath did not begin at midnight. It began at sunset on Saturday, and lasted until sunset on Sunday, and in a strict Puritan town, a man fiddling in the dark on a Saturday evening would have been in serious trouble. So, by the custom of the country they are anchored in, Nathaniel says, the Sabbath began three hours ago, and Owen ought to put his fiddle away.

There is a pause. Then James Gold proposes that the Delight keeps English time on Saturday nights, and the motion is carried, by eight hands and a great deal of laughter, and Owen tucks the fiddle under his chin.

Now, the drink of these crews was rum, and rum is a young drink.

It was born in the sugar islands of the Caribbean, in the middle of the 1600s, as a way of using up the waste of the sugar works. When sugar cane was boiled down in great copper kettles, a thick scum rose to the surface, and a dark, sweet syrup was left over at the end, which was molasses. Planters found that if they let these leftovers ferment, and then distilled them, they made a fierce, cheap, powerful spirit. It was soon the common drink of every sailor in the Atlantic, and by the early 1700s, the merchants of New England were importing shiploads of molasses from the islands and distilling their own rum, in Boston and Newport and Salem.

One of the first men to write about it was an Englishman named Richard Ligon, who lived on Barbados in the late 1640s. In his book A True and Exact History of the Island of Barbados, published in London in 1657, he described the drink that was made from the skimmings of the sugar coppers, which, he said, the islanders called kill-devil. An even earlier description of the island, written by another visitor in 1651, called the same spirit by an older name, rumbullion, and described it, memorably, as "a hot, hellish, and terrible liquor." That word, rumbullion, may well be where the word rum comes from, although nobody is entirely sure.

There was a warmer and more civilized way to drink it, too, and Nathaniel Cobb is talking about it now, rather wistfully. In the taverns of New England in these years, the favorite winter drink was called flip. You filled a big earthenware mug with beer, and stirred in rum and molasses, or sugar, and then you took a loggerhead, which was a long iron rod with a ball on the end, red-hot from the tavern fire, and you plunged it into the mug. The whole thing hissed and foamed and frothed up over the rim, and the hot iron burned the sugar, and gave the drink a dark, bitter-sweet taste, like toffee. Nathaniel says that on a January night in Marblehead, there was nothing better in the world. And he says it in such a way that every man in the hold can taste it.

Tonight there is no tavern, no loggerhead, and the deck fire is long out. There is only the memory of flip, and Owen's fiddle.

He starts with Lilliburlero, the old marching tune that nearly every Englishman knew, quick and bouncing. Matthew Searle starts to tap his foot on the planks. Then Owen plays Over the Hills and Far Away, the soldiers' song that was all over London in these years, and Henry Ash, unexpectedly, sings the words, in a rough, true, surprising voice. Then Owen plays a slow tune from Wales that nobody else knows, and the hold goes quiet while he plays it.

Now sit back against the hull, and let the music fill the hold around you.

You are leaning against the curved oak of the ship's side, and through the wood you can feel the cold of the sea, but in front of you there is the warmth of the men and the soft glow of the lantern. The fiddle is very loud in this small space, and very sweet. It fills the hold right up to the beams. It makes the lantern seem brighter. You watch Owen's bow arm move, quick and light, and his eyes are half closed, and his head is tilted against the fiddle as if he were listening to it. You feel your own foot tapping, almost without your permission, on the damp planks. The men around you are swaying, very slightly, and a few are humming. Someone is drumming softly on a barrel head with his fingertips.

And through it all, under it all, you can feel the ship herself, rocking gently on her cable, as if she, too, were listening.

Today, you can hear almost any music ever recorded, at any hour, by touching a small glass screen. In 1720, if there was no fiddler on board, there was no music at all, except what the men could make with their own voices.

There is one more of Roberts' rules that matters tonight. His third article said that no man should game for money, with dice or cards. Gambling, more than almost anything, started fights among sailors, and fights on a small armed ship could end in disaster. So when the fiddle rests, and Thomas Wills brings out a pack of greasy cards, the men play for dried peas, a handful each, which go back into the cook's bag at the end of the night. Nobody wins anything. Everybody plays as if a fortune depended on it. The peas click softly on the barrel heads. The candle-ends flicker.

You are dealt in, too, and you hold your cards close to your chest in the dim light, squinting to tell the hearts from the diamonds. The cards are soft and furred at the corners from years of damp hands, and one of the knaves has a thumbprint of tar across his face. Your little pile of dried peas sits on the barrel head in front of you, yellow and wrinkled. You win a hand. You lose two. Daniel Royce, across from you, groans each time he loses, very loudly, as if he were being robbed, and wins the next hand with a look of perfect innocence. Nobody is keeping score. Everyone is keeping score.

The songs, by the way, are not quite the songs you might expect. The rolling work songs that most of us now call sea shanties belong mostly to a later age, to the big merchant sailing ships of the 1800s. In 1720, sailors sang the popular ballads and tunes of the day, the same songs that were sung in taverns ashore, and hauled on ropes to short, rhythmic calls and chants. So Owen's tunes tonight are tavern tunes, and marching tunes, and dances. They are the songs of the whole English-speaking world in these years, carried to sea.

His fiddle has its own small story. He bought it in the market town of Haverfordwest, at the age of fifteen, from an old man who had played it at weddings for forty years, and he paid seven shillings for it, which was nearly every penny he had. It went with him to Bristol, and then to sea, and it has been to Jamaica and the Bahamas and the coast of Carolina. It has been wet more times than he can count. Twice its seams have come apart in the damp, and twice Thomas Wills has glued them for him, with fish glue warmed in a spoon.

Historians do not agree on how drunken pirate crews really were. Captain Johnson's book is full of crews who drank for days on end, and some captured captains described pirates who were rarely sober from one week to the next. Some historians accept this, and point out that men who expected short lives had little reason to save anything, least of all their rum. Others remind us that much of what we know was written by pirates' enemies, by the ministers who preached at them and the officials who prosecuted them, who had every reason to make them sound as wild as possible. And they point out that a crew that was always drunk would not have caught many ships, or kept them for long.

James Gold takes a turn on the fiddle, when Owen's fingers tire. He plays only one tune, with his eyes on the lantern, a quick, swinging dance from the wharves of Bridgetown, in Barbados, where he grew up, and he plays it surprisingly well. Owen watches his fingers with real respect. And when James hands the fiddle back, Owen asks him to teach him the tune, tomorrow, on the Sabbath, when the musician is resting, and James says that teaching is not playing, and so the articles allow it.

Matthew Searle, at the end of the next tune, gets up and dances a hornpipe, very gravely, in a space about the size of a doorway, between the ladder and the mast. He is forty-five, and broad, and the hold is so low that he has to dance with his head bent, and his arms folded across his chest, the way sailors often danced the hornpipe, and his feet patter on the planks like rain. When he finishes, the men drum on the barrels for him, and he sits down, very red in the face, looking pleased. Nobody has ever seen him dance before. Nobody aboard will mention it tomorrow, either.

Benjamin has not danced. He sits with his new coat round his shoulders and his new stockings in his lap, and he listens, and he watches them all in the lantern light, as if he were trying to remember them. Which, in a way, he is. He wants to keep this. The music, the faces, the warm light. He did not expect to want that, not on this ship.

Nathaniel Cobb glances up at the sandglass that hangs at the foot of the ladder, the twin of the one on deck, and watches the last of the sand run through. Then he looks at the lantern, and then at the captain, and John Darby nods. It is nearly time.

Owen plays one last tune, softly, and lets the final note fade away into the beams. The cards are gathered up, and the peas go back into their bag. Thomas Wills licks his finger and thumb, and leans over the two candle-ends on the barrels, and waits. And on its hook, the horn lantern glows on, among the last few lights on board the Delight, and very soon to be the only one.

## Part Seven — Eight O'Clock: Eight Bells, and Every Light But One

At eight o'clock, the last grain of sand runs through the glass on deck, and Daniel Royce turns it over and rings the bell. Eight strokes, in pairs, ding-ding, ding-ding, ding-ding, ding-ding, ringing out across the black water of the bay. The dog watches are over. The first watch of the night has begun.

What does eight bells actually mean, aboard a ship like this one?

It means that a four-hour watch has ended, and another has begun. Eight bells is rung at four, at eight, and at twelve o'clock, day and night, and so it is the sound that marks the great turnings of a ship's day. At eight in the evening, on most ships, it marked something more. It was the moment when the long evening ended and the night truly began. The men who had the first watch went up on deck. The men who did not would go below, and sling their hammocks, and try to sleep. And all over the ship, by order, the lights went out.

Below, in the hold of the Delight, Thomas Wills pinches out the first candle-end on its barrel, between a wet finger and thumb, with a tiny hiss. Then the second. Two thin threads of gray smoke rise from them and drift slowly up toward the beams, and the smell of hot tallow hangs for a moment in the air. On deck, James Gold opens the little door of the binnacle, and blows out the lamp beside the compass, and closes it again.

Now, all over the Delight, there is only one light left. The horn lantern, on its hook, by the foot of the ladder.

To understand why, you have to picture what a wooden ship of 1720 really was. She was built of oak and cedar, which burn. Her seams were packed with oakum, which is old rope, picked apart into loose fibers and soaked in tar, and which burns very fiercely indeed. Her rigging was of hemp, coated with tar, and her sails were of canvas. In her hold, there were casks of rum, which burns. There was tallow, and oil, and pitch, and spare tar in barrels. And there was gunpowder. Even a small sloop like the Delight carried a few barrels of it, and a big pirate ship carried tons.

A fire at sea was not like a fire on land. There was nowhere to run. There were no neighbors to help, no fire engine, no well. There was only the ocean all around, and a few leather buckets, and the hope that the men could stop the flames before they reached the powder. A ship that caught fire far from land could be lost entirely, with all her people, in a matter of minutes. Of all the things that sailors feared, many feared fire most, more even than storms, because a storm could be ridden out.

And the most dangerous hours, for fire, were the hours of darkness. That was when tired men, half asleep, would go below with a candle. That was when a candle might be knocked over by the roll of the ship, or left burning in a corner by a man who fell asleep beside it, or carried, unguarded, into the hold where the spirits and the powder were stored.

So the navies of Europe had made rules about lights at night, for a long time. In ship after ship, the lights below decks were ordered out at a set hour, except for a few lanterns kept burning under the watch of a trusted man. Pirates did not invent the idea. But, as with so much else, they wrote it down.

Here is the surprising thing about the one light that remains. It is not a naked flame. It is a candle shut inside a case of tin and horn, and horn is a remarkable material. Ox horn, heated until soft, split into thin sheets, and scraped and polished until the light shines through it, does not burn. It does not shatter if it is dropped, as glass does. It lets out a soft, even glow, enough to see by, and it keeps the flame safe inside it, away from loose straw, and rope, and the swinging edge of a hammock. Many people believe that the old English spelling of the word, lanthorn, came from exactly this use of horn, although some scholars think it was only a happy accident of spelling. Either way, a horn lantern was about the safest light that a ship of the time could carry.

The big warships of the time went further still. Their powder rooms, deep down in the bottom of the ship, were never entered with any flame at all. Instead, beside each powder room there was a small separate chamber called the light room, with a thick window of glass set into the wall between them. A lantern was hung in the light room, and its light shone through the glass into the powder room, so that the gunners working there could see what they were doing, while the flame itself stayed safely on the other side of the wall. Daniel Royce, who served as a boy in a ship of the line, has told the others about it more than once. On the Delight, there is no light room. There is only a rule that no flame goes near the powder at all, and Daniel enforces it like a sheepdog.

The candle inside the lantern is worth a moment, too. It is a tallow candle, made from the hard white fat of sheep and cattle, melted down and poured round a cotton wick. Wax candles, made from beeswax, burned cleaner and brighter and smelled sweet, but they cost several times as much, and were for churches and rich men's houses. Tallow candles were what ordinary people burned. They smoked a little, and smelled of the kitchen, and their wicks did not burn away cleanly, so that every so often someone had to trim the charred end with a pair of scissors called snuffers, or the flame would grow dim and sooty. In a shut lantern, this is the job of the man on watch below. It is a small job. It is done many times a night.

Now sit with that one light, here in the hold, and feel how dark the dark becomes.

With the candle-ends out, the hold seems to shrink around you. The far corners, where the barrels are stacked against the hull, have vanished into blackness. The beams overhead are only faint lines of shadow. All that is left is a small warm circle of honey-colored light, about as wide as you could stretch your arms, and in it, the faces of the men nearest to the lantern, and the foot of the ladder, and the dripping line of stockings. Beyond that circle, there is nothing at all.

But your ears seem to wake up as your eyes lose their work. You can hear the soft creak of the hull, and the slow drip of water somewhere in the bilge, and the faint ticking of the ice against the planks outside. You can hear someone breathing slowly in the dark beyond the light. You can hear your own heartbeat, if you listen. And above you, faint through the deck, you can hear the footsteps of the watch, slow and heavy, crossing from one side of the ship to the other, and back again.

The smell of the snuffed candles fades. The cold creeps back a little. You draw your coat closer around you, and lean toward the lantern, and its small glow lies warm across your face.

The rule that the crew of the Delight are keeping tonight is one of the most famous pirate rules of all. It is the fourth of Bartholomew Roberts' articles, as Captain Johnson printed them in London, in 1724. And it reads, in the spelling of the time, like this. "The Lights and Candles to be put out at eight a-Clock at Night: If any of the Crew, after that Hour, still remained inclined for Drinking, they were to do it on the open Deck."

Another pirate crew, that of John Phillips, who sailed out of Newfoundland a few years later, in 1723, wrote a rule of their own that was even more exact. It said that any man who smoked tobacco in the hold without a cap on his pipe, or who carried a candle there "lighted without a Lanthorn," would receive exactly the same punishment as a man who struck a shipmate. On Phillips' list, at least, a careless candle was taken as seriously as a blow.

Up on deck, James Gold has taken the first watch, with Benjamin Gale beside him. They stand in the bow, in their tarred coats, with their caps pulled down, and the cold is almost beyond describing. It sets the teeth aching. It makes the inside of the nose crackle with every breath. But the night is magnificent. There is no cloud. The moon, a little more than three quarters full, rides high in the south, and lays a long, broken, silver road across the bay toward them, and every rope and spar of the Delight is drawn against the sky in silver and black.

Benjamin looks back along the deck at the ship, dark from end to end, without a gleam of light anywhere, except for a faint, warm glow rising from the open hatch amidships. From the shore, he thinks, from that farmhouse on the point, the Delight would be almost invisible tonight. A dark shape, among dark islands, on a dark sea.

He says this to James, and James nods. He says that in the islands, that is half the reason for the rule. A ship that shows no lights cannot easily be found.

Henry Ash, below, has his own reason for loving the rule, and he has never told it to anyone aboard but Owen. In the last war, when he was a young man in the Queen's navy, he saw a ship burn. It was far off, across a wide harbor, at night, and he never learned what started it. He only remembers the light of it, orange on the water, and how very fast it grew, and how quiet his own ship went as every man aboard stood and watched. That was more than fifteen years ago. He still checks the lantern door every time he passes it, without thinking, the way some men touch wood.

Now go up the ladder yourself, for a moment, and look at the ship from the deck.

You put your head out of the hatch into the cold, and it is like putting your face into a basin of icy water. Your eyes water at once. Then they clear, and you see that the whole ship is dark. There is no light at the stern, no light in the binnacle, no light at the bow. The deck is a long pale shape, frosted silver by the moon. The mast rises black into a sky so full of stars that it seems crowded. And the only warmth you can see in all that cold silver world is the faint golden glow behind you, rising out of the hatch, from the one lantern below.

You hear the two men of the watch talking softly in the bow. You hear the cable creak in the hawse hole. You hear, very far off, somewhere on the island, an owl. Then the cold drives you back down the ladder, gratefully, into the small circle of light.

Historians do not agree on the main reason for the eight o'clock rule. Some see it as a simple matter of safety, the same fear of fire that ruled every wooden ship, written down by men who knew that a pirate crew far from any friendly port could not afford to lose their ship. Others point to the second half of the rule itself, about drinking on the open deck. Captain Johnson thought that Roberts, who by his account was a sober man himself, meant the rule as a check on his crew's heavy drinking, and found in the end that it did very little good. And some add the plain advantage that James Gold has just pointed out to Benjamin. A dark ship is a hidden ship.

So that is the answer to our second question. Every light was ordered out at eight o'clock because a single careless candle could burn a wooden ship to the waterline, with every man aboard, far from any help; because men who wanted to go on drinking past eight could do it in the cold, on deck, where no candle was needed; and because a ship without lights was very hard to find in the dark. And the one lantern that stayed lit was shut in horn and tin, and watched over, so that the men below would never be left in complete darkness.

Below, Henry Ash comes slowly down the ladder, rubbing his knee. He stops at the bottom, and looks up at the lantern, and checks the candle with his eye. It is burning well, straight and clear, with about two-thirds of its length still to go. It will last until after midnight. He knows that, by long habit, the way a farmer knows when the cows will want milking. He gives the lantern door a small push with one finger, to be sure that it is shut, and then he goes to his hammock.

And the lantern burns on in the dark hold, steady and small, the one light left on board the Delight, exactly as the articles allow.

## Part Eight — Around Nine O'Clock: Ice in the Rigging, and a Winter Moon

At nine o'clock, two bells of the first watch ring out softly across the bay, and James Gold turns the glass, and then he reaches up and lifts a short wooden mallet down from its hook on the mast. There is work to do. The ice is growing.

Why does the watch, on a quiet night at anchor, have to beat ice off the ship?

Because ice, at sea, is weight, and weight in the wrong place is danger. On a cold, still night like this one, every drop of spray that the small waves throw up against the bow freezes where it lands. The mist that rises off the water in the bitter cold settles on the rigging and freezes there, too, in a fine white fur. Every rope becomes a rope of ice, twice its own thickness. The rail grows a crust like a loaf of sugar. And all that ice adds up. In a long hard freeze, a small vessel could carry tons of it, much of it high up in her rigging, where it made her top-heavy and slow to right herself if she rolled. Fishermen on these coasts, in winter, knew that a ship could be made unsafe by ice alone.

There is a second problem, too, more ordinary but just as serious. A frozen rope cannot run. If the sails had to be set in a hurry, if the wind shifted in the night and the ship had to move, ropes frozen stiff into their blocks and pulleys would not budge. So, on a winter night, the watch went round the ship with mallets and wooden clubs, and beat the ice off the ropes, and chipped it from the rail and the deck, and swept it over the side. It was cold, slow, patient work, and it kept the men on watch moving, which in itself kept them alive.

The winters of these years were hard ones. The centuries between about 1300 and 1850 are often called the Little Ice Age, a long stretch when winters across much of the northern world were, on average, colder than they are now. In New England, harbors froze over in the worst winters, so that people could walk across the ice from one shore to another. Rivers froze solid for weeks. The sea along the coast steamed in the bitter cold, a white fog rolling off the water that the people of these coasts later named sea smoke.

And only three years before our night, New England had lived through the most famous winter storm of its colonial history.

In late February and early March of 1717, a series of great snowstorms struck the coast one after another, for more than a week, and buried the whole country from Connecticut to Maine. People in later years remembered it simply as the Great Snow. Drifts rose higher than the houses. Whole flocks of sheep and herds of cattle were buried where they stood. Roads vanished for weeks, and the post riders could not get through.

We have a careful witness to it. Cotton Mather, the famous Boston minister, was also a keen observer of nature, and a Fellow of the Royal Society in London, the great scientific society of the age. In 1717, he wrote to the Society to describe the storm, and his letter is full of small, astonishing details. Among them, he told of a farmer whose flock of sheep had been buried under a single great drift of snow. When the drift was at last dug away, weeks later, most of the flock were long dead. But two of them, he wrote, were found still alive, after twenty-eight days beneath the snow. He also told of hens that had survived for days under the drifts, and turkeys that lasted longer still. The snow, it seemed, had covered them so deeply that it kept the worst of the cold away.

Nathaniel Cobb was far away in Jamaica that winter. But he heard about the Great Snow that summer, from a Marblehead skipper he met on the wharf at Port Royal, who swore that the snow had reached the upper windows of his house, and that he and his sons had to tunnel out of the back door to reach the woodpile. Nathaniel has told the story so often since that the others half believe he was there himself. They like the story anyway, and he tells it every winter.

Historians do not agree on how cold the Little Ice Age truly was, or how evenly. Some describe it as a real and lasting change, a period of centuries in which the winters of Europe and North America were often far harsher than ours, with frozen rivers, failed harvests, and great storms. Others argue that the cooling was uneven, that it varied a great deal from one region and one decade to another, and that it is better thought of as a run of unusually cold spells, separated by milder years. A New England January in 1720 may well have been colder than one today. It was certainly cold enough.

Now come up on deck, and take a turn with the mallet.

You climb out of the hatch into a world of silver. The moon is high in the south now, very bright, and the whole bay is lit by it, the black water, the white ice along the shore, the dark spruce, the gray rock. Your breath comes out in great white clouds that hang in the air and slowly drift away. The cold is so deep that it seems to have a sound of its own, a kind of high, thin ringing in the ears. And low over the water, all around the ship, a faint white mist is rising, curling slowly off the sea as if the bay were a great pot just beginning to steam.

James Gold hands you the mallet. It is a short, heavy thing of oak, worn smooth by many hands. You go to the nearest shroud, one of the thick ropes that run from the side of the ship up to the masthead, and you see that it is cased in ice, glittering in the moonlight, and as thick as your wrist. You strike it, not too hard, the way James shows you. The ice cracks with a sharp, clean sound, and falls away in white chips that skitter across the deck. You strike again, higher up, and again. Under the ice, the rope is dark and stiff and cold as iron. Your arm begins to ache. Your hands, even in mittens, begin to burn with the cold. But you are moving, and the movement warms you a little from the inside, and there is a rhythm to it, a slow beat, crack and fall, crack and fall, that sounds, out here in the silver silence, almost like music.

Benjamin works along the other side of the deck, with a wooden scraper, pushing the ice from the rail and the planks over the side, where it falls into the water with a soft hiss. Now and then he stops, and stands, and looks north.

The moon that lights your work rose a little after one o'clock this afternoon, pale and nearly invisible in the daylight sky, and it will not set until nearly four in the morning. It is a little more than three quarters full, waxing toward the full, and its light is strong enough to read large print by. It also moves the water. Twice a day, here in Casco Bay, the tide rises and falls by around nine feet, and as it turns, the Delight swings slowly round on her cable to face it, like a weathervane turning in the wind. Matthew Searle allowed for that when he chose this anchorage. He gave her room to swing, all the way round, without touching the ledges.

Then James, resting on his mallet, tips back his head and looks up at the stars, and you look up with him.

There are more of them than you have ever seen. They are so many and so bright that the sky looks dusty with them, and the long pale river of the Milky Way runs across it from one horizon to the other. James points with his mallet. There, low in the north, above the dark trees of the island, is the Pole Star, the one star that never moves, while all the others wheel slowly round it through the night. A sailor of 1720 who could find it, and measure how high it stood above the horizon, could tell how far north he was. It is low tonight, lower than it ever stood in Bristol or in London, and higher than it ever stands in Barbados. You stand side by side and look at it, saying nothing, while your breath rises up toward it in two pale clouds.

By day, a navigator would measure the sun instead. At noon, he would turn his back on it, and sight along a wooden instrument called a backstaff, which an English seaman named John Davis had invented in the 1590s, so that he could measure the height of the noon sun by its shadow without having to stare into its glare. From that one measurement, and a printed table, he could work out his latitude, his distance north of the equator, to within a few miles on a good day. John Darby has a backstaff, in a wooden case, in the stern. It is one of the most valuable things aboard.

When the shrouds are clear, James and Benjamin stand together in the shelter of the mast for a few minutes, stamping their feet, and talk in low voices.

James was born in Bridgetown, in Barbados, thirty years ago. His mother was a free woman, a seamstress, and he grew up in a little house near the careenage, the harbor where the island's ships were hauled down on their sides to be cleaned and mended. He went to sea at fourteen as a ship's boy on a sugar ship, and he has been at sea ever since. He has sailed to London, and to Boston, and to the coast of Africa, and to the islands of the Caribbean. He has seen ice before, in Boston harbor. But he has never been so cold, he says, as he has been this winter.

He asks Benjamin what he will do when he gets home.

Benjamin says that first he will walk. That the farm on the point will put him on the road south, and that if the weather holds, he can be in Falmouth in two days, and from there, he thinks, he can find a coaster bound for Salem. And when he gets to Salem, he says, he will walk up from the harbor, along the street he grew up on, to his mother's house. And he will knock on the door. And when she opens it, he does not know what he will say. He has been trying to decide since October.

James says that he will not need to say anything, and Benjamin laughs, a little shakily, and admits that this is probably true.

They stand for a while longer without talking. The moon is very bright. The ice ticks against the hull. Somewhere on the island, a branch cracks under its load of snow, and the sound comes across the water, small and clear. James says that he has a sister, still in Bridgetown, who writes to him when she can find a ship to carry the letter, and that he has not had a letter in a year. Benjamin says that he will write to her, if James likes, from Salem. James thinks about that, and then says that he would like it very much. Then they go back to the ice.

At four bells, ten o'clock, the watch changes. Matthew Searle and Owen Price come up the ladder, bundled to the eyes, and James and Benjamin go thankfully down. Benjamin's left sleeve is soaked through to the elbow, where he leaned on the wet rail, and his feet are wet, too, inside his shoes, from the melting ice on the deck. He is shivering hard. But below, in the warm dimness, the lantern is waiting for him, and the hammocks are coming down from the beams.

Matthew Searle takes the mallet from James without a word, and weighs it in his hand, and looks up at the rigging, already furring white again in places. Owen, beside him, has his cap pulled down to his eyebrows and his kerchief pulled up to his nose, so that only a narrow slit of his face shows in the moonlight, and he is hopping from foot to foot. Matthew tells him that the hopping is a waste of good warmth, and that if he wants to keep warm he should take the scraper and start on the starboard rail. Owen goes. He has a tune in his head, from earlier, and he scrapes the ice to its rhythm, and Matthew, who would never admit it, finds himself striking the shrouds in time.

Down the ladder, James stops at the bottom, under the lantern, and holds his hands up to it, the way you would hold your hands to a fire. There is almost no heat in it. He knows that. But the light on his palms is warm, and the habit is older than he is.

And in the hold, the horn lantern glows on, small and golden, while above it, on deck, the ice begins to form again, very quietly, on the ropes that have just been cleared.

## Part Nine — Around Ten O'Clock: The Hammocks Come Down, and Benjamin Tells His Story

By ten o'clock, the hammocks are coming down from the beams, one by one, and the hold of the Delight begins to look like a different room. Rolled canvas bundles are untied and shaken out. Ropes are hooked to beams. And within a few minutes, the open space around the lantern has filled with long pale shapes, hanging in rows, swaying very gently with the ship.

Could a man really be made a pirate against his will?

He could, and it happened often. Pirate crews were always short of men, and short above all of men with particular skills. When they took a ship, they would look over her crew with a careful eye. A carpenter was precious, because a ship at sea, far from any dockyard, lived or died by her carpenter. So was a cooper, who made and mended the casks that held all the water and the food. So was a surgeon, or a navigator who could work out a ship's position, or a musician. Ordinary seamen, too, were sometimes taken, when a crew was badly short of hands. Many went willingly, glad to escape a hard captain. But others were taken against their will, and kept, and made to work, and were known among the pirates themselves as forced men.

A forced man lived a strange double life. He worked beside the pirates, ate with them, and sometimes came to like them. But he had not signed their articles, he did not share in their plunder, and he was watched. And if the ship was ever taken by the authorities, he would stand in the same dock as the rest, and have to prove, somehow, that he had never wished to be there.

Some forced men did what they could to prepare for that day. When a pirate crew captured another ship, a forced man might quietly ask her captain to remember his name, and to bear witness, if he ever reached port, that he had been held against his will. Some even asked for notices to be put in the newspapers of Boston, saying so. A forced man's whole future might hang on a few words from a stranger.

Here is a real case, from these very waters.

Do you remember Thomas Davis, the young carpenter who was one of the only two men from the Whydah to come ashore alive on Cape Cod, in April 1717? He had not chosen to be there. He had been the carpenter of a merchant ship that Bellamy's crew had taken in the Caribbean the winter before, and they had kept him, against his will, while letting others go. Later that year, in October 1717, he was tried for piracy in Boston, with several others, and in 1718 the proceedings of the trial were printed there as a little book, called The Trials of Eight Persons Indited for Piracy. In that account, a witness explained why the pirates had insisted on keeping Davis. It was because he was, in the words of the printed record, "a Carpenter & a Single Man." No family to mourn him, and a trade that they needed. The court believed that he had been forced. Thomas Davis was one of two men found not guilty, and he walked free.

A few years after our night, another young man from these coasts left an even fuller account. His name was Philip Ashton, and he was a fisherman from Marblehead, the same town as Nathaniel Cobb. In June 1722, he was taken, with many other Marblehead fishermen, in a harbor on the coast of Nova Scotia, by the crew of a pirate named Edward Low. Ashton refused, again and again, to sign the pirates' articles. He was held for the better part of a year. And at last, when the pirate ship stopped to take on water at an island off the coast of Central America, he slipped away into the forest and hid until the ship had gone. He lived alone on that island for many months, in great hardship, before he was rescued. In 1725, when he finally came home to Marblehead, the town's minister, John Barnard, wrote down his story, and published it in Boston, under the title Ashton's Memorial.

Benjamin Gale, of course, has never heard of Philip Ashton. Ashton's story lies more than two years in the future. But if he could hear it, he would understand it better than almost anyone.

Historians do not agree on how many of the men who claimed, at their trials, to have been forced, had really been forced. Some argue that the claim was simply the best defense a captured pirate had, that many willing pirates used it, and that courts knew this well and believed it only with strong evidence. Others point out that the accounts of the pirates' own captives, men with nothing to gain, describe exactly how skilled men were picked out and kept, and argue that forced men made up a real and sizable part of many crews. Both are surely right about some men. The difficulty, then as now, was telling which.

Now, before Benjamin begins his own story, sling your hammock, and climb in.

It is a long rectangle of heavy canvas, about six feet long and a little less than three feet wide, rolled up tight and tied with a cord. You untie it and shake it out. At each end, the canvas is gathered into a fan of thin ropes, called clews, which meet at a single iron ring, and from each ring runs a short length of rope. You reach up and tie one end to a hook in the beam above your head, and the other end to the next beam along, and the canvas hangs between them in a deep, sagging curve, like the inside of a pea pod. Inside it, folded, is a thin blanket of coarse brown wool.

Climbing in is a matter of trust. You take hold of the edge of the hammock with both hands, and hop, and swing your legs up and in, all at once, and for a terrible moment the whole thing swings away from you and tilts, and you are sure you will be pitched out onto the planks. And then, quite suddenly, you are lying in it, and it has wrapped itself around you, and it is still.

The canvas closes over you on both sides, like the walls of a narrow cradle. The cold air of the hold is shut out above you, and you are lying in a long, close pocket of your own warmth. Above you, just a foot or two away, is the dark underside of the deck, and the line of a beam, and the faint gold light of the lantern running along it. Beside you, on both sides, other hammocks hang, so close that when the ship moves, they all move together, and you can feel the gentle bump of the man next to you through the canvas. The whole row sways as one, slowly, like seaweed in a tide.

On the Delight, with only nine men, there is room to spare, and every hammock has a little space around it. On the great warships of the 1700s, it was very different. There, hundreds of men slept on a single deck, and a seaman was often allowed only about fourteen inches of width for his hammock, little more than the width of his shoulders. They hung so close that a whole deck of sleeping men would sway together like one creature. That was bearable only because, at any one time, half the crew were usually on watch, so that every other hammock hung empty.

You pull the blanket up to your chin. It is rough, and smells of wool and salt, and it is warm. The hammock rocks. Your shoulders loosen. Your feet, at last, begin to thaw.

Now Benjamin tells his story. The others have heard parts of it, but never all of it, and tonight, his last night aboard, he tells it from the beginning, in a low voice, from his hammock, into the lantern light.

He tells how the Salem ketch he sailed on, the Swallow, sixty tons, left Salem in September with a hold full of dried cod for Barbados, the way so many New England ships did every autumn, carrying the fish of the cold northern banks to feed the people of the sugar islands. He tells how, late in October, in the Bahama channel, a fast cedar sloop came up behind them out of a rain squall, flying no colors at all, and how the master of the Swallow, a careful old man, looked at her for a long time and then quietly told his crew to do nothing foolish.

He tells how the men of the sloop came aboard, seven of them, armed, and how frightened he was, and how they were not what he expected. How a tall, quiet man in a borrowed blue coat asked the master, politely, what he carried, and took only some of the fish, and the spare sails, and a cask of water, and paid for none of it. And how the quartermaster, a broad, slow man with a Marblehead voice, looked at the five men of the Swallow's crew, and asked which of them could hand and reef and steer, and how Benjamin, who could do all three very well, had foolishly stepped forward.

He tells how he cried that first night, in the hold of the Delight, very quietly, so that nobody would hear.

Somebody heard. It was Nathaniel. He did not say anything. But the next morning, he came and found Benjamin at the pump, and told him that the company would not keep him a day longer than it had to, and that when they came to a place where a man could safely be put ashore, near his own country, the matter would be put to the vote. Benjamin had not believed him. He had heard stories, all his life, about pirates.

The men in the hammocks listen in silence. The lantern hisses softly. The hull creaks. Somewhere, a hammock rope groans against its hook, and is quiet. Nobody laughs. One or two of them, perhaps, are remembering their own first nights at sea, long ago, as frightened boys on hard ships, crying quietly into their own blankets.

Then Owen, from the hammock nearest the ladder, asks Benjamin what he will tell his mother about them.

Benjamin thinks about it for a long time. Then he says that he will tell her that they were thieves, which is true, and that they fed him as well as they fed themselves, which is also true, and that they kept their word. And that she will not believe the last part. And that he will tell her anyway.

Nathaniel Cobb grunts, from somewhere in the shadows, and says that will do. Matthew Searle, from the far end of the row, says that it will do very well. And Daniel Royce says nothing at all, but you can hear, in the dark, that he is smiling.

While the hold grows quiet, John Darby does one last piece of work for the day. He brings a small writing box from the stern, and sits on a sea chest beneath the lantern, the only place on the ship where there is light enough to write, and opens it on his knees. Inside are a few sheets of paper, a quill, a little stoppered bottle of ink, and a stick of red sealing wax. The ink is thick with cold, and he has to warm the bottle in his hands for a while before it will flow.

Then he writes, slowly, in his neat slanting hand. You can hear the scratch of the quill in the silence. He writes that the bearer, Benjamin Gale of Salem, mariner, was taken by force from the ketch Swallow in October last, and kept against his will, and never signed any articles, nor took any share, and that he behaved himself at all times as an honest man. He does not sign his own name. He signs it, simply, from the company of the sloop Delight. Then he reads it through, and blows on it to dry the ink, and folds it in three, and holds the stick of red wax against the hot tin crown of the lantern, where the candle's heat gathers, until the tip grows soft and glossy, and presses a blob of it onto the fold to seal it.

He reaches up and puts the folded letter into Benjamin's hand, in his hammock. Benjamin holds it on his chest, under both hands, as if it might blow away. He does not open it. He does not need to. He heard every single word of it being written, in the quiet.

You watch all this from your own hammock, only a few feet away, swaying gently in the half dark. You can see the captain's face, lit from below, thin and tired and kind. You can see the small red seal, catching the light. And you can see Benjamin's eyes, wide open, staring up at the beam above him, not sleepy at all.

At the foot of the ladder, the lantern burns on, its soft gold light falling across the long row of swaying hammocks, and on the faces of the men in them, some already closing their eyes.

## Part Ten — Toward Midnight: A New Candle, and a Story About a Man on an Island

By eleven o'clock, the hold of the Delight is very quiet. Most of the hammocks are still. Somewhere in the row, someone is breathing slowly and deeply, almost a snore. And at the foot of the ladder, the candle in the horn lantern has burned down to a short, thick stub, and its light has begun, very slightly, to dim.

What happens when the only light on the ship begins to burn out?

The answer is that it is never allowed to. Long before the candle gutters, someone replaces it, and he does it so that the hold is never dark for even a moment. On the Delight, the candles are kept in a small wooden box with a lid, which lives in the quartermaster's locker in the stern, because candles, like rum and powder, are stores, and stores are shared out by the quartermaster. There are not many left. Each one is counted.

So, a little before midnight, Henry Ash climbs out of his hammock with a grunt, and limps aft, and comes back with a fresh candle in his hand, long and pale and smooth. He opens the little door of the lantern. He holds the wick of the new candle to the flame of the old one, and waits, patiently, until it catches, and burns with its own small flame. Only then does he lift out the old stub, and press the new candle firmly into the socket, and close the door again.

For a moment, while he does it, there are two flames in his hands, side by side. Then there is one again, taller and brighter than before.

Henry blows out the old stub, and saves it in his pocket. A thin curl of smoke rises from it, and drifts up between the hammocks, and is gone, and the smell of warm tallow lingers for a moment in the cold air, homely as a farmhouse kitchen on a winter evening. A candle-end can always be melted down for something. Nothing on this ship is wasted. Not a candle-end, not a crust, not a scrap of rope.

Now lie still in your hammock, and watch the light change.

You are lying on your back, with the blanket up to your chin and the canvas walls of the hammock rising on both sides of you, so that you see the hold through a long, narrow opening, like looking out of a cradle. The light has been growing dimmer for an hour, so slowly that you did not notice, and the shadows in the hold had grown long and soft. Then, quite suddenly, there is a small bright flare, and the beams overhead jump back into view, and you can see the curve of the hull again, and the swaying shapes of the other hammocks, and the gleam of a brass buckle on someone's shoe on the floor below. The new flame steadies. The light settles. And the hold looks just as it did at sunset, warm and gold and safe.

You hear Henry's slow, rolling step go back to his hammock. You hear the creak of the ropes as he climbs in. You hear him sigh, a long, comfortable sigh, the sigh of an old man getting off his bad knee. And then you hear only the ship.

In the hammock next to Benjamin's, Nathaniel Cobb is not asleep. In a little while, he will have the middle watch, from midnight until four, with Thomas Wills. And so, to pass the time, and because Benjamin is not asleep either, he is telling a story, very softly, so as not to wake the others. It is a story that every sailor in the Atlantic seemed to know in these years. It is the story of the man on the island.

His name was Alexander Selkirk, and he was a Scottish sailor, from a little fishing town on the coast of Fife. In 1704, he was the sailing master of a privateer called the Cinque Ports, in the far South Pacific, off the coast of Chile. The ship was old and leaking, and he quarreled with her captain, and he declared that he would rather be put ashore on the next island they came to than sail another mile in her. The captain took him at his word. Selkirk was put ashore on a lonely, mountainous island called Juan Fernández, hundreds of miles off the coast, with his sea chest, a musket, a little powder, some tools, and a Bible. As the boat rowed away, the story goes, he changed his mind, and called after it. But it did not come back.

He lived on that island alone for more than four years.

Then, at the very beginning of February 1709, two English privateer ships, commanded by a Bristol captain named Woodes Rogers, came to the island for water, and saw a fire burning on the shore. Rogers sent a boat in to find out who had lit it. And in his book about the voyage, A Cruising Voyage Round the World, published in London in 1712, Rogers described what the boat brought back. It was, he wrote, "a Man cloth'd in Goat-Skins, who look'd wilder than the first Owners of them."

Selkirk had lived, Rogers wrote, by catching the wild goats of the island, which he could outrun on foot. He had built himself two huts, and lined them with goatskins. He had tamed the wild cats of the island to keep the rats away from him at night, and he had tamed some of the young goats, too, and to pass the time he would sing and dance with them and with his cats. He read his Bible aloud, and prayed, every day. When he came aboard Rogers' ship, he could at first barely talk, and had to search for his words.

Rogers took Selkirk aboard, and found him so able a seaman that he soon made him a mate of one of his ships. And there was one more strange turn to it. The pilot of Rogers' expedition was the famous old navigator William Dampier, who had led the earlier expedition in which the Cinque Ports had sailed, years before, and who recognized Selkirk at once, and vouched for him as the best man aboard her. Selkirk sailed on with Rogers right round the world, and came home to Britain at last in 1711, after eight years away, a minor celebrity, with a share of the prize money in his pocket.

Nathaniel tells all this very slowly, in his low Marblehead voice, and Benjamin listens with his eyes half closed, and so do you.

Here is the part of the story that touches our night most closely. In London, on the twenty-fifth of April, 1719, less than a year ago, a new book was published, with a very long title. It was called The Life and Strange Surprizing Adventures of Robinson Crusoe, of York, Mariner, and it told the story of a sailor shipwrecked alone on an island for twenty-eight years. Its author, though his name was not on it, was Daniel Defoe. The book was an immediate success, and went through edition after edition within months. And among the trunks of the passengers on the Boston brig that the Delight met in November, there was a copy, which John Darby kept for himself, and which he has read twice.

John Darby, in his own hammock near the stern bulkhead, has the book with him now, as he often does at night. He does not read it. There is not light enough at that distance from the lantern. He only holds it, closed, on his chest, a small thick volume in brown calf, its corners already soft with handling. He knows parts of it nearly by heart. His favorite part is the one where the shipwrecked Crusoe, afraid of losing track of the days, sets up a great wooden post on the shore, and cuts a notch in it every day with his knife, with a longer notch for every Sunday, and a longer one still for the first day of every month. A calendar made of wood. John thinks about that, sometimes, on long night watches, and about the ship's glass, and the bell, and the traverse board with its little pegs, and how men will always find some way to count the days, wherever they are.

Now let your eyes close for a little, in your hammock, and just listen.

The ship is talking to herself, very softly, in the dark. There is the slow creak of the hammock ropes against the beams, rising and falling with the swell. There is the faint, wet slap of the water against the hull, just beyond the planks, so close that you could almost reach out and touch it. There is the tick, tick, tick of small pieces of ice drifting along the side, and the deep, occasional groan of the anchor cable as the ship swings. Someone turns over, and his hammock sighs. Someone murmurs a word in his sleep, and is silent. And under it all is the long, low hush of the sea itself, breathing in and out, in and out, on the rocks of the island.

You are warm in your blanket. The canvas holds you. The light on your closed eyelids is faint and gold. Your breathing slows to match the swell. In, and out. Up, and down. There is nowhere you need to be, and nothing you need to do, except lie here, and listen, and rest.

Historians do not agree on how much Robinson Crusoe owes to Alexander Selkirk. Many have long believed that Selkirk's story, as told by Woodes Rogers and others, was Defoe's main inspiration, and some have even suggested that the two men met in Bristol, although there is no firm evidence that they did. Others point out that Crusoe's island lies in the Caribbean, not the Pacific, that he is shipwrecked rather than put ashore by choice, and that several other castaway stories were in print in these years, any of which Defoe may have known. Defoe himself never said.

Benjamin, at the end of the story, asks Nathaniel whether Selkirk was glad to be rescued.

Nathaniel thinks about it for a long time, swaying gently in his hammock. Then he says that he thinks Selkirk was glad, and that he was also, in some way, sorry. That a man can grow used to almost anything, even an island. And that going home is not always as simple as it sounds, after a long time away.

Benjamin is quiet for a while. Then he says, very softly, that he has only been away since September.

Nathaniel laughs, almost silently, and says that this is true, and that Benjamin will be home long before he has learned to dance with goats.

Benjamin laughs, too, under his blanket. It is the first time anyone aboard has heard him laugh like that since October. Nathaniel hears it, and lies very still, and says nothing more, and is glad. And for a little while the two New Englanders lie side by side in the soft gold light, swaying together, listening to the ship.

Up on deck, the glass runs out, and a moment later the bell rings eight times, slow and soft. Midnight. The first watch is over, and the middle watch, the longest and coldest and quietest watch of the night, begins.

Nathaniel climbs out of his hammock and pulls on his tarred coat. Thomas Wills, in the next hammock, is already sitting up, rubbing his eyes, his leather apron still tied round his waist, as it always is. Together they climb the ladder, and the hatch opens, and a breath of freezing air pours down into the hold, and the lantern flame bows low and then stands up again.

A moment later, two other men come down. It is Matthew Searle and Owen Price, and they are soaked. The mist that rose off the water in the night has settled on everything, and frozen, and then, where their bodies warmed it, melted again, and their coats and caps are glistening with wet, and their mittens are sodden, and the knees of their breeches are dark with water from kneeling on the iced deck. Owen's teeth are chattering so hard that you can hear them across the hold. Matthew's beard is white with frost.

They stand under the lantern for a moment, dripping, and look at their hammocks.

Matthew pulls off his mittens, and wrings them out, one after the other, and a little stream of icy water patters onto the planks between his boots. Owen tries to do the same, but his fingers are too numb to grip, and Henry Ash, who was not asleep after all, leans out of his hammock and takes the mittens from him, and wrings them himself, and hangs them on the line. Then he looks Owen up and down in the lantern light, the way a mother looks at a child who has come in from the rain, and shakes his head.

There are no dry clothes left on the ship. Everything spare is hanging, wet, on the line between the beams. And it is midnight, and it is January, and they are wet to the skin, and they must sleep.

And above them, the new candle burns on, tall and clear inside its horn walls, lighting their wet faces with a warm and steady gold.

## Part Eleven — A Little After Midnight: Wet Wool and Warm Canvas

A little after midnight, Matthew Searle and Owen Price stand dripping under the lantern, and the whole sleeping hold seems to wait, as if it, too, were wondering what they will do.

Why is a man in wet wool so much better off than a man in wet linen?

Because wool, even soaked, does not give up its warmth. Every fiber of a sheep's fleece is crimped and curled, and springy, and a woven or knitted wool cloth is full of tiny pockets of air, caught between the fibers. When wool gets wet, much of the water is drawn inside the fibers themselves, rather than filling those air pockets, and so the cloth stays springy, and keeps much of its trapped air, and goes on holding warmth against the skin. Wool even gives off a little warmth of its own as it soaks up damp from the air, a small, real effect that anyone who has pulled on a damp wool jersey on a cold morning may have felt. Linen and cotton do none of this. Wet, they lie flat and cold against the body, and draw the heat out of it.

Sailors did not know about fibers. But they knew wool. And they knew what to do on a night like this.

Matthew does it first, without a word, and Owen copies him.

Off come the tarred coats, stiff and streaming, and they are hung on the hooks by the ladder, where the water runs off them onto the planks. That outer shell has done its work. It kept the worst of the wet away, and now it would only hold the cold against them. Off come the sodden mittens, and the caps, wrung out and hung up. Off come the shoes, and the soaked outer stockings, and they are wrung, too.

But the wool underneath stays on. The waistcoats, the jackets, the inner stockings. They are damp, and cold to the touch, but they are wool, and they will warm.

And then something small happens, which nobody planned. Benjamin Gale climbs out of his hammock, in his shirt, and goes to his sea chest, and takes out the new gray stockings that Nathaniel gave him at seven o'clock, still dry and thick and folded. He hands them to Owen. He says that he only needs them for the walk, and the walk is not until tomorrow, and that Owen can give them back in the morning, warm. Owen tries to say no, and his teeth are chattering too hard to say it, and so he takes them, and pulls them on, and his whole face changes.

Then they climb into their hammocks.

And here is the second part of the old trick. A hammock is not just a bed. It is a pocket. When a man climbs into it, the canvas closes up around him, on both sides, and shuts out the drafts of the hold. He pulls the blanket over himself, and the blanket and the canvas together make a small, close space, hardly bigger than his body. And then his body does the rest. All night, it pours out its warmth into that small space, and the warmth stays there, held by the canvas and the wool, and the damp clothes against his skin slowly warm, and then, very slowly, begin to dry. Sailors said that a man could dry his clothes by sleeping in them. On a cold night, it took hours, and it was not comfortable at first. But by morning, the wool next to his skin would be warm, and often nearly dry.

The hold itself helps too. A ship's hold full of sleeping men is never as cold as the deck above it. Every man in his hammock is warming the air around him. The deck overhead keeps out the wind. And the sea outside, cold as it is, never falls far below freezing, while the air above it, on a night like this one, falls far lower. A sleeping hold is a warm, close, damp, breathing space, crowded and smelly, and, compared with the deck, very nearly snug.

The bed that made all this possible had come from a long way off.

When Christopher Columbus sailed among the islands of the Bahamas in October 1492, he saw the Taíno people there sleeping in nets of woven cotton, slung between two posts. His journal of that voyage survives only in a summary made by the friar Bartolomé de las Casas, but that summary describes the people coming out to his ships in their canoes to trade cotton, and the nets in which they slept, which they called hamacas. It is the earliest known appearance of that word in European writing. And it is the word we still use, more than two hundred years later, aboard the Delight. A little more than a century after Columbus, in 1597, the English navy ordered canvas to make what it called hanging cabbons, or beds, for its seamen, and from then on, the hammock became the usual bed of sailors on English ships.

Historians do not agree on exactly when English sailors first began to sleep in hammocks. Some think the order of 1597 marks the real beginning. Others think sailors had been slinging nets and canvas of their own between the beams for years before any official order, and that the navy simply wrote down what its men had already learned. Either way, by 1720, a seaman on a ship like this one would hardly have known any other way to sleep.

So that is the answer to our third question. Men slept warm enough in wet clothes at sea in winter because they took off the wet outer layers and kept on the wool, which holds its warmth even when damp; because the hammock wrapped them in canvas and shut out the drafts; and because their own bodies, held close in that small space all night, slowly warmed and dried the wool against their skin, in a hold made warm by the breathing of their sleeping shipmates.

And that is exactly what is happening now, to Owen and to Matthew, and to you.

You are lying in your own hammock, and you feel it. Your clothes were a little damp, too, from the deck. But they are not cold any more. They have taken on the warmth of your body, slowly, without your noticing, the way a stone in the sun takes on warmth. Your feet are warm in their wool. Your hands are warm, tucked under your arms. The blanket is heavy and soft on your chest, and the canvas holds you on both sides, like two cupped hands.

The ship rocks you. Very gently. Up, and down. Up, and down.

In the next hammock, Owen's teeth have stopped chattering. He is lying curled on his side, with his knees drawn up, in Benjamin's stockings, with his Monmouth cap pulled down over his ears. His breathing is slowing. His fiddle hangs in its canvas bag from a nail on the beam above his head, where he can reach it, swaying very slightly with the ship. In a few minutes, Owen is asleep.

Matthew Searle lies on his back, with his arms folded across his chest, the way he always sleeps, like a stone knight on a tomb. His frosted beard has melted, and dripped, and is drying. He sighs once, deeply. And then he, too, is asleep, and a low, gentle snore rises from his hammock, like the purring of a large, contented cat.

Henry Ash is asleep, his stiff leg propped on a folded jacket, and James Gold's red scarf still lying over his knee.

James is asleep, very quietly, the way he does everything, his long hands folded under his cheek.

Daniel Royce is asleep, near the bulkhead that guards his powder room, as close to it as he can get, like a dog sleeping across a doorway.

John Darby is asleep, with the small brown book still lying closed on his chest, rising and falling with his breath.

Only Benjamin is still awake.

He lies in his hammock with the captain's letter tucked inside his shirt, against his skin, where he can feel the crackle of the paper when he breathes. He is looking at the lantern. He has been looking at it, on and off, for three months, every night, from this same hammock. It has been the last thing he saw before he slept, through all the warm nights in the islands and the cold nights off Carolina, and the long nights of the gale. And tonight, he realizes, is the last time he will see it.

He finds that he is not glad about that. Not entirely. He is surprised by it.

He watches the small gold flame, standing straight and still inside its horn walls. He watches the soft light it throws on the beams, and on the curve of the hull, and on the sleeping faces. And slowly, very slowly, his eyes begin to close.

Let your own eyes close now, too, if they have not already.

There is nothing more to do tonight. The ice has been cleared. The anchor is holding. The vote has been taken, and the letter has been written, and sealed, and given. Tomorrow is the Sabbath, and the fiddler may rest, and so may you.

Feel how heavy the blanket is. Feel how the canvas curves around you, close and safe, on both sides. Feel the slow sway of the hammock, up, and down, and up, as the ship breathes on the swell. Your clothes are warm now, all the way through. Your feet are warm. Your hands are warm. The cold is out there, beyond the planks, on the deck and on the dark water and on the snowy island. But it cannot reach you here.

Up on deck, the night has grown even stiller. The moon has moved westward across the sky, and hangs now above the dark spruce trees of the island, a little lower than before, and its light lies softer on the bay. The mist has settled. The water is like black glass. On the point to the north, the farmhouse window that glowed orange at dusk has long since gone dark. The people there are asleep, too, under their quilts, with their fire banked low on the hearth.

Nathaniel Cobb stands at the bow, with his hands deep in his pockets and his chin sunk in his kerchief, and looks south, along the coast, toward the town where he was born. He does not think about the sea. He thinks about his mother's kitchen, and the smell of bread, and the sound of the church bell on a Sunday morning, carrying across the harbor. He thinks that he might write to her, from the islands, when there is a ship to carry the letter. He thinks that he will.

Thomas Wills walks slowly aft, along the frosted deck, and stops by the mast, and lays his hand flat on the cracked topmast above his head, the way a man lays his hand on the neck of a tired horse. Tomorrow, he tells it, silently. Tomorrow I will mend you.

Then he goes back to the bow, and stands beside Nathaniel, and the two of them watch the moon on the water, saying nothing at all, for a long time.

Below, in the warm dark of the hold, the hammocks hang still in their row.

The lantern light is soft on them. It touches the edge of a blanket here, the curve of a cheek there, a hand hanging loose over the side of a hammock, the brown neck of the fiddle in its bag. The line of wet stockings drips more slowly now, one drop, and then a long while, and then another. The air is close and warm and quiet. It smells of wool, and tallow, and tar, and sleep.

You breathe it in. You breathe it out. You let it go.

Somewhere along the row, a man murmurs in his sleep, a word or two, too soft to catch, and then sighs, and is quiet again. A hammock rope creaks, and settles. The lantern flame does not even flicker. Everything in the hold is resting now, the men, the ship, the wet coats on their hooks, the fiddle in its bag. Even the cold seems to be resting, out there beyond the planks.

Benjamin, at the very edge of sleep, is thinking about tomorrow. Not with fear any more. Only gently, the way you might think about a road you know well.

He thinks of the boat pulling in to the shore below the farm, the oars dipping quietly in the still water. He thinks of stepping out onto the snowy rocks, in his new blue coat, with his bag over his shoulder. He thinks of the path up through the spruce trees, and the smell of wood smoke from the farmhouse chimney, and a dog barking somewhere, and a door opening, and a woman's voice asking who is there. He thinks of the long road south, white and quiet under the snow, and the sound of his own boots on it, every step taking him nearer home.

And then he thinks of Salem, and the harbor, and the street, and his mother's door.

He is smiling, a little, in the dark. His hand is resting on the letter under his shirt. His breathing has grown slow and even. And at last, without quite knowing when it happens, Benjamin Gale is asleep.

Above, on deck, Nathaniel Cobb and Thomas Wills walk slowly up and down in the moonlight, and the bell rings softly, once, and then twice. All is well. The anchor holds. The night is very still.

One bell. Two bells. The sand runs down in the glass, grain by grain, and is turned, and runs again. The hours of the middle watch pass slowly and softly, the way hours pass in a house where everyone is asleep. Nothing happens. Nothing needs to happen. The ship rests on the dark water, and the island rests beside her, and the stars turn slowly overhead, the way they have turned on every winter night since there were ships, and islands, and men to look up at them.

And below them, in the hold, the horn lantern burns on, small and steady, keeping its quiet watch over seven sleeping men.

## Part Twelve — Around Four in the Morning: The Small Hours, and the First Gray Light

At four o'clock, eight bells ring softly across the bay, and the middle watch is over. The moon has just gone down behind the dark trees of the island, and the night is at its deepest and its stillest.

Down the ladder, very quietly, come Nathaniel Cobb and Thomas Wills, and up the ladder, very quietly, go John Darby and Daniel Royce, for the morning watch. Nobody speaks above a whisper. Nobody needs to. They pass each other at the foot of the ladder, under the lantern, and Nathaniel touches the captain's arm, once, and nods. All is well.

Nathaniel and Thomas shrug off their tarred coats and hang them on the hooks. They are not wet. The night has been dry and still, and the frost on their shoulders is only a fine white dust, which they brush away. They climb into their hammocks, which are cold for a moment, and then, quite quickly, warm. Thomas is asleep almost at once, his leather apron still tied round his waist. Nathaniel lies awake a little longer, looking at Benjamin's sleeping face in the next hammock, and then at the lantern. Then he, too, closes his eyes.

You are lying in your own hammock, warm and heavy, half asleep and half awake, in that soft gray country between the two. You hear the quiet steps go up the ladder and across the deck overhead. You hear the hatch close softly. You hear the creak of the ropes as the two men climb into their hammocks, and their long sighs, and then their slow breathing, joining all the others. And under it all, the ship, rocking, rocking, very gently, on the swell.

Henry Ash stirs in his hammock, as old men do in the small hours. He does not quite wake. But his lips move, and he murmurs something very low, that only you, lying close beside him, can hear. They are words he learned as a boy, in a village church, long ago, and he has said them to himself on many night watches since, in many seas. They come from the hundred and seventh Psalm, in the words of the King James Bible, printed in 1611.

"They that go down to the sea in ships, that do business in great waters; these see the works of the Lord, and his wonders in the deep."

His voice drifts, and stops, and starts again, softer still. He has skipped ahead, the way you do with words you know by heart.

"Then are they glad because they be quiet; so he bringeth them unto their desired haven."

And then he sighs, and is silent, and sleeps on.

The hold is very quiet now. You lie and listen to it.

There is the breathing of the sleepers, all around you in the dark, slow and deep and unhurried, each man with his own small rhythm, so that together they sound like the sea itself, rising and falling. There is the faint creak of the hammock ropes, as the row sways, very slightly, all together. There is the soft tick of the cooling hull, and the drip, much slower now, of the last of the water from the stockings on the line. There is the tiny, steady hiss of the candle in its horn case.

The candle that Henry set in the lantern at midnight is still tall. It has burned down only a little way, and it will see them easily through to morning. Its flame stands straight and still, because there is no draft at all in the hold now, and the soft gold light it gives lies over everything like a blanket. Over the beams, and the barrels, and the hammocks, and the sleeping faces, and over you.

You do not need to keep your eyes open to see it. You can feel it there, warm and faint, on your closed eyelids. You let your hands go loose. You let your jaw go soft. You let the hammock hold the whole weight of you, and it does, easily, the way the sea holds up the ship.

The Delight herself seems to be asleep. Her cedar planks, cut long ago on a warm island far to the south, creak now and then, gently, as if she were murmuring in a dream of blue water and hot sun. Her mast stands quiet against the stars. Her cable holds. She has carried these men a long way, through a long gale, and tonight, in this still, sheltered place, she can rest, too.

Up on deck, John Darby and Daniel Royce sit side by side on the edge of the hatch, wrapped in blankets over their coats, with their backs against the mast. The night is so still that the bay is like black glass, and the stars are reflected in it, every one, so that the Delight seems to float in the middle of the sky. The anchor is deep in the mud. The cable hangs straight and quiet from the bow. And through the open hatch, between their knees, the warm gold glow of the lantern rises up from below and lies softly on their faces.

They talk, for a while, in low voices. About the topmast, and the water casks, and the wind they hope for, out of the northwest, to carry them south. About the warm islands, and the color of the sea there, so blue that it seems lit from underneath. About nothing very much. Daniel says that when they reach the islands, he means to sit in the shade of a tree for a whole day, and do nothing at all, and eat a mango. John says that is the best plan he has heard all winter. They agree that it should be put to the company, and both of them laugh, very quietly, so as not to wake the men below.

And then they talk less. And then they stop talking.

Daniel's chin sinks slowly onto his chest. John's head tips back against the mast, and rests there. They are not quite asleep, the way sailors on a quiet anchor watch are never quite asleep, with one ear always listening for the cable. But they are very nearly asleep. And the night is so still, and so safe, that it lets them.

The bell hangs silent. The glass runs quietly in its frame. The sea breathes against the rocks of the island, in, and out, in, and out, very softly, like someone sleeping in the next room.

And you, down in the hold, are drifting too. Your thoughts have grown slow and soft and far apart, like the bells. You think of the fiddle, and the tune from Wales, and the dried apples, and the taste of hot pease on a cold night. You think of eight hands rising in the lantern light. You think of the letter with its small red seal. And then you do not think of anything very much, only the warmth, and the sway, and the gold light, and the sound of the sea against the island, breathing, breathing, a long way off.

Now the hours pass, and you let them.

Five o'clock. The stars begin, very slowly, to fade at the edges of the sky. The air is utterly still. Not a breath of wind touches the water. A single gull drifts past, high and silent, a pale shape against the dark, and is gone toward the open sea. The sand in the glass runs down, and nobody turns it for a little while, and it does not matter at all.

Six o'clock. In the east, over the open sea, a line appears along the horizon, so faint that at first you think you have imagined it. It is not light. It is only a little less dark. A deep, cold blue, and then, above it, a gray, very pale, as if someone had breathed on a dark window.

The gray grows. It spreads slowly upward into the sky, and the stars above it go out, one by one, quietly, the way candles go out at the end of an evening. The island turns from black to a deep, soft green. The snow along the shore turns from gray to white. The ice on the rigging of the Delight begins to glimmer, faintly, like frost on a windowpane in the first light of morning. And the water of the bay, still as glass, turns from black to pewter, and from pewter to the palest silver.

Then, very slowly, a little color comes. A thin wash of rose along the horizon, under the gray, and then a faint gold above the rose, so soft that it is hardly a color at all. The underside of a long, low cloud far out to sea turns pink, as if it were blushing. The snow on the island catches the pink, too, very faintly, and holds it. And the whole quiet bay lies open to the sky, waiting for the sun, which has not yet risen, and will not rise for a while yet.

On the point to the north, a thin line of smoke begins to rise from the farmhouse chimney. Someone there is awake, and has stirred the fire. The smoke goes straight up, without bending, a thin gray thread against the pale sky, because there is not a breath of wind to bend it. It is a good sign. It is the sign of a calm, clear day.

On deck, John Darby opens his eyes, and sees it, and watches it for a long time, and does not move. Then he closes his eyes again, for just a little while longer. There is no hurry. Not this morning. The Sabbath has come, and the sea is calm, and the anchor holds.

In a little while, the whole ship will wake. Henry Ash will climb out of his hammock, rubbing his knee, and go up on deck, and light the fire in its box of sand, and put the kettle on. The smell of wood smoke will drift down the hatch. Thomas Wills will look up at the cracked topmast, and rub his hands together, and fetch his tools. Nathaniel Cobb will make ready the boat. And Benjamin Gale will wake, and feel for the letter in his shirt, and remember what day it is, and lie very still for a moment, and smile.

But not yet. Not yet.

Today will be a gentle day. It is the Sabbath, and the articles say that the fiddler may rest, and so Owen will sleep late, and wake slowly, and lie in his hammock for a while, warm in borrowed stockings, before anyone thinks to call him. There will be hot water for washing, for once, from Henry's kettle. There will be biscuit, and the last of the cod, fried in pork fat. There will be the sound of Thomas's mallet, tapping on the topmast, slow and steady across the still water. And in the afternoon, when the boat comes back from the shore without Benjamin in it, the men will be quiet for a while, and then Owen will ask James to teach him that tune from Bridgetown, and the day will go on.

For now, everyone is still asleep.

John Darby is asleep against the mast, in his borrowed blue coat, with the gray light on his face. Daniel Royce is asleep beside him, his chin on his chest, close to his beloved guns. Nathaniel Cobb is asleep in his hammock, beside the boy he has brought home, his broad hands folded on his blanket. Matthew Searle is asleep on his back, his beard dry now, snoring very softly. Henry Ash is asleep with James Gold's red scarf over his knee, and the words of the old psalm still somewhere in his dreams. James Gold is asleep, quiet and still, his hands under his cheek. Thomas Wills is asleep in his leather apron. Owen Price is asleep in his Monmouth cap and Benjamin's gray stockings, with his fiddle hanging above him, waiting for the Sabbath. And Benjamin Gale is asleep, with his captain's letter against his heart, a few miles from the road that leads home.

The gray light creeps a little further down the ladder, rung by rung. It finds the wet coats on their hooks, and the line of stockings, and the edge of the nearest hammock. It finds Owen's fiddle in its canvas bag, swaying very slightly on its nail. It finds the copy of the articles on the beam, with its columns of names and its little ink fiddle. And it finds the lantern, and stops there, as if it knew that it had come to something that deserved respect.

And you are asleep too, or very nearly, in your hammock, in the warm dark of the hold. You are safe here. You are warm. You can stay as long as you like.

The ship is quiet. The hold is warm. The night has passed safely, every hour of it.

The ice on the rigging glitters. The smoke from the farmhouse rises straight and gray. The water lies still as a mirror under the brightening sky. And on board the little cedar sloop, riding at her anchor in the lee of the island, nine men sleep on, warm in their wool, while the long winter night slips quietly away behind them.

And at the foot of the ladder, in the first gray light that steals down through the hatch, the horn lantern is still burning, small and golden and patient, just as it was when Henry Ash lit it at sunset. It has burned all night. It has watched over every man aboard. It has been the one light left burning in all that dark bay, and it is still glowing, softly, through its thin walls of horn, waiting, quietly, for the morning.

If you enjoyed spending this winter night aboard the Delight, a like or a subscription would mean a great deal, but only if you truly enjoyed it. And if there is a time or a place you would love to spend a night in, leave it in the comments, because your ideas truly help.

Next time, we will visit a very different kind of shelter, on a very different kind of night, somewhere far from the sea.

Sleep well, friend. Good night.

### Verification notes

- **Computed, not searched:** sunset 4:51 pm, civil dusk 5:26 pm, moonrise about 1:18 pm, moonset about 3:53 am, civil dawn about 6:56 am and sunrise 7:32 am, for outer Casco Bay (43.6° N, 70.1° W) on 20 January 1720 (Gregorian), in local mean solar time, computed with PyEphem; the moon about 77 percent lit and waxing. The narration ends at first gray light, before sunrise. The weekday (Saturday) and the Julian equivalent (9 January 1719/20, eleven days behind) were computed. The tide range of about nine feet in Casco Bay is from standard knowledge.
- **Verbatim, checked by search this session:** Roberts's Article IV ("The Lights and Candles to be put out at eight a-Clock at Night: If any of the Crew, after that Hour, still remained inclined for Drinking, they were to do it on the open Deck"), from secondary transcriptions of Johnson; Phillips's article phrase "lighted without a Lanthorn"; the *Whydah* bell inscription; the Boston printed account's phrase that Davis was kept as "a Carpenter & a Single Man" (from a secondary transcription); Woodes Rogers, "a Man cloth'd in Goat-Skins, who look'd wilder than the first Owners of them"; the 1651 Barbados manuscript's "a hot, hellish, and terrible liquor" (the narration attributes this to an anonymous 1651 description, not to Ligon).
- **Verbatim from standard knowledge, not freshly searched:** Roberts's Article I phrase "a vote in affairs of moment"; Shakespeare's "wearing leeks in their Monmouth caps"; Psalm 107:23–24 and 30 (King James Version).
- **Paraphrased:** the rest of Roberts's Article I (equal title to fresh provisions and liquor; voted retrenchment), Article II (a shift of clothes when boarding prizes), Article III (no gaming for money), Article IX (eight hundred dollars for a lost limb), Article X (shares) and Article XI (musicians' rest on the Sabbath); Johnson's remark that Roberts meant the lights rule as a check on drinking; Johnson's description of Roberts's crimson damask dress; Johnson's comment that pirates let a man be captain on condition they could be captain over him (wording not confirmed, so paraphrased); Johnson's account of Charles Vane deposed by vote in November 1718; Johnson's note of a crew swearing on a hatchet for want of a Bible; Snelgrave on the crew's offense when captains took clothes without the quartermaster's leave; Mather's two sheep found alive after twenty-eight days (sources differ on the poultry figures, so they are given generally); Ligon on "kill-devil"; Rogers on Selkirk's goats, cats, Bible and halting speech, and Dampier vouching for him; Las Casas's abstract on "hamacas"; the 1597 navy canvas order; Defoe's notched-post calendar.
