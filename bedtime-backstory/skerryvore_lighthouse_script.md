## Part One — Around Half Past Two in the Afternoon: A Tower at the Edge of the Sea

It is January in the year 1890, far out in the Atlantic, beyond the low green island of Tiree on the western edge of Scotland. There is no land here at all, only a scatter of black rocks that the sea covers and uncovers all day. The wind smells of salt and cold iron. The light is already thinning toward evening. And on the largest of those rocks stands a single pale tower of granite, rising straight out of the foam, and at its very top, behind a crown of glass, a lamp is waiting to be lit at sunset.

Not the haunted lighthouse of the picture books. Just one ordinary tower on one ordinary winter night.

Without a few old tricks, you would not have lasted until morning.

So tonight I would like you to carry three small questions with you. The first is this: how do three men share one tower without a fight? The second: how do you keep a flame steady through a whole winter gale? And the third: what happens when the relief boat cannot come?

These are not three separate questions, really. They are three windows in the same round wall, and we will look through them one at a time as the night goes on.

Now let your head grow heavy on the pillow. Let your shoulders loosen and drop, and let your eyes close whenever they like, because there is nothing out here you need to watch. The men in the tower will do the watching tonight. So let us begin.

The rock is called Skerryvore. The name comes from the Gaelic, the old language of these islands, and it means something like the great skerry, the big sea rock. That is a modest name for what it really is. Skerryvore is not one rock but a whole reef, a long ragged ridge of stone, most of it hidden just under the water, stretching for miles across the open sea southwest of Tiree. On a calm day you might sail right past it and see only a few dark heads of stone and a scribble of white water. On a rough day the whole reef breaks into foam at once, like a field of snow that will not lie still.

For centuries, that reef was one of the most feared places on the western coast of Scotland. Ships coming around the north of Ireland and up toward the Clyde, or out of the Clyde toward America, passed close by it. In the dark or in a fog, a ship could be on the rocks before her crew ever saw them. Nobody kept a full count, but people along this coast believed that the reef had wrecked a great many vessels, and they had the timber and the wreckage on their own beaches to prove it.

So now there is a tower. It stands on the one rock of the reef that is large enough to hold it, and it is the tallest lighthouse in Scotland. From the rock to the top of its lantern it rises about one hundred and fifty-six feet. That is taller than a twelve-story building, set down on a stone no bigger than a village garden, in the middle of the sea.

Here is a small question to begin with, and we will answer it before this part is over. How far are the men in that tower from the nearest house where someone else is sitting by a fire tonight?

Let us start with what every sailor on this coast knew in 1890. The tower was built between 1838 and 1844 by an engineer named Alan Stevenson, for the Commissioners of Northern Lighthouses, who looked after every lighthouse in Scotland and the Isle of Man. People usually just called them the Northern Lighthouse Board, and that is what we will call them tonight. Their offices were in Edinburgh, on George Street, a long way from this rock. But their rules reached out here as surely as the tide.

The Stevensons were a lighthouse family. Alan's father, Robert Stevenson, had built the famous Bell Rock lighthouse off the east coast of Scotland, in the first years of the century. Alan's younger brothers, David and Thomas, became lighthouse engineers too. And Thomas had a son, born in Edinburgh in 1850, who was supposed to become a lighthouse engineer as well, but who wrote stories instead. His name was Robert Louis Stevenson. We will meet him again later tonight, because he loved this tower, and he named his own house after it.

The tower is built of granite, most of it quarried on the Ross of Mull, a long finger of land to the east. It is round, and it curves gently inward as it rises, like the trunk of a great tree. At the bottom it is forty-two feet across. At the top, under the lantern, it is only sixteen. And for the first twenty-six feet above the rock, it is not hollow at all. It is solid stone, all the way through, so that the sea has nothing to grip and nothing to break into.

Here is the answer to our small question. The nearest home with a fire burning in it tonight is on Tiree, about twelve miles away across open water. Twelve miles. On a clear evening you can see the low line of that island from the top of the tower, a thin dark stroke on the horizon. Tonight it is hidden in a gray haze of spray. Out here, there is no neighbor, no road, no path, and no way to leave except by boat, and the boat only comes when the sea allows it.

And here is a surprising detail. The whole tower, all that granite, was reckoned to weigh roughly four thousand three hundred tons. Alan Stevenson made it heavy on purpose. A wave that strikes it does not push it. It simply breaks and falls away. The great weight of stone is what keeps the tower standing, and what keeps the men inside it steady while the sea goes on hammering at its foot.

When the work was finished, Alan Stevenson wrote it all down. His book, published in Edinburgh in 1848, was called Account of the Skerryvore Lighthouse, with Notes on the Illumination of Lighthouses. It is a long, careful, rather proud book, full of drawings and measurements. In it he described the reef as he first knew it, a place where the men could only land in calm weather, and where a whole summer of work might be undone in a single night of storm. He described how every block of granite was shaped on shore, numbered, carried out by boat, and lifted into place. He also described the light itself, which he cared about most of all. We will open his book again several times tonight, because almost everything inside this tower began as a line in his notebooks.

Now let us go up to the tower, just you, in the last of the afternoon.

You are standing on the rock at the foot of the wall. Under your boots the stone is dark and wet and slippery with weed, and it slopes away on every side toward the water. The sea is never still here, not even on a calm day. It heaves up against the rock, slides back, and heaves up again, and each time it comes it leaves a white lace of foam behind. The air is so full of salt that you can taste it on your lips. Above you the tower rises, pale gray granite, smooth as a candle, so tall that you have to tip your head right back to see the top. A ladder of metal rungs runs up the side of it to a small door, high above your head, well out of reach of the waves.

You put your hands on the cold rungs and you climb. The wind tugs at your coat. Gulls hang in the air beside you, watching, not bothered at all. The door at the top of the ladder is heavy and thick, made of wood and bound with metal, and when it opens and you step through it, the sound of the sea suddenly drops away. It is quieter in here than you expected. The walls are so thick that the roar becomes a low, steady hum, more felt than heard.

Inside, a narrow stair curls upward around the wall, and the air smells of coal smoke and paraffin and boiled tea and, faintly, of wet wool. From somewhere far above you, someone is whistling.

Today, if you came here, you would find no one at all. The tower was made automatic in 1994, and now the light switches itself on and off, and it is watched from Edinburgh. But tonight, in 1890, there are men here, and everything depends on them.

Historians who write about the Stevensons sometimes disagree about how much of the credit belongs to Alan alone. Some see Skerryvore as the work of one brilliant engineer, who chose the rock, shaped the tower, and designed the light. Others point out that it was also the work of his father's long experience at the Bell Rock, of dozens of masons and seamen, and of a foreman and storekeepers whose names hardly anyone remembers. Both views are fair. Alan Stevenson's name is on the book. But a great many other hands are in the stone.

The people of Tiree have an old name for their own island. They call it the land below the waves, because it is so low and flat that, from a boat out at sea, its fields and houses seem to sit beneath the crests of the swell. From the top of Skerryvore, on a clear day, that is just how it looks. A long thin line of green and gold, lower than the waves themselves, with a few white houses scattered along it like crumbs on a tablecloth.

The keepers did not live on the rock all the time. They took turns. Each man spent a spell of some weeks out here in the tower, and then a spell ashore at Hynish with his family, and then came out again, so that there were always three keepers on the rock and one at home. The boat from Hynish carried them back and forth, whenever the sea was kind enough to let it.

This family never quite existed under one name, but every detail of their night is real.

There are four men in the tower tonight, and one cat.

The Principal Keeper is Alexander Grant. He is fifty-six years old, born in the little harbor town of Portsoy on the coast of Banffshire, far away in the northeast. He went to sea as a boy on the herring boats and joined the lighthouse service at twenty-five, and he has been a keeper for thirty-one years now. He is gray-bearded and careful and slow to speak, and he keeps the station journal in a neat, slanting hand. His wife, Margaret, lives ashore on Tiree, in the row of keepers' cottages at Hynish.

The first assistant keeper is Hector MacLean, forty-one years old, born on Tiree itself, in the township of Balemartine, only a short walk from Hynish. Gaelic was his first language, and he still thinks in it, and sometimes, when he is busy with his hands, he sings in it under his breath. His wife, Catriona, and their three children live in one of those same cottages ashore. On a clear day, from the gallery at the top of the tower, he can see his own island.

The second assistant is Thomas Rennie, thirty years old, from Anstruther, a fishing town in Fife. His father built boats, and Thomas trained as a joiner before he joined the service. He is good with his hands and good with machines, and he has a dry way of saying things that makes the others laugh when they least expect it. He was supposed to go ashore today. We will come back to that.

And the fourth is Neil MacKinnon, twenty years old, from the Isle of Skye. He is not a full keeper yet. He is a learner, sent out by the Board to spend his first winter on a rock station, so that he can watch and learn from older men before he is trusted with a light of his own. He speaks Gaelic too, which is a comfort to him, and he asks more questions than anyone in the tower has heard in years.

The cat is called Bess. She is gray, with one white foot, and she belongs to nobody in particular, which means she belongs to all of them.

High above them, at the very top of the tower, inside its crown of glass, the great lamp sits cold and ready, and in two hours it will be lit.

## Part Two — Around Half Past Three: Drawing Back the Curtains

The afternoon light is going copper now over the western sea, and inside the tower Alexander Grant climbs the last turn of the stair to the lightroom, with Neil MacKinnon close behind him, carrying a can of paraffin in each hand.

Here is a small question for this part, and we will answer it before we go back down the stair. Why would a lighthouse, of all buildings in the world, need curtains?

To understand the answer, you first need to see what is up here. At the very top of the tower is the lantern, a round room made almost entirely of glass, framed in metal, with a domed roof of copper above it. The glass panes are thick and strong, set in a frame that leans slightly, so the bars cast as thin a shadow as possible. A narrow iron gallery runs all the way around the outside, with a railing, so that the keepers can step out and clean the glass from the outside in fair weather.

Inside the lantern stands the lens. It is the heart of the whole tower. If you have never seen a lighthouse lens up close, it is hard to imagine. It is taller than a man, a great round cage of glass, built up from dozens of curved rings and prisms of polished glass, all set in a frame of brass. Each piece is cut so precisely that it bends the light of a single lamp and throws it out over the sea as one strong beam. In daylight the whole thing glitters like ice on a winter morning. In the middle of it, in a hollow space, sits the lamp.

So the answer to our small question is this. A lens that can gather the light of one small flame and send it twenty miles out to sea can also gather the light of the sun. On a bright day, sunlight coming in through the lantern glass could pass through the prisms and be focused into hot, narrow points inside the lantern. It could scorch the brass, crack fittings, and even set things smoldering. So every morning, after the lamp was put out, the keepers hung linen curtains all the way around the inside of the lantern, to shade the lens through the day. And every afternoon, before sunset, they drew them back again. That is what Alexander and Neil are doing now, unhooking the long pale curtains and folding them away, one panel after another, until the lens stands bare and shining in the last light.

Here is a surprising detail. The rules for this work were astonishingly exact, and they had been exact for a very long time. As early as 1802, the keeper of the lighthouse at Portpatrick, on the southwest coast of Scotland, was told to trim the wicks of his oil lamps down to precisely three-sixteenths of an inch. Not about that much. Precisely that much. A wick trimmed too long smokes and blackens the glass. A wick trimmed too short gives a weak, low flame. The difference between a good light and a poor one, out at sea, could come down to a sliver of cotton no thicker than a fingernail.

Neil has been told about the three-sixteenths. He has also been told, by Thomas Rennie, that if he ever gets it wrong the Board will hear about it in Edinburgh within the week, by some magic nobody understands. Neil is almost sure this is a joke.

The rules that governed every keeper in Scotland were printed in a small book called the Instructions to Light-Keepers. The Board issued it, revised it now and then, and expected every keeper to know it almost by heart. An edition from 1850 still survives today in the National Records of Scotland, in Edinburgh, among the Board's own papers. Its pages set out how a keeper was to live, and how he was to behave, from the cleaning of the lens to the keeping of the journal. The lamp was to be lit at sunset and kept burning bright and clear until sunrise. The lantern and lens were to be cleaned every day. Every keeper took his turn at the watch, and the keeper on watch was not to leave the light. The time of lighting, the time of putting out, and the state of the weather were all to be written down. Nothing about it was left to chance, or to mood, or to whether a man felt like it on a cold night.

And the keepers knew that they could be checked at any time. A superintendent from the Board traveled the coast inspecting the stations, and he did not always say when he was coming. Once a year, too, the Commissioners themselves sailed around Scotland in the Board's own steamship, the Pharos, calling at lighthouse after lighthouse, climbing the stairs, running gloved fingers along the brass, and reading the journals. A keeper with a dull lens or a careless journal would hear about it. A keeper whose station shone might be remembered when the time came to choose a new Principal.

Now let us go up into the lantern with them.

You come up through a small hatch in the floor, and the light is the first thing you notice. After the dim stair, it is like stepping into the inside of a cloud. Glass on every side of you, and beyond the glass, nothing but sea and sky, gray and silver and copper, moving all the time. You can see the swell rolling in from the west in long, slow ridges, each one breaking white across the reef. You can see gulls far below you, small as moths. The wind presses against the glass, and the panes hum very faintly in their frames.

The lens stands beside you, close enough to touch, though you must not touch it. It is cold and perfectly clean, and in its curves you can see the whole sky reflected over and over again, tiny and bent. The brass of its frame has been polished so many times that it is soft and pale at the edges. The floor is spotless. There is not a speck of soot anywhere, not a fingerprint, not a smear. Alexander runs a folded linen cloth along a rail that is already clean, out of pure habit.

Alexander is wearing his uniform jacket this afternoon, as the Board expects. It is dark blue cloth, with brass buttons that he polishes along with everything else, and on the peak of his cap there is a small badge. Out here, where nobody can see him but three other keepers and a cat, he could easily go about in an old jersey. He does not. He says that a man who keeps a light for the Board should look as if he keeps a light for the Board, and that the lens can tell the difference. Thomas once asked him how the lens could possibly tell the difference. Alexander only said that it could, and Thomas, after a moment, decided not to argue.

Neil wears his own jersey, thick and oily and smelling of sheep, knitted by his mother on Skye the winter before he left. He is not entitled to the full uniform yet. When he is, he has privately decided, he will polish the buttons every day, just like Alexander.

Neil hands up the paraffin, can by can. There is a smell to this room that you will never forget, oil and brass polish and cold glass and the sea. Alexander checks the level in the lamp's reservoir, checks the wicks, and sets out his trimming scissors and a clean cloth on a small shelf, ready for the moment of lighting. He looks out to the west, at the sun, judging. He does not need a clock to tell him how long there is. But he will check the clock anyway. That is the rule.

Today, a lighthouse lamp needs no one to trim its wick, because it has no wick. A modern light can run for months untouched, and the keeper, if there is one at all, is a computer in an office on the mainland.

It is a fitting moment to mention a story that hangs over every Scottish lighthouse, though it has not happened yet. Ten years from now, in December of 1900, on a small island called Eilean Mòr in the Flannan Isles, well to the north of here, three keepers will go missing from their station, and the relief boat will find the light unlit and the tower empty. It became one of the most famous mysteries of the sea. But you may have heard it told with dramatic entries from the keepers' logbook, about storms and weeping and prayer. Those famous lines were a later invention. They do not appear in the real record. The real report, written by the Board's superintendent in January 1901, is plain and sad and sensible. He thought the three men had most likely been caught by an unusually large wave while they were securing equipment at the landing during a storm.

Historians and writers still disagree about what happened on Eilean Mòr. Some accept the superintendent's view, that a great sea came up the cliff and carried the men away. Others think something else happened at the landing that day, and wonder whether one man went to warn the others. What nearly everyone now agrees on is that the dramatic logbook entries were added to the story later, by people who wanted to make a sad and simple tragedy into a ghost tale. We will leave that story there, early in our night, and not come back to it.

Back in the lantern at Skerryvore, Neil is folding the last of the curtains. He folds them the way Alexander showed him, corner to corner, then in thirds, so that they will not crease badly against the lens tomorrow. His hands are cold, and he is concentrating so hard that his tongue is caught between his teeth.

Before the curtains came down, Neil had spent most of the afternoon at the lens with Alexander, cleaning it, as the rules said it must be cleaned every single day. It is slow, careful work. Every prism is wiped with a soft linen cloth, then gone over again with a chamois leather, and any stubborn mark is rubbed away with a pinch of fine polishing powder, so fine it feels like flour between the fingers. You never use anything rough. You never press hard. You work from the top down, ring by ring, so that nothing you wipe away falls onto a ring you have already cleaned. And you breathe away from the glass, because even a man's breath leaves a mist on it.

Neil had cleaned the lower rings himself today, for the first time without Alexander standing at his shoulder. When he finished, Alexander came and looked, and bent close, and tilted his head to catch the light along each curve. Then he straightened up and said nothing at all, and moved on to the brass. Neil has learned already that this is very high praise.

Alexander watches him without saying anything. He remembers his own first winter, at a station far to the north, and an old Principal Keeper who said almost nothing for a month and then told him, one evening, that he would do. He had never been prouder of anything in his life.

Thomas Rennie comes up the stair behind them, his head and shoulders appearing through the hatch like a man rising out of the sea. He has been in the oil room below, checking the stores. He looks out at the swell rolling white across the reef, and says that the relief boat will not be coming today, then, and that he hopes Bess likes his company, because she will be having it a while longer.

He was due ashore today, you see. His month on the rock was up. The boat from Hynish should have come out at midday, bringing fresh stores, and letters, and taking him home for a spell. But the swell left over from yesterday's gale is still running heavy across the reef, and nobody could land a boat on this rock in a sea like that. From the top of the tower, earlier, they looked across through the telescope at the signal tower at Hynish, and they knew without being told. The boat is staying in harbor.

Thomas says it lightly. He is not one for complaining. But Alexander notices that he looks out toward Tiree for a moment longer than he needs to.

Neil asks whether this happens often. Alexander says it happens often enough. And then he says that they have food for weeks, and water, and coal, and oil for the light, and that the boat will come when the sea allows it, and not a moment before. And that, he says, is the end of that.

Neil finishes folding the last curtain and stacks it neatly on the others. Beside him the great lens stands bare now, catching the last copper light of the afternoon, every prism glowing faintly, as if it already knows what it is for.

## Part Three — Half Past Four: The Lighting of the Lamp

The sun touches the edge of the sea at almost exactly half past four, and for a few minutes the whole western sky burns orange and rose above the reef, and the wet granite of the tower glows like warm bread.

Here is a small question for this part. How do you light a lamp that sits inside a cage of glass, at the top of a tower, in a cold wind, so that it catches the first time and burns steadily all night?

Let us start with what every keeper in Scotland knew. The lighting of the lamp was the most important moment of the day, and it happened at sunset, not a minute before and not a minute after. Each station had tables of the times, worked out for its own place on the map, so that the keeper knew for every evening of the year when the sun would go down. In mid-January out here, that time comes at about half past four by the Greenwich clock. By midsummer it will be nearly half past nine at night, and the light will burn for only a few short hours. In midwinter, it burns for more than sixteen.

That clock is worth a moment of our attention. Only ten years before our night, in 1880, Parliament had made Greenwich time the legal time for the whole of Great Britain. Before that, many towns kept their own local time, set by the sun overhead, so that noon in Edinburgh came a little later than noon in London. Out here, so far to the west, the sun is about half an hour behind Greenwich. By the sun alone, it is barely four o'clock when the keeper lights his lamp. By the clock on the wall, it is half past four. The keepers lit by their tables, and their tables were worked out for this very rock, so the light came on at the true sunset, whatever the clock face said.

The keeper whose watch it was stood in the lantern with the lamp trimmed and the oil ready, and he watched the sun. When it went, he lit. Then he wrote the time down in the journal. The Board did not want approximately. It wanted the minute.

The lamp itself, by 1890, burns paraffin. For most of the century, Scottish lighthouses had burned colza oil, which is pressed from the seeds of a kind of rape plant, and before that, whale oil and fish oil. But in the early eighteen seventies the Board tested the new mineral oils, found that they gave a brighter, whiter flame and cost less, and began changing its lights over. Paraffin was cheaper, it kept well, and the wicks lasted longer before they charred. The smell of it, sharp and clean, has soaked into every board and rope in this tower.

The burner is not like a lamp in an ordinary house. It has several round wicks, set one inside another in rings, like the rings of a tree, so that together they make one broad, tall, bright flame. Above it sits a tall glass chimney to draw the air up through the flame and keep it steady. Oil is fed up to the wicks at a constant level, so that they never run dry.

So here is how it is done. Thomas Rennie, whose watch it is, opens a small door in the brass frame of the lens and reaches in with a lit taper. He lights the innermost wick first, then the next, then the next, turning each one up very slowly. This is the part that takes patience. If he turns the flame up too fast while the glass chimney is still cold, the chimney may crack. So he coaxes it, a little at a time, until the whole burner is alight and the chimney is warm, and the flame stands up tall and white and does not smoke. Then he closes the little door, and the lens takes the light.

And that is the answer to our small question. You light it from the inside, ring by ring, slowly, and you never hurry the glass.

Here is a surprising detail. The glass of the great lens around that flame was not made in Scotland. When Alan Stevenson designed the light for Skerryvore, he went to France. The finest lighthouse lenses in the world were being made in Paris, using the ideas of the French scientist Augustin Fresnel, who had died young in 1827. Fresnel's brother, Léonor, carried on his work, and the prisms for Skerryvore were made in Paris under his direction. Alan Stevenson then improved on the design himself, adding extra rings of prisms below the great central band of the lens, to catch light that would otherwise be lost and send it out to sea as well. The light first shone from this tower on the first of February, 1844.

In his book of 1848, Alan Stevenson wrote at great length about these lenses. He explained how the old lights had worked with polished metal mirrors behind each lamp, and how the new French way used glass to bend the light instead, and how much brighter and steadier it was. He wrote warmly of Fresnel and his family, and of the care with which every prism had to be ground and set. You can feel, reading those pages, that this was the part of the work he loved best. The granite was a battle against the sea. The light was something closer to art.

Historians of science still disagree about who deserves the credit for this kind of lens. Most give it to Augustin Fresnel, who built the first working lighthouse lenses in France in the eighteen twenties. But the Scottish scientist David Brewster had proposed building up a lens from separate rings of glass some years earlier, and he argued for the rest of his life that he had been first, and that Britain had been slow to listen to him. Both men were brilliant, and both, in a sense, were right. Fresnel made it work. Brewster had seen it coming.

Now come and stand in the lantern while the light comes on.

You are standing just behind Thomas, out of his way. Outside, the sun has gone, and the sky in the west is fading from orange to a pale clear green, and the sea below has turned the color of slate. The wind is picking up a little. You can hear it now, not a roar yet, just a long low note against the glass.

Thomas bends to the lens. There is a faint scratch as the taper catches, then a small blue flicker deep inside the glass. Slowly it grows. It turns yellow, then white. And then something happens that you did not expect. The whole lens comes alive. Every ring of glass, every prism, every curve suddenly fills with light, so that the great cage of glass beside you is no longer cold and clear but burning, shining, alive with brightness, from top to bottom. You have to narrow your eyes. The light pours out through the lantern panes and out over the darkening sea, a great broad blade of it, reaching away into the dusk.

You feel a little warmth on your face from the flame, even through all that glass. You smell the paraffin as it takes. You hear Thomas let out a long breath through his nose, quietly, a sound of satisfaction, the sound of a man who has done this a thousand times and still likes it.

You step closer to the glass and look out along the beam. It is not a narrow ray, like the light from a hand lamp. It is broad and soft at the edges, and as it sweeps across the sea it lights up everything it touches for an instant and then lets it go. The tops of the waves flash white. A gull, gliding low over the reef, turns silver for a moment and then vanishes into the dusk again. The spray from a breaking sea hangs in the air, lit from within, like a cloud of white dust. Then the beam moves on, and the sea goes dark, and you wait for it to come round again.

Far out to the west, where the sea meets the last of the evening sky, there is nothing at all. No ship, no land, no other light. Just the long gray line of the Atlantic, darkening, and the beam reaching out toward it, over and over, as if it were feeling its way into the night.

Then he bends to the clockwork. Below the lens is a machine, a heavy brass mechanism like the inside of a great clock, with a weight hanging on a chain that runs down through the tower. When the weight is wound up and released, it turns the whole lens, slowly, steadily, round and round. As it turns, the beam sweeps across the sea, so that a ship far out sees a flash, and then darkness, and then another flash, in a fixed pattern that tells the captain exactly which lighthouse he is looking at. Every lighthouse on the coast has its own pattern, like a signature written in light. Thomas sets the clockwork running, and the lens begins to turn.

Today, most lighthouses switch on by themselves, when a small sensor feels the daylight fading. No one needs to watch the sun go down at all.

Below, in the kitchen, Hector MacLean has heard the clockwork start. You can hear it all through the tower if you listen, a faint, steady ticking and turning, very far off, like a clock in another room. He has the kettle on and potatoes boiling, and he does not need to go up and look. He knows by the sound that the light is lit.

Alexander Grant comes up to the lantern anyway, as he does every evening, whether it is his watch or not. He stands for a moment looking at the flame through the lens, with his hands behind his back, judging it. He nods, once. Thomas nods back. Nothing else is said, and nothing else is needed.

Then Alexander goes down to the room below the lantern, where the station journal lies open on a small desk. He dips his pen, and in his neat slanting hand he writes the date, the fourteenth of January, 1890, and beside it the time of lighting, and the state of the wind and the sea. Wind west by south, fresh. Heavy swell. No boat.

Neil had wanted very much to light the lamp himself tonight. He had asked Thomas at dinnertime, half joking, and Thomas had said that he might, on some calm evening soon, with Alexander standing beside him, but not on an evening with a gale coming. So Neil stood behind Thomas and watched every movement, the taper, the rings, the slow turning up of each wick, the moment of waiting for the chimney to warm. He was counting under his breath. He wanted to know how long Thomas waited between one ring and the next, so that when his own turn came he would get it exactly right.

Thomas noticed, of course. When the lamp was lit, he turned round and said, very seriously, that the secret was to count to forty between each ring, in Gaelic, because the lamp did not understand English. Neil looked at him for a long moment. Then he laughed, and Thomas laughed, and down below them, at the desk, Alexander pretended not to have heard, and went on writing.

The truth, Thomas told him afterward, was that there is no number to count to. You watch the flame, and you watch the glass, and when the flame stands up clean and the glass has stopped misting, you go on to the next ring. Your eyes do the counting. It takes about a year, he said, before you stop thinking about it.

Neil watches him write. He asks how long the journals are kept. Alexander says they are kept for always. Every night of every year since this light was first lit, someone has written the time in a book like this one. If you went to Edinburgh, he says, and asked, they could tell you when the lamp at Skerryvore was lit on any night you liked, back to the year it began.

Neil thinks about that for a while. It seems to him a strange and rather wonderful thing, that this one cold evening, the fourteenth of January, will be written down and kept, along with all the other evenings, in a long row of books, in a city he has never seen.

Up above, Thomas pulls a wooden stool close to the lens and sits down, with his coat buttoned to his chin, to begin the first watch. Bess the cat comes up through the hatch, looks at him, looks at the light, and settles herself against his boot. She seems to think the lamp is lit for her.

And above them all, at the top of the tower, the light turns, steady and bright, throwing its long beam out across the darkening water, every sweep the same as the last.

## Part Four — Around Half Past Five: Supper High in the Tower

The last green light has gone from the western sky now, and the sea is black, and every half minute or so the beam from the lantern passes over the water like a slow white hand, while down in the kitchen the window has turned into a dark mirror.

Here is a small question for this part. In a tower with no wife, no mother and no cook, who makes the supper?

The kitchen is one of the rooms stacked up inside the tower, high above the solid base, round like all the others, with a wall of granite several feet thick and a single deep window cut through it like a tunnel. There is a small iron range against the wall, with a fire in it, and a black kettle that is almost never off the boil. There is a table, scrubbed white, and a bench, and a dresser with plates set on edge behind a wooden rail so they will not slide off when the tower trembles in a storm. There are shelves of tins and jars, a sack of oatmeal, a crock of salt, and a barrel of potatoes from Tiree, dug last autumn and kept dry in the dark.

Every room in the tower is round, and that changes everything. No furniture fits against a round wall quite properly. The beds, which are up in the rooms above, are built against the curve. The table stands in the middle of the floor. A cupboard sits at an angle. A man learns to move about these rooms without corners, turning gently, the way you move around a round table at a crowded supper.

Everything in this kitchen came up the tower the hard way. The coal for the range is stored in the rooms near the bottom, above the solid stone, and it was hauled up from the landing in sacks, by a rope and a winch, then carried up the stair a scuttle at a time. The water is kept in casks and tanks in the lower rooms too, brought out from Hynish on the boat or caught from the rain, and every drop of it that comes to the kettle has been carried up, step by step, in a bucket. A keeper on a rock station climbed his own stairs dozens of times a day. Men who served long years in towers like this one often said they could tell how many steps were in any staircase in the world just by climbing it once, because their legs had learned to count.

Hector says there are more than a hundred and fifty steps between the door and the lantern, though he has never actually counted them, and Thomas says that is because Hector has never once climbed them without stopping to talk.

Life out here was simple, and most of it was the same every day. The men kept a rough share of the work between them. Whoever was not on watch and not asleep did the cooking, the cleaning, the hauling of coal up from the storerooms, the scrubbing of stairs. Each keeper usually brought out his own box of provisions from the shore, oatmeal and tea and sugar and salt meat and whatever his wife had baked, and in many stations the men pooled their food and took turns at the stove, because it was easier and friendlier that way.

Sundays were a little different. The light was kept as always, and the watches turned as always, but on a Sunday morning, in many Scottish towers, the keepers who were awake gathered in the kitchen and one of them read a chapter of the Bible aloud, and perhaps they sang a psalm together, slowly, in the old way, with one man giving out each line and the others singing it back after him. On Tiree, as on Skye and Lewis, the psalms were sung in Gaelic as often as in English, long and wavering and beautiful, and Hector still sings them that way, under his breath, when he thinks no one is listening. The rest of the day was for rest, and for letters, and for sitting with a book, and for no hammering or scrubbing that could wait until Monday. Even on a rock in the Atlantic, the week had a shape.

So the answer to our small question is simple. They all do. Tonight it is Hector's turn. Tomorrow it will be someone else's. There is a list, pinned up by Alexander's hand, and nobody argues with the list.

And that brings us, a little early, to the first of our three questions. How do three men share one tower without a fight?

Here is a surprising detail. Ninety years before our night, in 1801, a lighthouse called the Smalls stood on a tiny rock off the far southwest coast of Wales. It was kept, at that time, by only two men, Thomas Howell and Thomas Griffith, and people said afterward that they did not get along. During a long spell of bad weather, Griffith fell ill and died. Howell was left alone on the rock with his companion, and he was afraid that if he gave the body to the sea, people ashore might think he had killed him. So he kept the light burning, alone, for weeks, until at last a boat could come. When it did, the men in it are said to have hardly recognized him.

It is a sad old story, and it was told and retold in lighthouses all around the coasts of Britain. Long before our night, the usual practice on the great rock stations had become not two keepers, but at least three. With three men, nobody is ever completely alone with one other person. If one falls ill, there are still two to keep the watches. If two men quarrel, there is a third to stand between them, and to change the subject, and to put the kettle on.

So here is the answer to our first carry-question, and it is not really about friendship at all. Three men share one tower without a fight because almost nothing in their day is left for them to argue over. The watches are fixed, written up, and rotated, so that no one has the cold small hours every night. The Principal Keeper has the final word, and everyone knows it, and he knows not to use it more than he has to. Each man has his own duties, his own bed, his own shelf, his own box. The journal records what is done and who did it, so there is no quarreling over who forgot the oil. And there is a long, quiet habit of courtesy, almost formal, that the older men teach the younger ones. You do not crowd a man who is reading. You do not take the last of someone's sugar without asking. You do not tell the same joke twice in one month, if you can help it.

And there was one more thing, harder and plainer than the rest. The Board was very strict. A keeper who fell asleep on watch could be dismissed, and so could any keeper who knew and did not report it. In 1871, at Sumburgh Head, at the southern tip of Shetland, one keeper fell asleep on duty, and he and another keeper agreed between them to say nothing about it. They were found out. Both men were dismissed, and one of them was a Principal Keeper with twenty-three years of service. In the whole second half of the century, lighthouse records in Scotland hold only a handful of cases like it, about fifteen in fifty years, and that one was remembered as the worst. Every keeper on the coast heard about it. The lesson was clear. You could be kind to your neighbor in the tower. You could be patient with him, and cook for him, and cover for him in a hundred small ways. But you could not cover for him about the light.

The records of those years still survive. The Board kept registers of every keeper it employed, with each man's stations, his promotions, and sometimes the reasons he left the service, written in long careful columns. The registers from 1869 to 1912 are among the Board's papers in the National Records of Scotland. Turning their pages, you find whole careers in a few lines. A man joins as a young assistant, moves from station to station every few years, is made Principal Keeper in his forties, and retires at last to a cottage by the sea. And once in a long while, you find a single blunt line where a career ends early, because a light went dim one night and someone did not tell.

Historians who study these stations disagree a little about the Smalls story. Some think it truly changed the rules, and that lighthouses began keeping three men on rock stations because of it. Others point out that larger crews were already becoming common for other good reasons, such as illness and the sheer length of the winter watches, and that the old story simply became the famous explanation for something that was happening anyway. Both may be partly true. A rule often grows from many reasons, and a story is how people remember it.

Now come down to the kitchen and sit at the table.

You are on the bench, with your back against the curving wall. The stone behind you is cold, even through your coat, but the room is warm from the range, and the window is black, and every now and then a faint white glow slides across it and away again, as the beam passes overhead. The fire ticks and settles in the grate. Steam rises from a big iron pot on the stove, and the smell of it fills the room, salt beef and barley and onion and pepper, simmering since the middle of the afternoon.

Hector stands at the stove in his shirtsleeves, stirring. He tips the potatoes into a colander, shakes them, and drops them onto a dish in a cloud of steam. He puts a plate in front of you, and a bowl of broth with a spoon standing in it, and a thick slice of bread that his wife Catriona baked and sent out on the last boat, ten days ago now, still good if a little firm. There is butter in a crock. There is tea, very strong, very black, with condensed milk stirred into it from a tin, which makes it sweet and pale and wonderful.

You eat. The broth is thick and salty and hot, and you can feel it warming you all the way down. The potatoes are floury and fall apart when you touch them with a fork. Somewhere below your feet, through the floor, the sea booms against the rock, and the whole tower answers it with a long, low hum. Nobody at the table seems to notice. Hector is talking in Gaelic to Neil, quietly, and Neil is laughing.

When you have finished, Hector takes your plate without asking and puts a second potato on it, and a spoonful of butter that melts at once into a small golden pool. In the islands, a guest who leaves the table with room inside him is a sign that the house has failed, and Hector will not have that said of his kitchen. So you eat the second potato. It is just as good as the first. The tea is refilled, black and sweet. The warmth of the range reaches your knees. Somewhere above you, very faintly, through all the floors of stone, you can hear the clockwork turning the light.

Today, people who live and work in remote places, on research stations at the poles or on ships far from land, are often chosen carefully for how well they get along with others, and they have telephones and video calls to their families every week. The men in this tower have a list on the wall, a pot of tea, and a boat that may or may not come.

Alexander comes down the stair and takes his place at the head of the table. He says a short grace, in English, and the others bow their heads. Then he eats slowly, as he does everything. Thomas is still up in the lantern on the first watch, so Hector sets a covered bowl at the back of the range for him, where it will keep hot.

Neil tells a story about his grandmother on Skye, who kept a hen that would only lay in the minister's hat. Nobody believes a word of it, and everyone enjoys it. Hector tells one back, about a cow on Tiree that walked all the way to Scarinish on its own to see the steamer come in. Alexander says nothing, but his eyes crinkle at the corners, which, Neil is beginning to learn, is how Alexander laughs.

Alexander says, without looking up from his plate, that he once met that very cow at the Scarinish pier, and that she had better manners than some of the inspectors the Board sends out from Edinburgh. It is the longest joke anyone has heard him make in a month, and Hector laughs so hard that he has to put down his spoon.

When the plates are done, Neil washes them in a basin of water heated on the range. Water is precious in the tower, because every drop of it has to be carried out from the shore in casks or caught from the rain, so he uses as little as he can, the way Hector showed him on his first night. He dries each plate and slides it back into the rail of the dresser.

Hector takes the covered bowl and a mug of tea up the stair to Thomas in the lantern. When he comes back down, he says that Thomas sends his compliments to the cook, and that the cook is a genius, and that the cook should also know that the wind is rising.

Alexander takes a small, worn book from his pocket and opens it. It is not the Bible, though he reads that too. It is a book of seamanship he has owned for thirty years, its pages soft as cloth. He does not really read it anymore. He just likes to hold it.

Bess comes down the stair from the lantern, very dignified, and walks once around the kitchen, inspecting everyone. Then she jumps onto the bench beside Neil and curls up against his leg, because he is the youngest and the warmest, and because he has been feeding her scraps of beef under the table when he thinks nobody is looking.

And far above them, through all the floors of stone, the light goes on turning, steady and patient, sending its beam out over the dark water once more.

## Part Five — Around Half Past Six: The Men Who Built the Tower

The kitchen clock on the dresser says half past six, and the dishes are dried and put away, and the wind outside has risen from a long low note to a steady, deeper sound, like a great breath drawn in and never quite let out.

Here is a small question for this part. How do you build a tower of stone on a rock that the sea covers whenever it likes?

Hector is sitting by the range with a coil of old rope across his knees. He is making a mat for the floor of his cottage at Hynish, plaiting the rope into a tight flat spiral, and as he works he tells Neil about the building of this tower, because his own father was there.

Here is the common story, the one every family on Tiree knew. The work began in 1838. Before a single stone could be laid on Skerryvore, the Board needed a base on land, so Alan Stevenson chose Hynish, at the southern tip of Tiree, the nearest shore that faced the reef. There he built a work yard, a pier, a small harbor, workshops, a store, houses for the workmen and, later, the cottages where the keepers' families would live. Granite was brought from the Ross of Mull, across the water to the east, and at Hynish the masons shaped every block by hand, fitted each one against its neighbors on the ground to make sure it was perfect, numbered it, and then took the courses apart again for the voyage out.

The harbor at Hynish had a problem of its own. Sand kept washing into it, choking the berth where the boats lay. So Stevenson built a reservoir above the harbor, a pond held behind a wall, and when the harbor silted up the men opened sluices and let the stored water rush down through a channel to scour the sand away. It is still there today, the stone channel and the reservoir, if you walk around Hynish.

At first, the masons tried a different stone. The lowest three courses of the tower, the rings of stone that sit right down on the rock, were cut from the hard gneiss of Hynish itself, the old gray rock of Tiree. But it was so tough and so difficult to work that the masons struggled to shape it, and after those first courses Stevenson turned to the granite of Mull, which was kinder to the chisel and still strong enough to stand against the sea. So if you could look at the very foot of the tower, under the weed and the foam, you would see a thin band of Tiree stone, and above it, rising all the way to the lantern, the pale granite of Mull.

The whole undertaking, when it was finished, was reckoned to have cost nearly eighty-seven thousand pounds. That figure covered not only the tower and its light, but the harbor and the buildings at Hynish, and even a small steamship bought to tow the stone boats out to the reef. It was an enormous sum for the time. The Commissioners had hesitated over it for years. But when it was spent, they had something that no money could easily have bought, a light where there had never been one, on the most feared reef on the coast.

Out on the reef, the work could only go on in summer. Even then it stopped whenever the swell came up. In the first season the men began by building a barrack, a wooden house set high up on tall legs of timber and iron, fixed into the rock, so that the workmen could live out on the reef and not lose hours every day rowing back and forth from the ship. They had nearly finished it by the autumn of 1838, and they left it standing for the winter.

On the third of November that year, a great gale came in. When the storm had passed, the storekeeper at Hynish, a man named Hogben, wrote to Alan Stevenson with the news. The barrack was gone. Every timber of it, the whole summer's work, had simply disappeared from the rock. Stevenson thought afterward that it had probably been struck by wreckage from a ship, driven against it by the sea.

So they began again. In 1839 they built a new barrack, stronger than the first, and this one stood. And in 1840 the workmen went out to live in it, and the first real stones of the tower were laid.

Here is a surprising detail. That barrack stood on its legs well above the rock, high enough, you would think, to be safe from anything. But in a summer gale, broken water from the waves could come down onto its roof, about seventy-five feet above the sea. The men inside were living in a wooden room on stilts, with the Atlantic falling on the roof above their heads.

Alan Stevenson shared that barrack with his men for long spells of each season. In his book of 1848 he describes the first night the workmen spent in the barrack, in 1840, with a strong wind from the northwest and the waves striking the rock without a pause. Nobody, he wrote, managed to sleep at all. He describes the barrack trembling, and the spray, and the long work of the following days, and the men coming to trust their strange home little by little, until they could sleep through a gale as if it were rain on a cottage roof. He writes about them with real respect, as men who did hard and frightening work far from home, and did it well.

So here is the answer to our small question. You build a tower on a rock like this by doing everything you possibly can on land, so that out on the reef you only have to set the stones in place. You live on the rock in a house on stilts. You work only in summer and only in calm weather. You wait. You lose a season, and you begin again. And you do it, year after year, for six summers, until the tower stands.

Robert Louis Stevenson, Alan's nephew, later wrote about his uncle's work with great admiration. He thought Skerryvore was the noblest of all the deep-sea lights then standing anywhere, and he said so in his writing about the family. Not every historian agrees with him. Many engineers would give that honor to the older Bell Rock, built by Alan's father, Robert, half a century before, because it was the first great rock tower in Scotland, and showed everyone else that it could be done at all. Others point out that Skerryvore is taller and stands much farther out in the Atlantic, in a heavier sea. Both views are fair. A son built on his father's work, and made it greater. And a nephew loved them both.

Today, an offshore light might be built of steel in a factory, carried out in sections by a heavy ship, and lowered into place by crane in a matter of days, with helicopters bringing out the workers. At Skerryvore, every stone went out by sail and oar.

Now let us go back in time, just for a moment, to see it being built.

You are standing on the reef on a summer morning in 1841. The sea is calm, for once, a long slow swell under a pale sky. The rock is crowded with men. There are masons in shirtsleeves, sunburnt and dusty, and laborers hauling on ropes, and boys running with buckets of mortar. Beside the rock, rising and falling gently on the swell, a small vessel lies at anchor, loaded with dressed granite blocks, each one numbered in paint. A tall wooden crane swings out over the water, and a block rises from the deck on its chains, slowly, turning a little in the air.

You hear the clink of iron on stone, and the creak of the crane, and the shouts of the foreman. You hear the sea sucking and slapping at the edges of the rock. Above your head, on its tall legs, stands the barrack, with washing hung out to dry on a line between two of its posts, shirts and stockings flapping in the breeze. From inside it comes the smell of frying bacon.

A boy runs past you with a bucket of fresh water from the boat, slopping a little over the rim onto the hot rock, where it steams. A dog that belongs to one of the seamen is lying in the only patch of shade, under the barrack, with its chin on its paws, watching the gulls. Somebody is singing on the far side of the tower, in Gaelic, a work song with a strong pulling beat, and every time the chorus comes round, the men on the crane rope heave together.

The tower itself is only a few courses high this summer, a broad round stump of pale granite, but you can already see what it will be. Each block is cut to lock against the next, top and bottom and side to side, so that the whole ring holds itself together like the stones of an arch. A mason runs his palm along a newly set block and nods. It fits perfectly. It fitted perfectly on the ground at Hynish, months ago, and it fits perfectly now.

Back in the kitchen in 1890, Hector is telling Neil about his father. His father was a young man then, from Balemartine, and like many men on Tiree he found work in the yard at Hynish, hauling and loading stone. He never went out to live on the rock himself, Hector says. He was a farmer's son and the sea frightened him. But he loaded the boats, and he watched them go out, and every evening he looked across to the reef to see whether they had come safely home. And when the light was first lit, on that February night in 1844, the whole township of Balemartine came out to see it. His father told him that some of the old women wept, because for as long as anyone could remember that reef had been a place of drowning, and now, for the first time, there was a light upon it.

Hector's father used to say that the strangest thing about those years was the noise. For six summers, Hynish rang from morning to night with the sound of hammers and chisels on stone, hundreds of them, so that on a still day you could hear it on the far side of the island. Then the work was done, and the masons went home to the mainland, and one morning the yard was silent. He said that for weeks afterward the people of Balemartine kept waking at dawn and listening for the hammers, and feeling that something was missing, before they remembered that the tower was finished.

Neil asks whether Hector's father ever came out to see the finished tower. Hector says yes, just once, years later, on a calm summer day, when Hector was a boy. He remembers his father standing at the bottom of the ladder, looking up, for a very long time, without saying a word. And then he said, in Gaelic, that it was a good deal taller than it had looked from Tiree.

Neil laughs. Hector smiles and goes on plaiting his rope.

Alexander, at the table, has been listening without seeming to. He says that every lighthouse on the coast has stories like that behind it, if you ask the right families. Men who cut the stone, women who fed the men who cut the stone, children who carried water. It is a thing to remember, he says, on a bad night, that the tower around you was built by people who meant it to last.

Neil asks what is left of the old work yard at Hynish now. Hector tells him that most of it is still there, if he looks. The long pier, and the harbor, and the reservoir on the hill, and the houses the workmen lived in, some of them turned into homes for the keepers' families and the boatmen. The big workshop where the masons shaped the stone stands empty and quiet, its great doors shut, and the children play hide and seek around it on summer evenings. In wet weather you can still find chips of Mull granite in the grass, left over from the shaping, pale and sparkling, and the children collect them like treasure.

Hector says that his own two boys have a whole box of them under their bed. They believe that every chip is a piece of the lighthouse that did not quite fit. He has never had the heart to tell them otherwise, because in a way it is true.

Thomas, if he were here, would say that he had heard every word of that story before, and that the chips grow larger every time Hector tells it. But Thomas is up in the lantern, and Hector is free to tell it his own way.

Neil looks around the kitchen at the curving wall, at the deep window, at the thick dressed blocks of granite fitted together so closely that you could not slide a knife blade between them. He puts his hand flat against the wall. It is cold and smooth and utterly still, even though the wind outside is rising and the sea is booming at the foot of the rock. He thinks of the young man from Balemartine loading stone onto a boat, half a century ago, and he feels, for the first time since he came out here, that the tower is not only a place of work but a kind of shelter that someone made for him.

Bess, on the bench, opens one eye and closes it again. And high above, through all those fitted rings of granite, the light turns on, as it has turned on every night since that February evening in 1844, when the old women of Balemartine stood on the shore and wept to see it.

## Part Six — Around Eight O'Clock: The Change of Watch and the Signal Tower

At eight o'clock exactly, by the clock on the dresser, Hector MacLean puts down his rope, takes his coat from its peg, and climbs the stair toward the lantern, and a few minutes later Thomas Rennie comes down it, rubbing his hands, with Bess trotting at his heels.

Here is a small question for this part. With no telegraph wire, no post office and no road, how does a man in this tower send word to his family twelve miles away?

The change of watch is a small ceremony, and it happens the same way every time. The keeper coming up does not simply walk in and sit down. He looks at the flame. He checks the oil. He looks at the clockwork, and the weight on its chain, and he listens to the turning of the lens to hear that it is running smoothly. He looks out through the glass at the sea and the sky. Only then does he say that he has the watch, and only then may the other man leave. Up in the lantern, Hector has done all of this, and Thomas has told him the wind has gone round a little to the west and is freshening, and that he has trimmed the wicks once at half past six, and that the light is good.

Now, the answer to our small question lies on the shore of Tiree, at Hynish, beside the keepers' cottages. It is a tower too, though a much smaller one, round and stout, built of granite like its big sister on the reef. It is called the signal tower. It was built while the lighthouse was going up, at the beginning of the eighteen forties, and it has a tall flagstaff on top of it, and a room inside with a window facing out to sea.

From the signal tower, on a clear day, a man with a good telescope could see Skerryvore, twelve miles away, a pale thin pencil standing up out of the water. And from the top of Skerryvore, a keeper with a telescope could see Hynish. So the two towers talked to each other with signals. The keepers on the rock could hoist a flag or a shape on their gallery to say that all was well, or that they needed something, or that someone was ill. At Hynish, a man watched for it, and when he saw the signal he answered by hoisting a ball to the top of the flagstaff on the signal tower, so the keepers knew their message had been seen.

The watching was done at set hours, so that both sides knew when to look. One account says that the lookout at Hynish was kept for an hour in the morning, between nine and ten, and again for an hour in the afternoon, between two and three. Then the families ashore would know, if the day was clear, that their men were well. And the men on the rock would know that someone at home was looking.

So that is the answer. You send word home with a flag, a ball, and a telescope, at nine in the morning and two in the afternoon, if the weather allows. And in a winter like this one, with the spray so thick that Tiree is often hidden for days at a time, very often it does not allow at all.

Here is a surprising detail, and it is one of the loveliest things anyone remembers about this station. If a keeper was out on the rock when his wife gave birth at Hynish, the families ashore had a way of telling him whether the baby was a boy or a girl. They hoisted it on the flagstaff of the signal tower. For a girl, a little dress. For a boy, a pair of trousers. Somewhere out on the reef, a man with a telescope pressed to his eye would see a tiny dress fluttering on a flagpole twelve miles away, and know that he had a daughter.

The signal tower still stands at Hynish today. For many years it was a museum of the building of Skerryvore, with the telescopes and the story of the signals, and the whole little settlement around it, the pier, the harbor, the reservoir, the cottages, has been carefully restored. In the summer of 1892, two and a half years after our night, the Board will move the Skerryvore families away from Hynish to a new shore station on the island of Erraid, off the Ross of Mull, and Hynish will grow quiet. But tonight, in January of 1890, Catriona MacLean and her children, and Margaret Grant, are asleep or nearly asleep in those cottages, under the signal tower, facing the sea.

The keepers' families lived in a row of cottages on the slope above the signal tower, solid little houses of stone with slate roofs, built by the Board, each with a door facing the sea. Behind them were strips of garden, where the wives grew potatoes and kail in the sandy island soil, and kept a few hens. The children went to school on Tiree with the island children, and learned their lessons in English, and spoke Gaelic in the playground. On a Sunday the families walked together to church. When a keeper was out on the rock, his wife ran the house alone, as the wives of fishermen and sailors had always done, and the women of the row looked after one another.

And at nine o'clock every clear morning, and again at two in the afternoon, someone in that row would glance up at the flagstaff on the signal tower, and then out to sea, toward the thin pale line of the tower on the reef, to see what the day's signal said.

Historians of the station do not quite agree about the signal tower. Some date it to 1839, at the very start of the work. Others date it to 1843, near the end. Some accounts say it went on being used long after the families left, for decades more. Others say it was given up in 1892. The truth may simply be that it was built in stages, and used now and then long after its main job was done. Either way, for half a century, it was the thread that tied this rock to the shore.

Today, the keeper of any light, anywhere, could simply pick up a telephone. A man on a rock could hear his newborn daughter cry within minutes of her birth.

Now let us go up to the gallery, just for a moment, with Hector, while there is still a little light to see by.

It is not light, really. It is full dark now, but the clouds have thinned in the east, and there is a faint grayness over Tiree where the sky meets the land. You step out through a narrow door from the lantern onto the iron gallery, and the wind takes you at once. It is cold, and wet, and very strong, so strong that you hold the railing with both hands and lean into it. The air is full of salt. You can taste it, and feel it settling on your face like fine rain.

Below you, a very long way below, the sea is white. Every wave that comes in across the reef breaks into foam, and the foam spreads and glimmers in the dark. Behind you, through the glass, the great lens turns, and every few moments its beam swings out over your head and away across the water, lighting up the falling spray in a long silver streak before it vanishes into the dark.

And there, to the northeast, very faint, so faint you are not sure at first that you have seen it, there is a single small yellow light. It is a window in Hynish. It might be Catriona's. It might be the signal tower. It might be only a lamp left burning in the store. But it is there, twelve miles away, across the black and roaring water, and you find that you cannot stop looking at it.

Hector stands beside you, holding the rail. He does not say anything. He looks at that small light for a long time. Then he goes back inside, wipes the salt from the inside of the lantern glass where the door let the wet in, and sits down by the lens.

Inside the lantern, after the cold of the gallery, the air feels as warm as a kitchen. Your cheeks sting. Your fingers tingle as the blood comes back into them. The salt has left a fine stiff crust on your eyebrows and in your hair, and when you lick your lips, they taste of the sea. Through the glass, the beam goes on sweeping out into the dark, lighting up the spray for a moment, silver, and then letting it go.

You stand for a while beside the lens, with your back to the warmth of the flame, and you look out toward the place where that one small yellow window was. You cannot see it from in here, through the glare of the lens. But you know it is there. And somehow, knowing that, the darkness outside seems a little less wide.

Down in the kitchen, Thomas eats his supper out of the covered bowl, which has been keeping warm at the back of the range for two hours and is now somewhat thicker than broth ought to be. He says it is the best thing he has eaten all day, which is true, because he has eaten almost nothing else. Then he takes out a letter from his inside pocket. It is from Jessie, in Anstruther, the girl he is going to marry in the spring. It came out on the last boat, ten days ago, and he has read it so many times that the folds are going soft. He does not read it now. He just holds it, and puts it back.

Neil, at the end of the table, is writing a letter home to his mother on Skye. It will not go anywhere until the boat comes, but he writes it anyway. He tells her about the tower, and the lamp, and the lens. He tells her about Bess. He tells her he is eating well. He does not tell her about the swell, or the gale, or the boat that did not come, because he does not want her to worry.

Thomas finishes his supper and scrapes the bowl clean, and then he sits back and stretches his long legs out toward the range until his boots are nearly in the fire. He has been on watch for three and a half hours, and he will sleep now until midnight, when he goes up again. But he is in no hurry to move. The kitchen is warm, and the lamp on the table is bright, and he likes the company. He asks Neil whether he has written down everything about Bess, and Neil says yes, and Thomas says good, because Bess is the most important member of the station and the Board would want to know.

Alexander, who is reading by the fire, asks Hector's question for him, without looking up. Did Hector ever tell Neil about his youngest?

Neil says no. So Thomas tells it, because Hector is up in the lantern and would never tell it himself. Three winters ago, Thomas says, Hector was out here on the rock when his wife Catriona was due. The weather was bad for a week, and no boat could come or go, and Hector paced up and down these stairs like a caged bear. And on the eighth morning, it cleared. At nine o'clock exactly, Hector was up on the gallery with the telescope pressed so hard to his eye that it left a ring. And there, on the flagstaff at Hynish, was a little blue dress. Thomas says Hector came down the stair so fast he missed six steps, and that he sang in Gaelic for the rest of the day, and that it was the only time in all the years Thomas has known him that he has heard Hector sing out loud.

The girl's name is Mòrag. She is three now. Neil has seen her, on the pier at Hynish, the day he came out, waving at the boat.

Alexander closes his book on one finger and says that Margaret watches the signal tower more faithfully than any man the Board ever paid to do it. In thirty-one years of marriage, he says, she has never once missed the nine o'clock look when the weather was clear. She stands at the cottage door with her shawl around her and her hand over her eyes, and she looks out at the reef, and then up at the flagstaff, and then she goes in and puts the kettle on. He knows this, he says, because the other wives have told him. Margaret herself has never mentioned it.

Neil asks whether Alexander ever looks back at her, through the telescope. Alexander says that it is twelve miles, and that even the Board's best glass will not show a woman at a cottage door at that distance. Then he says that, yes, he looks anyway.

Neil goes back to his letter. He adds a line at the end. He tells his mother that the men here are kind.

Then he signs his name, folds the letter, and puts it in his own inside pocket, where it will wait, with Thomas's letter from Jessie, for the boat that will come when the sea allows. And far above, Hector sits by the lens, and the light turns, and its long beam sweeps out across the water toward Tiree, as if it were trying to reach that one small window on the shore.

## Part Seven — Around Half Past Nine: The Flame in the Gale

By half past nine the wind has become a gale, a full winter gale out of the west, and it has stopped sounding like wind at all. It sounds like a train that never arrives, roaring and roaring past the tower, while the sea throws itself white against the rock below.

Here is a small question for this part. Why does the keeper on watch keep a spare glass chimney and a box of fresh wicks right beside him, within reach of his hand, all night long?

Neil has come up to the lantern to keep Hector company and to learn. Alexander has said he may stay until ten, and then he must go to bed, because he is to sit with Thomas on the middle watch in the small hours and a tired learner is no use to anyone.

So Hector is teaching him, in Gaelic, over the noise of the wind. He shows Neil the spare chimney, standing upright in its rack, a tall cylinder of clear glass. He shows him the box of wicks, each one coiled neatly, white and new. He shows him the trimming scissors, and the little brass tool for raising and lowering each ring of the burner.

The answer to the small question is that glass breaks and cotton burns, and the light must not fail even for a minute. A chimney can crack from a sudden draft of cold air, or from a flame turned up too high, or for no reason anyone can see. A wick can char unevenly, so that one side of the flame burns lower than the other and the light goes dim and smoky. When that happens, there is no time to go downstairs and look for a spare. The keeper must change it at once, quickly and carefully, without letting the light go out, or, if it must go out for a moment, relight it as fast as his hands can move. So everything he might need is kept within his reach, in the same place, every night, so that he could find it in the dark.

Hector tells Neil about the one time it happened to him. It was years ago, at another station, on the north coast, on a night of hard frost. He had opened the lantern door for a moment to step onto the gallery, and a blast of freezing air came in and struck the warm glass chimney, and it cracked from top to bottom with a sound like a pistol shot. The flame flared and smoked. Hector says he does not remember deciding what to do. His hands simply did it. He turned down the wicks, lifted off the broken chimney with a cloth around his fingers, set the new one in its place, and turned the wicks slowly back up again, ring by ring. The whole thing, the Principal Keeper told him afterward, took less than a minute. Hector says it felt like an hour. And he says that from that night on, he never opened the lantern door again without first glancing at the spare chimney in its rack.

The lantern holds other spares too, if you know where to look. Behind a low cupboard door there are sheets of thick glass, cut to the size of the lantern panes, with putty and tools to set them. These are the storm panes. If a pane were ever broken in a gale, by a stone flung up from the reef, or by a large seabird flying blindly into the light at night, the keeper on watch would fit a storm pane in its place before the wind and the spray could reach the lens. In thirty-one years Alexander has had to do it only twice. But the panes are always there, and always checked, and always ready.

Most of a keeper's night, though, was not about emergencies. It was about small things, done carefully, over and over again. Trimming the wicks at the right times. Watching the oil level. Winding the clockwork. Wiping the mist from the inside of the glass, which forms when the warm air of the lantern meets the cold panes. Writing down the weather. Looking at the flame, and then looking at it again.

Here is a surprising detail. The paraffin burning in this lamp tonight came into Scottish lighthouses after a careful round of tests, and the questions about it went all the way to Parliament. In August of 1871, in the House of Commons in London, a member asked whether mineral oil could safely and cheaply replace colza oil in the lighthouses of Scotland. The answer given was that the Commissioners of Northern Lighthouses had reported favorably on their experiments with mineral oils, and that the government had asked Professor John Tyndall, one of the most famous scientists in Britain, to compare the different oils and report. The fuel in this one lamp, on this one rock, had been weighed and tested by some of the best minds of the age.

The record of that exchange still exists, in the printed debates of Parliament for the eighteenth of August, 1871. It is only a few lines long, a question and an answer, plain and official. It mentions the Northern Commissioners, the mineral oils, and the experiments. It does not mention keepers at all. But it is about them. Every word of that dry little answer ends up here, in a glass chimney on a cold January night, with a young man from Skye learning how to trim the wick.

Historians who study the history of lighting disagree about how safe that change really was. Some point out that paraffin gave a brighter, whiter light, lasted longer, and saved a great deal of money, and that the keepers soon preferred it. Others note that the early mineral oils could be dangerous if they were badly refined, that there were fears of fire and explosion, and that the lighthouse services were wise to test them so slowly. Both are fair. It was a better oil, and it was treated with respect.

Now come and stand in the lantern in the middle of the gale.

The noise is the first thing. It is everywhere, all around you, a huge roaring that seems to come from every side at once and from inside your own chest. The wind leans on the glass, and the panes shiver in their frames with a fine high hum, and every now and then a gust hits so hard that you feel it through your feet. Outside, there is nothing to see but darkness, and the spray, which flies past the glass in long white streaks, lit up silver every time the beam swings round.

And in the middle of it all, inside the lens, the flame is perfectly still.

That is the thing you will remember. Outside, the world is screaming. Inside the lens, the flame stands up tall and white and calm, not flickering, not bending, not even trembling, like a candle in a church on a summer evening. It is the stillest thing you have ever seen.

You watch Hector move about it. He does not hurry. He checks the ventilators at the foot of the lantern, small brass shutters set into the wall, and he closes the ones on the windward side and opens the ones on the lee, so that the air comes in gently and evenly and does not gust. He glances up at the cowl in the roof, where the hot air from the lamp goes out. He looks at the flame again. Then he reaches into the lens with the brass tool and lowers one ring of the burner, very slightly, because it was burning a hair higher than the others.

You put your hand flat against the brass frame of the lantern, low down, away from the lens. It is cold, and it is trembling, very faintly, with the wind. Then you put your other hand against the granite of the wall below the glass, and it is not trembling at all. It is as still as the ground in a field. The storm is in the glass and the iron. It is not in the stone.

You look down through the hatch in the floor, at the lamplit room below, where the journal lies open on its desk. Everything there is quiet and ordinary. A pen in its stand. A clock on the wall, ticking. A coat on a peg. It is very strange, and very comforting, that a room like that can exist only a few feet below a room like this.

Hector moves on to the reservoir that feeds the burner. He checks the level of the oil through a small glass window in its side, and tops it up from one of the cans, pouring slowly through a fine brass funnel with a gauze in it, so that not a speck of grit can reach the wicks. The paraffin glugs softly as it goes in. Its smell fills the lantern, sharp and clean. When he is done he wipes the funnel and the rim of the can with a cloth, and puts the cloth away in its own place, and the can in its own place, and the funnel in its own place. Nothing in the lantern is ever left lying about. Everything has a home.

Through all of it, the gale goes on roaring, and the glass goes on humming, and the beam goes on sweeping round. And the flame goes on standing perfectly still.

Neil, beside him, is watching every move. Hector hands him the tool and lets him lower the next one himself, and puts his hand over Neil's hand to show him how gently. A little. Then a little more. There.

So here, at last, is the answer to our second carry-question. How do you keep a flame steady through a whole winter gale?

You never let the gale touch it. The flame does not live in the wind. It lives inside a glass chimney, inside a cage of glass prisms, inside a room of glass, inside a tower of granite weighing more than four thousand tons, which does not move. Each of those walls takes a little of the storm away, until by the time it reaches the flame there is nothing left of it at all. Then you give the flame exactly the air it needs, no more and no less, through small brass shutters that you open and close by hand as the wind changes. You keep the oil flowing to the wicks at the same steady level, so the flame never starves and never floods. You trim the wicks so they burn evenly. You keep the glass chimney clean and the lantern panes clear of salt and mist, so that the light gets out. And above all, you never stop watching. All night, every night, someone sits beside the flame and looks at it, and the moment it changes, even by a hair, he puts it right. The steadiness is not in the lamp. It is in the man.

Alexander comes up the hatch just after ten. He has come to send Neil to bed, but first he stands and looks at the flame, as he always does, with his hands behind his back. Then he looks at the lantern glass, and sees what Hector has already seen. The spray is coming up so high tonight that salt is beginning to crust on the outside of the panes, and on the windward side the beam is a little dimmer than it should be.

The two men talk for a minute in low voices. It is too wild to go out on the gallery now, and there is no need to. They will clean the glass at first light, when the wind drops, as the look of the sky suggests it will. Until then, they will turn up the burner a little on the lee side, and keep a close eye.

Neil asks how high the spray must be coming, to reach the lantern. Alexander says that on a night like this the sea is breaking a good way up the tower, and the wind is lifting the spray the rest of the way. He has seen it, he says, on other nights, at other stations, come over the top of the lantern like rain.

Neil looks up at the copper dome above him and thinks about the sea coming over it like rain. He finds, to his surprise, that he is not frightened. The tower does not move. The flame does not move. The two older men are calm, and their voices are calm, and their hands are steady.

Before he goes, Neil looks at the flame one more time. He tries to fix it in his memory, the way it stands so tall and white and still, so that he can remember it later, when he is lying in his bunk with the gale outside. Hector sees him looking, and says, in Gaelic, that he will see it every night for the rest of his working life, and that he will never once get tired of it. Neil says he hopes that is true. Hector says it is the only thing about this work he can promise.

Alexander tells him it is time for bed. Neil says goodnight to Hector in Gaelic, and to Alexander in English, and to Bess, who has crept up the stair behind him, in both. Then he goes down through the hatch and down the turning stair, past the room with the journal, past the oil room with its tall cans, to the room where the beds are.

Thomas is already there, asleep, with his face to the curving wall, his letter from Jessie on the shelf beside his head. Neil climbs into his own bed, which is built against the wall like a ship's bunk, with a wooden board along the edge, and pulls the blankets up to his chin. The roaring of the gale is softer here, farther down in the stone. It is just a long, deep sound, far away, like the sea in a shell.

Above him, very far above, the light is turning. He can hear the clockwork, if he holds his breath. Tick and turn. Tick and turn. And with that sound in his ears, and the stone around him, and the flame up there that the wind cannot touch, Neil MacKinnon lies awake for a while in the dark, listening to the light.

## Part Eight — Around Eleven O'Clock: A Poem for a Strong Tower

It is eleven o'clock now, and two of the four men in the tower are asleep, and the gale goes on roaring outside the stone, steady and enormous, while in the small room below the lantern Alexander Grant sits by a lamp of his own with a book open on his knee.

Here is a small question for this part. What does a lighthouse keeper do with the long hours when he is neither on watch nor asleep?

The answer, in most towers, was that he kept his hands busy. There was always work, of course. The brass to polish, the stairs to scrub, the coal to carry, the oil to strain, the journal to write up, the windows to wash. But even with all of that, a winter on a rock station left a man with a great many hours between his watches, and the long dark of January, and nowhere at all to go.

So the keepers made things. They made rag rugs and rope mats for the floors of their cottages ashore, like the one Hector was plaiting earlier. They carved. They built model ships, sometimes so fine that they were kept in families for a hundred years afterward. Some knitted, for knitting was not only women's work in the islands and the fishing towns, and a man who could knit his own stockings had warm feet all winter. They mended their clothes and their boots. They wrote letters. And they read. They read the Bible, and newspapers weeks out of date, and books passed from hand to hand and station to station until the covers fell off.

Thomas is building a ship. It sits on a shelf in the bedroom, half finished, a little brig with her hull carved from a single block of pine and her masts made of knitting needles. When she is done she will go to Jessie, in Anstruther, as a wedding present. Hector has his rope mat. Neil has, so far, nothing of his own, except his letter, and a great many questions. And Alexander has his books.

Newspapers came out with the boat in a bundle, tied with string, a week or two or three old by the time they arrived. Nobody minded. On Tiree and in the islands around it, the paper that most people read was the Oban Times, which had been printed in the little harbor town of Oban since the eighteen sixties, and which carried the news of the Highlands and the islands, the prices of cattle and wool, the comings and goings of steamers, letters from readers in Canada and Australia, and sometimes a poem. The keepers read every page, including the advertisements, and then passed the paper from hand to hand, and then used it to light the kitchen fire. A paper that was three weeks old was still news, out here. Everything was news, out here.

There were books too, borrowed and lent and swapped, so that a novel might go from a cottage at Hynish to the rock and back, and then to another station on the next posting, and come back years later with someone else's name written inside the cover. Some keepers studied in those long hours. Men taught themselves navigation, or mathematics, or another language. Alexander once knew an assistant keeper, at a station in Orkney, who learned enough Latin in four winters to read Virgil, and who would recite it to the gulls from the gallery on calm mornings.

So the answer to our small question is simple. In the hours between the watches, he makes something with his hands, or he reads, and in that way the winter passes, one quiet evening at a time.

If you had spent a winter out here, you would have found your own way to fill those hours too. You might have learned to knit, or to carve, or to read a whole shelf of books you had always meant to read. You would have found, as the keepers did, that your hands grow restless in the long dark, and that they are happiest when they have something to make.

Tonight Alexander is reading a book of poems. It is a slim volume, published three years ago, in 1887, and it was sent out to him by his wife, Margaret, who bought it in Oban. It is called Underwoods, and it is by Robert Louis Stevenson.

Here is a surprising detail. The man who wrote it is, tonight, as far from this rock as it is possible to be. Robert Louis Stevenson, the nephew of the man who built Skerryvore, the boy who was meant to become a lighthouse engineer, is on the other side of the world. He arrived in Samoa, in the South Pacific, only a few weeks ago, a thin, ill, restless man of thirty-nine, with his wife, Fanny, and his stepson, Lloyd. Soon he will buy land on a green hillside above the harbor of Apia, and build a house there, and never come home to Scotland again. But he never forgot the lighthouses. Five years ago, when he and his wife took a house in Bournemouth, on the south coast of England, he named it Skerryvore, after this tower.

And in Underwoods there is a short poem with that same name. He wrote it for the lintel of his house, the beam of wood or stone above the door. It begins with these words.

For love of lovely words, and for the sake of those, my kinsmen and my countrymen.

The poem goes on to speak of the men of his family who toiled early and late in the windy ocean, building towers on bare rocks where only seals and seabirds had lived before. They did it, he wrote, to plant a star for seamen. And he said that he was putting the name of their strong tower over his own door, in honor of them.

Alexander reads it twice. Then he takes it up the stair to the lantern, and reads it to Hector, over the sound of the wind.

Hector listens with his head on one side. When it is done, he says it is a fine thing, to plant a star for seamen. That is just what it is, he says. And then he says something in Gaelic that Alexander does not catch, and when Alexander asks, he says it was only that his father would have liked to hear it.

After a while, while Alexander is still standing in the lantern, Hector begins to sing, very softly, under the noise of the wind. It is a psalm, in Gaelic, one of the old slow tunes that the islands have sung for generations, the kind that rises and falls and wanders like the wind over the machair. He does not sing it loudly. He sings it the way a man sings to a child who is nearly asleep. Alexander does not have the Gaelic, not really, after all his years in the west. But he knows the tune. He stands quite still and listens until it is finished, looking out through the glass at the dark.

Historians and biographers of Robert Louis Stevenson have long disagreed about his feelings for the family trade. Some say he always hated engineering, that he trained in it only to please his father, and that he escaped into writing as soon as he could. Others point out that he spent whole summers as a young man at the lighthouse works on the west coast, that he loved the sea and the islands and the men who worked there, and that the islands and the wrecks and the lighthouses came back again and again in his books. Both are fair. He did not want to build towers. But he never stopped loving them.

In fact, he had stayed not far from here. In 1870, when he was nineteen and still studying to be an engineer, he spent some weeks on the little tidal island of Erraid, off the Ross of Mull, where the family firm had a work yard for the building of another lighthouse, out on a rock called Dubh Artach. Years later he put Erraid into his novel Kidnapped, and shipwrecked his hero David Balfour there. And in two summers' time, the Board will move the keepers' families of Skerryvore to that very same island. Neil does not know any of this tonight. But one day, perhaps, he will live in the cottages on Erraid himself, and look out at the same sea.

Hector, alone again in the lantern after Alexander goes down, sits with his hands on his knees and listens to the gale. He thinks about his father, standing at the foot of the ladder all those years ago, looking up. He thinks about Mòrag, who is learning to say lighthouse, and says it as if it were all one long soft word in Gaelic. Then he gets up and trims a wick.

Now let us go down to the bedroom, where Thomas and Neil are sleeping.

You come down the stair quietly, keeping one hand on the curving wall. The room is dark, except for the faintest glow from the stair above, where Alexander has left his lamp. It is round, like every room in the tower, and the beds are built against the wall, one above another in places, curving with it, like the bunks in a ship's cabin. Each has a wooden board along its edge to keep a sleeper from rolling out. The air smells of wool and of the sea, and very faintly of pine shavings from Thomas's little brig on the shelf.

You can hear the gale here, but it is softer, farther away, a deep round sound that seems to come up from the stone itself. You can hear the two sleepers breathing. Thomas breathes slow and deep, with a little catch at the end of each breath, almost a snore but not quite. Neil breathes lightly and quickly, the way the young do, and every so often he murmurs something in his sleep, in Gaelic, and turns over.

On the shelf by Thomas's bed, the little brig sits in the dark, her knitting-needle masts just catching the faint light from the stair. Someone has tucked a scrap of sailcloth into her rigging, the beginning of a mainsail, neatly hemmed. On the shelf by Neil's bed there is a comb, a small Bible his mother gave him, and a smooth white stone from the shore at Elgol, which he brought with him from home and keeps where he can touch it in the night.

You sit down on the edge of an empty bed, very quietly. The blanket under your hand is thick and rough, gray wool, the sort that is woven on Tiree and in the islands and lasts for a lifetime. The wall beside you is cold granite, but the blankets are warm. You can feel, through the stone, a faint long trembling every time a big sea comes in against the rock, far below, and then a pause, and then another. It is not frightening. It is like being on a ship at anchor, rocked very gently, on a rough night, in a harbor you trust.

Up the stair, faint and steady, the clockwork turns the light. Tick and turn.

Down in the room below the lantern, before he goes to bed, Alexander writes up the journal for the evening. He writes the wind, west, a whole gale. He writes the sea, very heavy. He writes that the light has burned well, and that salt has formed on the lantern glass on the windward side, and that the burner has been adjusted. He writes it all plainly, in the same few words that keepers have always used, because the journal is not a place for feelings. It is a place for facts. Then he blots the page, closes the book, and sets the pen back in its stand.

Under the facts, though, if you knew how to read them, there is a whole night. A whole gale. Four men and a cat, and a light that did not fail. Alexander knows that. He has read old journals at other stations, written by men long dead, and he has always felt that he could hear the wind in them.

Alexander comes down at half past eleven to go to bed himself. He has a long rest ahead of him, until four, when he will take the morning watch, and he means to use it. But first he stops by Neil's bed and looks at the boy for a moment, sleeping with one arm flung over his face and his letter to his mother tucked under his pillow. He thinks of his own son, grown now and married, working in a shipyard on the Clyde. He was this age once too, and far from home, and full of questions.

He pulls Neil's blanket back up over his shoulder, where it has slipped. Neil does not wake.

Then Alexander climbs into his own bed, against the curve of the wall, and takes off his spectacles and folds them and puts them on the shelf, beside his book of poems. He lies on his back for a while, with his hands folded on his chest, listening to the gale. He is not worried about it. He has heard a great many gales, in thirty-one years, in towers up and down the coast. This one is loud, but it is only weather, and weather passes. He says a short prayer in his head, for Margaret, and for his son, and for Thomas's Jessie, and for Neil's mother on Skye, and for Hector's three, and for the little one in the blue dress. And then, because he is a practical man, he says one more, for the ships out there on the dark sea tonight, that they will see the light.

Then he closes his eyes. And above him, through the floors of stone, the light goes on turning, a star planted for seamen, sending its long beam out into the gale.

## Part Nine — Around Midnight: The Weight of the Sea

Midnight comes, and with it the change of watch, and Thomas Rennie climbs up through the hatch into the lantern with his coat buttoned to the chin and his hair still flattened on one side from the pillow, while the gale reaches the very top of its strength outside.

Here is a small question for this part. When one of these great Atlantic waves strikes the foot of the tower, how hard does it actually hit?

Hector hands over the watch the way it is always done. The flame, the oil, the wicks, the clockwork, the ventilators, the glass. He tells Thomas about the salt crusting on the windward panes, and the ring of the burner he turned up a little on the lee side to make up for it. He tells him the wind has been steady from the west for two hours and has not yet begun to back. Thomas listens, nods, looks at everything himself, and then says that he has the watch.

Hector goes down. He is asleep, Thomas will later swear, before his head has properly touched the pillow.

Now, for a long time, nobody knew how hard the sea could strike. Sailors and engineers knew it was a great deal. They had seen the evidence, huge stones moved about on breakwaters, iron bars bent, whole piers torn away. But nobody had ever measured it. And then, while Skerryvore was being finished, Alan Stevenson's younger brother Thomas decided to try.

Thomas Stevenson, the father of Robert Louis, was the most inventive member of that inventive family. In 1843 he designed a small instrument he called a marine dynamometer, a word that simply means force measurer. It was a sturdy iron cylinder with a round plate at one end, held out by a strong spring. When a wave struck the plate, it pushed the plate in against the spring, and the plate pushed a set of small leather rings along rods inside the cylinder. When the wave fell back, the plate sprang out again, but the leather rings stayed where they had been pushed. Afterward, a man could come along, measure how far the rings had moved, and work out from that the greatest force the wave had struck with.

He fixed his instruments to the rocks at Skerryvore, and at the Bell Rock, and at other places around the coast, and he read them through the summers and winters of 1843 and 1844, and on into the following year.

Here is the answer to our small question, and it is a surprising one. Across those first two years at Skerryvore, the average force of the waves in summer was a little over six hundred pounds on every square foot of the plate. In winter, the average was more than three times that, about two thousand and eighty-six pounds on every square foot. And then, in March of 1845, during a great westerly gale, the instrument at Skerryvore recorded a force of six thousand and eighty-three pounds on a single square foot. That is close to three tons, pressing on a patch of rock no bigger than a dinner plate.

Thomas Stevenson set out his results in a paper for the Royal Society of Edinburgh, which he called an account of experiments upon the force of the waves of the Atlantic and German Oceans. The German Ocean was the old name for the North Sea. The paper was printed in the society's Transactions in the eighteen forties, and it was read all over the world by engineers who were trying to build harbors and lighthouses that would stand. For the first time, they had numbers to build against. And the number that impressed them most came from this rock, on a March night, in a gale very like the one blowing now.

The tower's own shape helps it too. If you could stand back and look at Skerryvore from the side, you would see that its wall is not straight. It curves, broad and sweeping at the foot, narrowing gracefully toward the top, like the trunk of an oak where it meets the ground. Alan Stevenson chose that curve with great care, working it out on paper with long calculations. At the bottom, where the sea strikes hardest, the tower is wide and massive, and its curving face turns the force of a wave upward and aside, so that the water climbs the wall and falls back rather than striking it square. Higher up, where only spray and wind can reach, the tower can afford to be slimmer, and lighter, and there is room inside for the rooms where men live.

And then there is that solid base, twenty-six feet of granite with no rooms in it at all, no door, no window, nothing hollow for the sea to find. The door into the tower is set high above it, at the top of the metal ladder, well above the reach of all but the very greatest seas. Everything precious, the men, the food, the coal, the oil and the light, is kept above the reach of the water.

Historians of engineering have disagreed about how much those famous readings really mean. Some treat them as one of the great early measurements of the power of the sea, and they still appear in books today. Others point out that the little instrument measured only the hardest blow of a wave, at one instant, on one small plate, and that a large wall feels something rather different, spread out and softened over a broad surface. Both are fair. The readings were real. But the tower itself, with its curving walls and its enormous weight, was designed precisely so that it would never have to take that blow all at once.

Now come and stand with Thomas in the lantern at the height of the storm.

It is very loud. That is the first thing, still, and it will be the first thing for another hour yet. The wind has reached the steady, endless roar of a gale at its full strength, and the glass shivers, and the copper dome above you rings very faintly, like a bell struck far away. But the floor beneath your feet does not move. You keep waiting for it to, and it does not.

Every so often, a really great sea comes in out of the west. You feel it before you hear it. A long, deep shudder rises up through the tower from the rock below, as if the whole column of granite has drawn one slow breath. Then comes the sound, a deep boom, muffled by a hundred and fifty feet of stone, and a second or two later the spray arrives, flung up the side of the tower by the wind, rattling against the windward panes like a handful of gravel.

And then it passes, and the tower is still again, and the flame inside the lens has not so much as flickered.

Thomas sits on his stool by the lens with his hands in his pockets. He has the look of a man sitting in his own kitchen at home. Now and then he leans forward and peers at the flame, and once he takes the scissors and trims one wick, very lightly, with a small neat snip. Then he sits back.

You notice the weight of the clockwork, hanging on its chain, a little lower now than when the watch began. At the right moment Thomas stands up, takes hold of the winding handle, and turns it, steadily, round and round, until the weight has been drawn back up to the top again. You hear the ratchet clicking as he winds. Then he lets go, and the lens turns on, smooth and even, as if nothing had happened at all. He sits down again.

Thomas has kept the middle watch on nights like this for nine winters now, at three different stations, and he has his own small habits for it. He keeps his pipe in his pocket, unlit, because there is no smoking near the lamp, and he likes to hold it. He hums under his breath, old Fife fishing songs his father sang in the boatyard. And every hour, on the hour, he gets up and walks once slowly round the whole lantern, looking at every pane of glass, every fitting, every bolt, as if he were walking round a boat on the slip before a launch. Nothing ever needs doing. He does it anyway.

Bess has come up to keep him company. She sits on the floor between his boots, washing one paw with great attention, apparently quite unaware that the Atlantic is throwing itself at the building she lives in. Thomas says to her, quite seriously, that she has more sense than most of the people he knows.

You go and stand by the glass on the lee side, where the spray does not reach, and look out. At first there is only blackness. Then the beam swings round behind you and out over the sea, and for one long moment you can see it all. The whole reef, white from end to end, every rock buried in foam, the breakers rising and toppling and spreading in great slow sheets of white, and the spray streaming away to the east on the wind like smoke from a chimney. Then the beam moves on, and the dark comes back, and you are left with the picture of it printed on your eyes, glowing faintly, until the beam comes round again and paints it fresh.

It is the wildest thing you have ever seen. And you are watching it from a warm room, with a cat at your feet and a man beside you who is calmly trimming a wick. You find that you are not afraid at all. You are only full of wonder.

Here is the thing you begin to understand, sitting there in the noise. The storm is outside, and it is enormous. But it is outside. All that force, all those tons of water striking the rock, are meeting granite blocks locked together so tightly that the sea cannot find a way in. The wind is meeting glass set in iron and thick walls. The cold is meeting a coal fire in the kitchen, and wool blankets on the beds. And the darkness is meeting the light. Everything in this tower has been made, by careful people, to stand exactly between the storm and the men inside it.

Thomas tells Bess, because there is no one else awake to tell, about the boatyard at Anstruther on nights like this. When he was a boy, he says, his father would sit up through the big gales, listening, with his boots on, in case one of the boats in the harbor broke loose from her moorings and had to be saved. And every time the wind roared, the boy Thomas would lie in bed and think that this must be the worst storm there had ever been in the world. And then in the morning the boats would all be there, rocking quietly on the gray water, and his father would be asleep in his chair by the fire with his boots still on, and the gulls would be calling over the harbor as if nothing had happened at all.

He says that is the thing about a storm. It always feels like the worst one. And in the morning there are always the gulls.

Bess, who has heard all of this before, purrs.

Down in the bedroom, three men are sleeping. Alexander on his back with his hands folded, as if he were in church. Hector on his side, with his face to the wall, breathing deep. Neil curled in a ball, his letter to his mother still under his pillow. The gale goes on roaring, and none of them wakes. They are used to it already, even Neil, after only a few weeks. The sound has become part of the tower, like the ticking of the clockwork and the hiss of the kettle on the range.

At about one o'clock, Thomas notices something. The roar outside has changed. Not much. It is still a gale, still loud, still flinging spray against the glass. But the note of it has dropped a little, and the gusts have begun to come further apart. He stands up, goes close to the glass on the north side, and peers out into the dark, cupping his hands around his eyes against the glow of the lens.

He cannot see anything yet. But he has been a keeper for nine years, and a boatbuilder's son for thirty, and he knows a gale that has reached its top and begun to tire.

He sits back down. He takes the small notebook from his pocket in which he keeps his notes for the journal, and he writes, in pencil, by the light of the lens, the time, and beside it a few short words. Wind west, strong gale. Beginning to ease.

Then he puts the notebook away. Bess climbs up onto his knee, turns around three times, and lies down. Thomas lets her stay. And beside them both, in its cage of glass, the light turns on through the last hard hour of the storm, its beam sweeping out over the white and roaring sea, every sweep exactly like the one before.

## Part Ten — Around Two in the Morning: The Moon Comes Up

A little before two o'clock, the clouds in the east begin to come apart, and through a long ragged gap, away to the right of the low dark line of Tiree, a half moon rises out of the sea, pale and tilted, and lays a broken silver path across the water toward the tower.

Here is a small question for this part. What does this tower look like tonight, to a sailor far out on the dark water?

Neil has come up the stair at two, as Alexander told him to, rubbing his eyes, his hair standing up at the back. He was asleep, deeply asleep, when the little brass alarm clock on his shelf began to rattle, and for a moment he did not know where he was. Then he heard the clockwork, and remembered.

Now he stands beside Thomas in the lantern, looking out at the moon. The wind has dropped a great deal in the last hour. It is still strong, still blowing from the west, and the sea is still heavy, breaking white across the reef. But the roar has gone out of it. It is only a loud wind now, the kind that whistles and moans, the kind you might hear around a farmhouse on Skye on a winter night.

Thomas answers the small question for him. Out there, he says, pointing west, toward the open Atlantic, there may be a ship tonight, coming in from America, or out of the Clyde, or up from Ireland, riding out the end of the gale. Her captain does not see the tower at all. He sees only the light. A flash, bright and white, low on the horizon. Then darkness. Then another flash. He counts the seconds between the flashes, and he looks at his chart, and he knows at once that this is Skerryvore, and that the reef is there, and that he must keep well off it. That is all the tower is, to him. A pattern of light in the dark, saying here I am, keep away, and go safely home.

Here is a surprising detail. The idea of a light on this reef is much older than the tower. Alan Stevenson's father, Robert, first landed on Skerryvore in 1804, eighty-six years before our night, and he urged the Board to put a light there. Ten years later, in the summer of 1814, he came back to the reef in the Board's own yacht, with a party of the Commissioners, and with a guest. The guest was Walter Scott, the poet and novelist, already one of the most famous writers in Europe.

Scott kept a diary of that voyage. Of Skerryvore, he wrote that it was a long ridge of rocks, chiefly under water, on which the tide breaks in a most tremendous style. He was not a man who was easily impressed by the sea. But the reef impressed him.

And then, for more than twenty years, nothing happened. The reef was too far out, the rock too small, the sea too heavy, and the cost too great. It was not until 1838 that Alan Stevenson's men first landed with their tools.

Scott enjoyed that voyage enormously. He sailed right round the north of Scotland in the lighthouse yacht that summer, to Shetland and Orkney and down through the Hebrides, and he filled his diary with seals and caves and castles and the talk of sailors. Robert Stevenson was his guide to the lighthouses, and the two men, very different, got on well. Not long afterward Scott began a long poem of the islands, called The Lord of the Isles, full of the seas and the places he had passed. It is pleasant to think that a little of the wild water around Skerryvore went into it.

Historians disagree a little about why it took so long. Some say the Commissioners were simply frightened of the cost, which in the end came to tens of thousands of pounds, a fortune for that time. Others say the engineers of the day were not yet sure such a tower could be built at all, so far out in the Atlantic, and that it needed Robert Stevenson's long experience at the Bell Rock, and a new generation to carry it, before anyone dared to try. Both may be true. It was a frightening thing to attempt. And it was a frightening sum to spend.

Now come out onto the gallery with Neil and Thomas, because the wind has dropped enough at last to step outside.

Thomas opens the narrow door, and the air comes in, cold and wet and clean. You step out after him onto the iron gallery. The wind is still strong, so you hold the rail, but it no longer pushes you. It just streams past, smelling of salt and of something else, something fresh and wide, like the smell of rain on a field.

Above you, the sky has opened. Not all of it. The clouds are still racing overhead, tattered and silver-edged, but between them there are wide spaces of clear black sky, and in those spaces there are stars, more stars than you have ever seen, thick and bright and very close. Low in the east, off to the right of the dark line of Tiree, the half moon hangs, white and calm. Its light lies on the water in a long, shaking path, all the way across the sea to the foot of the tower.

Below you, the sea is still breaking on the reef, but you can see it now, by moonlight. It is not frightening. It is beautiful. Long white lines of foam spreading and shining on the black water, and the rocks of the reef showing dark between them, and the spray rising in slow white plumes and falling again.

And behind you, through the glass, the light turns. Every few moments its beam swings out over your head, a great soft blade of white, reaching away across the sea toward the west, toward the ships, toward the open ocean, and then it swings away, and comes round again.

You lean on the rail and breathe. After the long hours of the gale, the air out here feels enormous, and clean, and very cold, like a drink of water from a hill spring. Your breath goes up in a small white cloud and is whisked away. Somewhere below, out on the reef, you can hear a seal calling, a long wavering cry, half like a dog and half like a child singing. Then another answers it, farther off. The seals have been sheltering on the lee side of the rocks all through the storm, and now they are telling one another that it is over.

Thomas hears them too. He says that Hector once told him the old people on Tiree say the seals are singing, and that some of them used to say the seals were people once, long ago, who went into the sea and never quite forgot the land. He does not say whether he believes it. He just listens.

Thomas takes a soft cloth from his pocket and begins, carefully, to wipe the salt from the outside of the windward panes, as far as he can reach. Neil watches, and then takes another cloth and begins on the next pane. They work slowly, in the moonlight, side by side, in silence. Under the cloth, the crust of salt comes away, and the glass behind it is clear again, and the light shines through brighter.

Then they go back inside and close the door. The lantern is warm after the gallery, and smells of paraffin and brass, and the flame stands in the lens as still as ever.

Neil looks at the half moon for a long time. It hangs low in the southeast, a little way off from Tiree, as if it had paused there to look across at the cottage windows. He thinks of Catriona and the children, and Margaret, and the little house with the signal tower beside it, all asleep down there under that same pale light. He thinks that if any of them woke now and looked out to sea, they would see the beam of Skerryvore sweeping across the water toward them, and they would know that someone was awake out here, keeping watch. The thought pleases him very much. He decides it is the kind of thing he will put in his next letter home.

Thomas shows Neil how to wind the clockwork. He lets him do it himself, this time, with his own hands on the handle, turning it slowly and evenly, feeling the weight rise on its chain, hearing the ratchet click. Not too fast, Thomas says. Not too slow. Like rowing. There.

Neil lets go. The lens turns on, smooth and steady. He looks up at Thomas, and Thomas nods.

Before they sit down, Thomas sends Neil down the stair to the kitchen, because the keeper on watch does not leave the light, and a few minutes later Neil comes back up, carefully, with a tin can of tea that Thomas set to stew at the back of the range at midnight, and two thick oatcakes wrapped in a cloth. The oatcakes are from Catriona's last batch, a little hard now, but good, with a scrape of butter on each. Thomas pours the tea into two enamel mugs, black and strong, and hands one to Neil. They hold the mugs in both hands for the warmth, and blow on them, and drink.

Neil says it is the best tea he has ever tasted. Thomas says that every keeper says that about the tea in the small hours, at every station on the coast, and that it is always true.

They sit down, side by side, on the two stools by the lens. Neil asks Thomas about Jessie. And Thomas, who does not often talk about such things, tells him. They have known each other since they were children, he says. Her father has a boat in Anstruther harbor, a fishing boat, and Thomas's father built it. She is the best singer in the kirk choir and the worst cook in the whole of Fife, and he means to marry her in April, in the old church above the harbor, with half the town there. He has written to the Board to ask for a mainland station after the wedding, somewhere with a cottage beside the light, so that she can live with him. He does not know if they will say yes. But he has hopes.

Neil asks what he will do with the little brig when she is finished.

Thomas says he will give her to Jessie on the morning of the wedding. He will put her on the windowsill of their cottage, wherever it is, so that she can look out at the sea.

Neil says it is a good plan. Thomas agrees that it is the best plan he has ever had.

After a while, Neil asks Thomas whether he will miss the rock, when he has his mainland station and his cottage and his Jessie. Thomas thinks about it for a long time, longer than Neil expected. Then he says yes, he thinks he will. Not the gales, and not the salt meat, and not the stairs. But this. The small hours. The moon on the water. The light turning, and nobody else awake in the whole wide sea but you and the men on the ships. He says there is nothing else in the world quite like it, and that Neil will find that out for himself, in a winter or two, and then he will understand why the old keepers never really want to stop.

Neil says he thinks he understands already. Thomas looks at him sideways, and smiles, and says that perhaps he does.

Then they are quiet for a while. The wind moans softly around the lantern. The moon climbs a little higher in the east. Bess, who has been sleeping in the corner on a folded curtain, gets up, stretches, walks across the floor, and settles herself on Neil's feet.

You sit there with them, on the floor by the lens, with your back against the cool granite and your knees drawn up. The lantern is warm. The flame is bright and still. Every few moments the beam passes out over the sea, and the light inside the lantern shifts very slightly, a soft brightening and dimming, like breathing. Through the glass you can see the moon and the stars and the long silver path on the water. Your mug of tea is warm in your hands. Your eyes are heavy.

There is no hurry about anything now. The gale is going. The sea is settling. The men are calm, and the light is lit, and the night has turned, the way a tide turns, so quietly that you cannot say the moment it happened. From here on, everything is going toward the morning.

Down below, the two sleepers sleep on. Alexander, on his back, his hands folded, his spectacles on the shelf beside his book of poems. Hector on his side, his face to the wall, his breathing slow and deep. The tower around them is quiet now, almost still, only a faint low trembling now and then as a sea comes in against the rock, softer than before.

And up in the lantern, between the young man from Skye and the young man from Fife, the light goes on turning, white and steady, its beam sweeping out over the moonlit sea, while the last of the gale goes moaning softly away to the east.

## Part Eleven — Around Four in the Morning: The Boat That Will Come

At four o'clock, Alexander Grant climbs up through the hatch into the lantern, wide awake and neatly dressed, his beard combed, his spectacles on, to take the morning watch, and he finds the wind fallen to a fresh breeze and the moon climbing higher and whiter in the southeast.

Here is a small question for this part, and it is the last of our three carry-questions too. What happens when the relief boat cannot come?

Neil, still half asleep, stands by the hatch and watches Alexander make his checks, one after another, in the same order as always. He is beginning to know that order by heart. Flame first. Then oil. Then wicks. Then the weight on its chain. Then the glass, all the way round. Then, last of all, a long look out at the sea.

Alexander takes the watch the way it is always done. He looks at the flame, the oil, the wicks, the clockwork, the glass. He sees that the windward panes have been wiped clean, and he nods. Thomas tells him the gale reached its top a little before one and has been easing ever since, and that the sea is going down. Alexander looks out at the moonlit reef, where the breakers are lower now and slower, and says that if it holds like this, the boat may well come out tomorrow, or the day after.

And that, really, is the answer to our third question. When the relief boat cannot come, the men simply wait.

Nothing breaks. Nothing is lost. The light is lit at sunset as always, and put out at sunrise as always, and the journal is written up as always, with two plain words at the end of the day's entry. No boat. The man who was due to go ashore stays on, and takes his turn at the watches, and his month on the rock becomes a month and a few days. The letters wait in the men's pockets. The families ashore, at Hynish, see from the signal tower that the flag is flying on the gallery, and know that all is well.

And the men do not go hungry, because this was always planned for. A rock station like this one was stocked with far more food, water, coal and oil than four men could use in a single month, precisely because everyone knew that in winter the sea might close the way for days or weeks. There are sacks of oatmeal in the storeroom, and barrels of salt meat and ship's biscuit, and tins of condensed milk, and tea, and sugar, and potatoes, and casks of water stacked in the lower rooms of the tower, and coal enough for weeks of fires, and oil enough for the light to burn far into the spring.

In the earliest years, before the Board had learned how long a winter could keep a boat in harbor, men on far stations did sometimes find their stores running low, and people in the islands still remember stories of the supply boat that could not get out to the rock. But by our night, every keeper on the coast knew the rule. You did not count on the boat. You counted on the store.

Even in the building years, this was understood. A report from the works, printed in a Scottish newspaper in 1842, described how a short spell of fair weather had not lasted long enough to make the passage out to the rock in daylight. The men waited in harbor, patiently, for a better day. That was simply how the reef was.

So the boat that did not come today will come when the sea allows. Thomas will go home to Fife, a few days late, with his letter and his little brig. Neil's letter will go to his mother. Catriona will send out more bread. And Alexander will write the date and the time in the journal, and the words, boat arrived, and that will be the end of it.

People who have written about the lives of keepers have never quite agreed about how the waiting felt. Some describe it as one of the hardest parts of the work, the days of looking out at an empty sea, wondering about home. Others, and many of the keepers themselves among them, remembered it quite differently, as a kind of peace, a few extra days with nothing to do but keep the light and let the world go on without them. Probably both were true, for different men, on different days. Tonight, in this tower, it feels much more like the second.

Thomas yawns. Alexander tells him to go to bed, and to take the boy with him.

They go down through the hatch together, Thomas first, then Neil, then Bess, who has decided that she will sleep with Neil tonight.

Now let us go down with them, slowly, into the quiet.

You come down the turning stair, one step at a time, with your hand on the cool smooth wall. The tower is very quiet now. The roar has gone. There is only the soft wash of the sea far below, and a faint whisper of wind at the narrow windows, and up above you, growing fainter as you go down, the tick and turn of the clockwork.

You pass the room where the journal lies, closed now, on its desk, with the pen in its stand. You pass the oil room, with its tall cans standing in a row, gleaming faintly. You pass the storeroom door, and behind it, you know, are the sacks and barrels and tins, enough for weeks, stacked neatly in the dark. Each room is a little cooler than the one above, and a little quieter. The stone of the stair is worn smooth in the middle of each step, by nearly fifty years of keepers' boots going up and down, up and down, every night.

The light from Thomas's little hand lamp moves ahead of you along the curving wall, and your shadow moves with it, long and soft. Bess pads down in front of you all, one step at a time, her tail held high, as if she were showing the way.

The bedroom is dark and warm. You can hear Hector breathing, slow and deep, on his side by the wall. You can hear Alexander's empty bed, which is to say you can hear nothing at all from it, only the soft settled quiet of a bed someone has just left.

Across the room, Hector sighs in his sleep and turns over, and murmurs a few words in Gaelic, soft and blurred, and is quiet again. Perhaps he is talking to Mòrag. Perhaps he is on the shore at Balemartine, on a summer evening, with the machair full of flowers and the sea as flat as glass. Whatever it is, it is a good dream. You can tell from his breathing, which is slow and deep and easy, the breathing of a man with nothing at all to worry about.

Thomas takes off his boots and his coat and climbs into his bunk. He lies on his back for a moment, looking up into the dark. He thinks of Jessie, and the church above the harbor in April, and the brig on the windowsill. He turns onto his side, toward the wall, and pulls the blanket up, and his breathing slows, and deepens, and catches a little at the end, almost a snore but not quite. He is asleep.

Thomas's breathing is the slowest in the room now. One arm hangs a little over the edge of the bunk, his fingers loose and curled. On the shelf above his head, the little brig sits with her one small sail, waiting for April, and the letter from Jessie lies folded beside her, soft at the creases from so much reading.

Neil climbs into his own bed. Bess jumps up after him and walks along the blanket and settles herself in the hollow behind his knees, warm and heavy, purring very faintly. Neil lies with his eyes open in the dark for a while.

He thinks about the light. He thinks about the flame, standing so still inside its cage of glass while the whole Atlantic roared outside. He thinks about winding the clockwork with his own hands, and the weight rising on its chain, and Thomas nodding. He thinks about the moon over Tiree, and the stars over the gallery, and the salt coming away from the glass under his cloth.

He thinks about his mother on Skye. She will be asleep now, in the little house above the shore, with the peat fire banked down and glowing. He thinks that perhaps she is dreaming of him. He thinks that he will tell her, in his next letter, about the moon.

Neil turns onto his side, toward the wall, the way Hector sleeps. He puts his hand out and finds the smooth white stone from Elgol on the shelf, and holds it for a moment, and puts it back. The blanket is heavy and warm. The pillow smells faintly of the sea. Bess shifts behind his knees, and settles again, and her purring slows until it is almost too soft to hear.

He thinks, drowsily, that tomorrow he will ask Alexander whether he may light the lamp himself, one calm evening soon. He thinks that he will count to forty between the rings, in Gaelic, just to make Thomas laugh. He smiles in the dark at the thought.

His eyes close. They open again. They close.

You sit on the edge of the empty bed beside him, very quietly. The blanket is warm and rough under your hand. The stone wall is cool. The air smells of wool and salt and paraffin, and, very faintly, of pine shavings, from the little brig on the shelf. Somewhere far below, the sea comes in against the rock, softly now, and goes out, and comes in, like breathing.

You let your own breathing slow to match theirs. In, slowly. Out, slowly. The sea below does the same, washing in against the rock, drawing back. The clockwork far above ticks and turns, ticks and turns, so faint now that you are not sure whether you are hearing it or only remembering it.

The stone around you is thick and old and kind. It has stood here for nearly half a century, through more gales than anyone could count, and it will stand here long after tonight. Nothing can reach you in here. The wind is outside. The sea is outside. The dark is outside. In here there is only warmth, and quiet, and the slow breathing of sleepers, and the faint smell of wool and salt and pine.

Your eyes are very heavy now. You can let them close.

There is nothing you need to do. Nothing you need to watch. Alexander is up in the lantern, and the light is lit, and it will be lit until morning. All you have to do is listen to the quiet, and let it settle over you, like a blanket.

Neil is asleep now. His breathing is slow and even. One hand lies open on the blanket, near Bess, and Bess has put her chin on it.

All through the tower, now, the quiet is settling, room by room, from the top down. In the kitchen, the fire in the range has burned low, and its embers glow softly red behind the bars, and the kettle sits on the side of the stove, still warm, ready for the morning. In the room below the lantern, the clock ticks on the wall. On the stair, a faint draft moves, cool and slow, smelling of stone. Outside, the wind has dropped to a breeze, and the sea has grown long and slow and gentle, washing up against the rock and drawing back, washing up and drawing back, like someone breathing in their sleep.

Everyone who needs to sleep is sleeping now. And everyone who needs to keep watch is keeping it.

And far above them all, Alexander Grant sits by the lens in the lantern, with his hands folded in his lap and the moonlight on the glass, and the light turns, steady and quiet, sending its long soft beam out over the calming sea.

## Part Twelve — Toward Eight in the Morning: The First Gray Light

The hours before dawn pass slowly and softly, and the moon climbs slowly round into the south, and the sea around the rock grows quieter with every turn of the light.

Here is a small question for this last part. What does a keeper think about, alone in the lantern, in the last quiet hours of a winter night?

Alexander sits on his stool beside the lens. He is not tired. He slept well, and deeply, and the morning watch has always been the one he likes best. The work of the night is done. The gale has gone. All that is left is to keep the light, and to wait for the sun.

He looks at the flame, now and then. He trims a wick, once, very lightly. When the weight on its chain has sunk low, he stands and winds the clockwork, slowly, evenly, the way he has done it every night for thirty-one years, and then he sits down again.

And he thinks. He thinks about very simple things. He thinks about Margaret, asleep in the cottage at Hynish, and the kettle she will put on when she wakes. He thinks about the garden behind the cottage, which is only a strip of sandy ground, but in which, last summer, she grew the best potatoes on Tiree. He thinks about his son on the Clyde, and his son's small daughter, whom he has met only twice. He thinks about the book of poems on the shelf below, and the words about planting a star for seamen, and he decides that he likes them.

He thinks, too, about the day he married Margaret, in the little church at Portsoy, more than thirty years ago now, on a bright windy morning in May. The fishing boats in the harbor had flown flags for them. Her father, who did not approve of lighthouse keepers, because he thought they were neither proper seamen nor proper landsmen, came round in the end, and danced at the wedding, badly, and told everyone afterward that it was the finest day of his life. Margaret had laughed so hard at his dancing that she had to sit down.

Alexander smiles at the memory, there in the lantern, all alone, with nobody to see. Then he looks at the flame again, and it is just as it should be.

That is the answer to our small question. In the last hours of a winter night, a keeper thinks about home. And then he looks at the flame again.

Out in the south, the half moon hangs over the sea, white and small and calm, and its light lies across the water in a long soft path that rocks gently on the swell. All the clouds have gone now. The stars are very clear, and they shine down on the quiet water in their thousands, and the moon sails slowly among them, in no hurry at all.

Alan Stevenson, in his book, wrote of the first night this light was shown, on the first of February, 1844. Every night since then, someone has sat where Alexander sits now, beside the flame, until morning. Alexander thinks of all those nights, one after another, like the pages of the journal on the desk below. Tonight is one more page. He is glad it is a quiet one.

Now come and sit with him, for the last little while of the night.

You are on the second stool, beside the lens. The lantern is warm. The flame is steady. Its light falls across your hands and your knees, soft and white. Beyond the glass the sea is calm now, or nearly calm, a long low swell rolling gently in from the west, and breaking quietly on the reef, without hurry.

You can hear the clockwork. Tick and turn. Tick and turn. You can hear the faint hush of the sea. You can hear Alexander breathing, slow and easy, beside you. And that is all.

The lantern is very peaceful now. The glass has stopped humming. The wind outside is only a soft breath, now and then, against the panes. The great lens turns beside you, slowly, smoothly, its brass frame gleaming, its prisms full of light, and the faint sound of its turning is like the sound of a spinning wheel in a quiet kitchen, far away. You can feel the warmth of the flame on one side of your face, gentle and steady, like sitting by a fire.

You let your hands rest in your lap. You let your shoulders drop. You let your head lean back, very gently, against the cool curve of the wall. There is nothing at all you need to do, and nowhere at all you need to be. The light is being kept. The night is nearly over. And you are safe, high up in a strong tower, in the middle of a quiet sea.

You do not need to say anything. Neither does he.

Down below you, through the floors of stone, the others are sleeping.

Hector sleeps on his side, his face to the curving wall, deep in a dream of Tiree, of the machair in summer, of his three children running on the white sand at Balemartine, and the little one, Mòrag, in her blue dress.

Thomas sleeps with one arm under his head, and somewhere in his dream it is April, and the bells of the old church above the harbor at Anstruther are ringing, and Jessie is laughing.

Neil sleeps curled under his gray blanket, with his letter to his mother under his pillow, and Bess the cat curled in the hollow behind his knees, both of them warm, both of them breathing slowly, both of them far away in sleep.

In the kitchen, below them, the range has burned down to a deep red glow, and the room is still warm, and the kettle sits ready on the side of the stove. Hector's rope mat lies coiled on the bench, nearly finished. Neil's letter waits under his pillow. Thomas's little brig waits on her shelf, with her one small sail. Alexander's book of poems lies closed beside his folded spectacles. Everything in the tower is in its place, the way it always is, the way the rules say it must be, and the way the men like it.

The tower holds them all, as it was built to do. Its granite rings are locked together, stone on stone. Its walls are thick and quiet. The sea at its foot is gentle now, and it will not trouble them.

In the east, over Tiree, the sky begins very slowly to change. Not light. Not yet. Only a little less dark. The black above the island turns to the deepest blue, and then to a softer blue, and the stars there fade, one by one, until only the brightest are left.

Out on the reef, very softly, the first birds begin to stir. A gull calls once, sleepily, from a ledge somewhere below, and is quiet. A little flock of small dark birds flies low over the water toward Tiree, and is gone. The seals have hauled themselves up onto the rocks, and lie there in a row, like long gray stones, with their heads tucked down. The swell comes in, slow and low, and breaks with a sound like a sigh, and draws back, and comes in again.

High in the south, the half moon grows pale and thin as the sky brightens around it, until it is only a faint white shape, like a shell on the sand.

The line of Tiree comes up out of the dark, low and long and gray. And far away on it, so faint you are not sure at first that you have seen it, there is a small yellow light. A window, in a cottage, at Hynish. Somebody has woken early and lit a lamp, and put the kettle on.

It might be Margaret. It probably is. She always wakes early when Alexander is on the rock, because she knows that he has the morning watch, and she likes to think of them both awake at the same hour, with twelve miles of sea between them, each with a light burning. She will be standing at the window now, with her shawl around her shoulders, looking out toward the reef, where a small white star is turning, low on the dark water, the way the lights of the coast have turned through all her married life.

Alexander sees it too. He does not say anything. But he smiles.

Alexander stands, and walks once slowly round the lantern, the way Thomas does, looking at every pane. They are clear. He goes back to his stool and sits down. In a little while, he will go down to the desk and dip his pen and write the last lines of the night in the journal. The wind, west, moderate. The sea, going down. The light, burned well all night. He already knows the words. He will write them in his neat slanting hand, the same as always, and blot them, and close the book.

But not yet. For now, he sits by the light, and keeps it, and watches the morning come.

Soon the sun will rise, and Alexander will put out the flame and write the time in the journal, and the curtains will be hung around the lens again for the day, and the kettle will boil in the kitchen, and the boat will come, perhaps, from Hynish. But that is all for later. For now, it is still night, just barely, and the light is still lit.

Here they all are, then, one last time, in the quiet tower at the edge of the sea.

Hector MacLean, the Tiree man, asleep by the curving wall, dreaming of home.

Thomas Rennie, the boatbuilder's son from Fife, asleep with his little brig on the shelf beside him, waiting to be finished.

Neil MacKinnon, the young man from Skye, asleep with his letter under his pillow, his first winter on the rock already more than half done.

Bess, the gray cat with one white foot, asleep on Neil's blanket, purring very faintly in her dreams.

And Alexander Grant, the Principal Keeper, wide awake and perfectly content, sitting quietly by the lens in the lantern, keeping the watch for all of them, so that they can sleep.

Above them all, at the top of the tower, the light is burning. Its flame stands tall and white and still inside its cage of glass. Its beam turns slowly out over the calm gray sea, reaching away toward the ships, and toward the morning, and toward the small yellow window on Tiree. It was lit at sunset. It has not failed. It will not fail tonight.

If you enjoyed this night with us, you are very welcome to like and subscribe, so that we can spend another night together soon. And if there is a time or place you would love to spend a night in, leave it in the comments, because your ideas truly help.

Next time, we will visit a very different kind of house, in a very different corner of the world, with another household keeping watch until morning.

But for now, the light at Skerryvore is turning, steady and bright, and everyone in the tower is safe and warm.

Sleep well, friend. Good night.

### Verification notes

- **Verbatim quotes.** Two. (1) Robert Louis Stevenson, "Skerryvore" (*Underwoods*, 1887): "For love of lovely words, and for the sake of those, my kinsmen and my countrymen", and the phrase "to plant a star for seamen". Both checked through search results quoting the poem; the rest of the poem is paraphrased. (2) Walter Scott, 1814 diary: "a long ridge of rocks (chiefly under water), on which the tide breaks in a most tremendous style", as quoted in a Canmore record; not checked against Lockhart's printed text. It is spoken without quotation marks, and the parenthesis is said as a phrase.
- **Paraphrased, not quoted.** Everything attributed to Alan Stevenson's *Account* is paraphrase: the reef only workable in calm weather; the blocks shaped, numbered and fitted at Hynish; the sleepless first night in the barrack in 1840, with a strong northwest wind and the constant lash of the waves (a fragment of his wording was seen in a search, but the full sentence was not confirmed); his warm account of Fresnel's lenses. The Hogben letter is paraphrased. The *Instructions to Light-Keepers* are paraphrased as general rules (lighting at sunset, burning until sunrise, daily cleaning, journal entries, not leaving the light on watch); no wording from the 1850 edition was seen. RLS's opinion that Skerryvore was "the noblest of all extant deep-sea lights" is paraphrased, because it was found only in a secondary news item. The 1871 Commons answer is paraphrased. The 1842 *Caledonian Mercury* report is paraphrased. Muirhead's 1901 conclusion is paraphrased from secondary accounts.
- **Cut or softened.** Cut: a claim that no lives were lost on the rock during building (not confirmed). Softened: the signal from the rock is described as "a flag or a shape" (sources mention semaphore and light signals as well as the acknowledging ball); the change from colza oil to paraffin is dated to "the early eighteen seventies" for the Board in general, as the year Skerryvore itself changed was not found; Skerryvore's 1890 flash pattern and range are not given, as they were not confirmed; the Smalls story is told without the grimmer details, and its link to the three-keeper practice is presented as disputed; the interior is described as "rooms stacked up inside the tower" with beds built against the curve, not as named rooms on named floors; the keepers' rota is given as "a spell of some weeks" because sources differ.
- **Stated from standard knowledge without a fresh search.** The Stevenson family relationships (Robert; sons Alan, David and Thomas; RLS born 1850); RLS's arrival in Samoa in December 1889 with Fanny and Lloyd Osbourne, and his Bournemouth house named Skerryvore (1885); RLS's stay on Erraid in 1870 during the Dubh Artach works and Erraid in *Kidnapped* (1886); Scott's *The Lord of the Isles* (1815); Augustin Fresnel (died 1827) and the Brewster priority dispute; the Statutes (Definition of Time) Act 1880; the NLB's George Street offices and the Pharos inspection voyages; Tiree's name "the land below the waves"; the *Oban Times* (founded 1861); keepers' uniforms, crafts, Gaelic psalm singing, lens cleaning, storm panes, ventilators and clockwork rotation; the Flannan Isles disappearance discovered in December 1900.
- **The household.** Alexander Grant, Hector MacLean, Thomas Rennie, Neil MacKinnon, their families and Bess the cat are invented, and the script says once that the household is a composite. The card asks for three keepers; the script keeps three keepers on duty and adds one learner (a supernumerary) to meet the brief's minimum of four named people. The learner's role is described generally and is not tied to a documented NLB grade name. None of the names is meant to be a real keeper, and none matches the Flannan Isles keepers.
