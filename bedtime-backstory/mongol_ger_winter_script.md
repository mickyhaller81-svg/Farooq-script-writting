## Part One — Around Two in the Afternoon: The Smoke Above the Snow

It is January on the Mongolian steppe, in the year 1254, and the afternoon sun hangs low and white over the snow. In a wide, quiet valley, a single round tent of pale felt stands with its door to the south, and a thin thread of smoke rises straight up from the ring at the top of its roof. Inside, under that ring, a small fire burns on the hearth, low and orange. The air outside is so dry and so cold that the snow squeaks under a boot, and every breath you take stings the inside of your nose.

Not the Mongols of the picture books, all thundering hooves and conquering armies. Just one ordinary family, on one ordinary winter night, in one ordinary tent of felt.

And without a few old tricks, you would not have lasted until morning, not out here, where the cold of a clear January night can sink to thirty degrees below zero Fahrenheit.

So tonight I would like you to carry three small questions with you. The first is this. How can a few layers of felt, no thicker than the width of your hand, keep out the winter wind of a land whose modern capital, Ulaanbaatar, is now the coldest capital city on earth? The second. What do you burn, night after night, all winter long, in a valley where you could ride for a whole morning and not pass a single tree? And the third. Why does every person who comes into this tent, the grandmother, the smallest child, even a stranger in from the snow, go to one fixed place inside, and sit there, and nowhere else?

These are not three separate questions. They are three doors into the same round house, on the same night, and we will open each of them in its own time.

This family never quite existed under one name, but every detail of their night is real.

So let your head grow heavy now. Let your shoulders loosen and drop. Your eyes may close whenever they like, and the fire under the roof ring will keep burning without you. Let us go back, gently, to a white afternoon on the steppe, and to the low round house with the smoke above it. Now, let us begin.

The valley runs roughly east and west. To the north rises a long, low ridge of brown hills, their tops scraped almost bare of snow by the wind, and their lower slopes softened by drifts. To the south, the valley floor opens out, flat and white, toward a frozen river lined with a few gray willows and a thin fringe of dead reeds. And between the hills and the river, tucked into a fold of the slope where the ridge bends a little to make a kind of shallow bowl, stands the tent.

The people who live in it would not call it a tent. In their own language, the word is ger, which simply means home. Much of the world now knows it by a Turkic word, yurt.

It is round, and low, and pale. Its walls rise to about the height of a man's shoulder, and then the roof slopes gently up from all sides to a small round opening at the very top, where the smoke comes out. Around the outside, broad bands of rope made from braided horsehair are wound tightly round the walls, holding everything in place. A heavy felt flap hangs over the doorway, and the doorway faces south, toward the low sun.

Inside that ger, tonight, live six people.

The oldest is a woman of sixty-three named Mönggen. Her name means silver, and her hair has gone that color too, though she wears it hidden under a dark felt hood lined with lambskin. She is the mother of the man of the house. Her husband died six winters ago, and since then she has lived here with her son's family, and she has taken one job above all others for her own. Mönggen keeps the fire.

Her son is named Bolad, which means steel. He is thirty-eight, broad in the shoulders and a little bowed in the legs, the way men become who have spent their lives on horseback. Bolad is a herdsman. He looks after the family's horses, which are its wealth and its pride, and he makes saddles, and repairs the carts, and carves the wooden parts of the ger itself when they crack or wear.

His wife is Saran, which means moon. She is thirty-four, quick and capable, with a wide calm face and strong red hands. Saran is the mistress of the ger. She milks the cows and the ewes in their season. She makes the butter and the dried curds. She sews the family's clothes from skins and furs, and she and the other women of the camp make the felt that covers the house.

They have three children. The eldest is a boy of fifteen named Temür, which means iron. He is already almost a man, already as good on a horse as his father, and he spends his days riding with the herds. The middle child is a girl of eleven named Gerel, which means light. She helps her mother and her grandmother with everything, and she notices everything too. And the youngest is a little boy of six named Toghan. His name means cauldron, the big iron cooking pot that sits over the fire, and it is a good, warm, homely name for a small round boy who is always hungry.

And there is one more member of the household who sleeps outside. He is a big shaggy dog, black with tan spots over his eyes, and his name is Bars, which means tiger. He is lying in the snow now, just beside the door, with his nose tucked under his tail, perfectly comfortable.

That is the whole household. Six people and a dog, under one round roof of felt.

You may have noticed that their names are all ordinary words. Steel and iron, silver and moon, light and cauldron. This is how many Mongol names of the time worked. A child might be named for a metal, for a star, for an animal, or for something the family hoped the child would become. Strength. Brightness. Endurance.

Why does this family live in a house that can be folded up and carried away?

The answer lies all around you, under the snow. This family lives on grass. Their horses eat grass. Their sheep and goats and cattle eat grass. And on the steppe, the grass in any one place does not last forever. It is grazed down, and it must be left to grow again. So the family moves with its animals, from pasture to pasture, through the year. In summer they go to the open, high, breezy places, near water, where the grass is long and the flies are few. In autumn they move toward shelter. And in winter they come here, to this fold in the hills, where the ridge to the north breaks the wind, and the slope faces the low southern sun, and the snow does not lie too deep for the animals to dig through it.

A family like this one might move several times in a year, depending on the grass and the weather. And each time, they take their home with them. The ger can be taken down in about an hour, by a few people who know what they are doing. Its wooden frame folds up. Its felts roll up. And the whole house, along with the family's chests and pots and bedding, can be loaded onto two or three carts pulled by oxen, or onto the backs of a few camels. When they reach the new pasture, they put it up again, in about the same time, and light the fire, and they are home.

And some of those houses were enormous.

Here is something that may surprise you. In the thirteenth century, the greatest families did not always take their gers apart at all. They simply lifted the whole house, still standing, onto a vast wooden cart, and drove it across the steppe like a ship under sail. A traveler who saw these carts with his own eyes, and who is spending this very winter not far from here, set it down in writing.

His name is William of Rubruck. He is a Franciscan friar, a man in a rough gray-brown robe with a cord around his waist, born in Flanders, in the low country by the North Sea. He set out in the spring of 1253, sent by King Louis of France, carrying letters and hoping to preach. He crossed the steppe through the summer and autumn, and just after Christmas he reached the winter camp of the Great Khan, Möngke, the grandson of Genghis Khan and the ruler of the whole Mongol world. Five days ago, on the fourth of January, he was led in to see the khan for the first time.

Later, when he wrote his long report for the king, Rubruck described the great house-carts he had passed on the road. "I myself once measured a breadth of twenty feet between the wheeltracks of a wagon," he wrote. When the house was set upon it, it stuck out beyond the wheels by at least five feet on either side.

He counted twenty-two oxen pulling a single one of these carts. Half of them walked in a row across the width of the wagon, and the other half walked in a second row in front of them. And a single driver stood in the doorway of the house, guiding them.

Our family owns nothing so grand. Their ger is an ordinary one, and it comes apart and goes onto carts in pieces. But it is the same kind of house, built the same way.

You are standing in the snow now, about fifty paces from the door. The light is clear and pale gold, and already slanting, though it is only a little after two. Your shadow on the snow is long and blue. All around you, the valley is perfectly silent, except for the faint creak of your own boots and, somewhere off toward the river, the low, contented grunting of cattle. The cold presses against your face like a cool, flat hand. When you breathe out, your breath hangs in the air in front of you, white and slow, and then drifts away. The fine hairs inside your nose prickle and stiffen. The snow here is not soft and wet like the snow of a wetter country. It is dry and fine, almost like sand, and when the wind lifts it, it hisses along the ground in low streams. You can smell the smoke from the roof ring, thin and sweet and a little sharp, not like wood smoke at all. And you can smell animals: the warm, grassy, woolly smell of sheep, drifting from a low fold behind the ger.

The felt walls in front of you are not pure white, not after a season of smoke and weather. They are the color of old cream, and darker near the ground, and darker still around the roof ring, where the smoke has stained them brown. The horsehair ropes around the walls are tight and frosted.

In our own time, if you visit Mongolia, you will still see gers like this one, everywhere. Herding families still live in them out on the steppe, and still move them with the seasons. And on the edges of the capital, whole districts are made of gers, row after row of round white roofs among the wooden fences, with stovepipes poking out of the tops.

Historians do not entirely agree about how common the great house-carts really were. Rubruck describes them clearly, and so do other travelers of the time, and some scholars think that in the thirteenth century many families, not only the rich, kept at least part of their home permanently on wheels. Others think the giant carts were rare, a kind of royal and noble luxury, and that most ordinary families, like ours, took their houses down and put them up again, just as herders do today. The truth may be that both were common, in different places and among different kinds of people.

The felt flap over the door lifts, and Gerel comes out, ducking under it. She is wearing a long padded coat of sheepskin, the wool turned inward, belted tightly at the waist, and a fur hat pulled down over her ears, and soft boots of felt and leather. She carries a wooden bucket. She steps over the threshold carefully, high and deliberate, the way every child of the steppe learns to do almost before she can walk, and then she trots off through the snow toward the river, where her brother Temür has broken a hole in the ice for water.

Inside, her grandmother Mönggen is sitting by the hearth. She is not doing anything very much. She has a heap of dry, pale, crumbly brown cakes in a basket beside her, and every so often she picks one up, breaks it in her hands, and lays the pieces on the fire. The fire takes them quietly. It does not crackle or spit the way a wood fire does. It simply glows a little brighter, and gives off its thin, sharp, grassy smoke, which rises in a slow column straight up through the roof ring and away into the cold blue sky.

Saran is at the east side of the ger, on the right as you come in from the door, kneeling beside a big leather bag and stirring something inside it with a long wooden paddle. Little Toghan lies on his stomach near his grandmother, playing with a handful of small polished bones. And Bolad is away, out on the slope with the horses, a small dark figure against the snow.

Mönggen leans forward and lays another piece on the fire, and the little flame under the roof ring brightens, and steadies, and burns on.

## Part Two — Around Three in the Afternoon: The Herds Come Home

By three o'clock the sun has slid a little further down the sky, and the shadows of the hills have begun to creep out across the valley floor. Up on the slope, two riders are turning the herds toward home.

Why do the horses stay out in the snow all night, while the sheep come in to shelter?

To answer that, we have to meet the animals. A family like this one lived with five kinds of livestock, and the Mongols counted them together, almost as one household of creatures. There were horses. There were cattle, and in the higher, colder valleys, shaggy yaks. There were camels, the two-humped Bactrian camels of Central Asia, with their thick winter coats and their long, patient faces. And there were sheep and goats, which were the most numerous of all, and which gave the family most of its meat, its milk, its wool and its skins.

Each kind of animal had its own needs, and each was good at its own kind of grazing. Cattle liked the wetter grass near the river. Camels could eat the dry, bitter, thorny shrubs that nothing else would touch. Sheep and goats nibbled the short grass close to the ground. And horses ranged widest of all, over the open slopes.

In winter, that difference mattered more than ever. Because the horses of the steppe have a skill that the sheep do not. When snow covers the grass, a horse paws it away with its front hooves, scraping down through the crust to the dry, frozen grass beneath, and eats. It can do this all winter, day after day, so long as the snow is not too deep and not frozen into a hard sheet of ice. The sheep and goats cannot dig nearly so well. So the herders often let the horses graze a patch of snowy pasture first, and then brought the sheep in behind them, to eat the grass the horses had uncovered.

And the horses themselves are built for this cold. They are small, sturdy, short-legged animals, with big heads and thick necks, and in winter they grow a coat as dense and shaggy as a bear's. These herds are not stabled, and they do not sleep under a roof. All through the winter they stay out on the steppe, day and night, standing together in loose groups with their tails to the wind, and they come through it.

Not without cost. By the end of a hard winter, a steppe horse might lose a great deal of its weight, sometimes as much as a fifth of it, and come into spring thin and rough-coated and slow. But it lives, and with the new grass it fattens again.

The sheep are another matter. They are tough too, with their thick fleeces and their fat tails, which store energy through the lean months. But on a bitter night, in the open, the young and the weak among them might die. So every evening, the herders bring the sheep and goats back to the ger, and pen them in a fold beside it, where they can huddle close together out of the wind.

Here is something that may surprise you. The walls of that fold, and its floor, are partly made of the sheep themselves. Or rather, of what the sheep leave behind. Through the autumn and into the winter, the dung of the flock builds up in the fold, and is trodden down by hundreds of small hooves into a thick, firm, dry layer. The herders cut this layer into blocks and stack them up into low walls around the fold, to break the wind. And the dry, packed floor itself keeps the sheep off the frozen ground, and gives off a faint warmth of its own. A good winter fold, on the sheltered side of a hill, might be used by the same family winter after winter, and the dried dung floor grows thicker every year.

We will come back to that dung later tonight, because it has another, even more important use.

The place itself was chosen with the same care. A good winter camp had to answer several needs at once. It needed a hill or a ridge on its north side, to stand between the family and the worst of the wind, which in a Mongolian winter comes most often from the north and the northwest, straight down from Siberia. It needed to face south, so that the low winter sun would fall on the door and on the fold through the short hours of daylight. It needed pasture that had been left ungrazed all summer, saved on purpose for these hungry months. And it needed water, which in deep winter often meant a river that could be broken open with an axe, or else nothing but snow, gathered in great armfuls and melted in the cauldron over the fire.

Snow, in fact, was a gift as well as a danger. A thin, even layer of it meant water for the animals without a journey to the river, because sheep and horses will eat snow to quench their thirst. It was only deep snow, or snow that thawed in a warm spell and froze again into a hard crust of ice, that the herders feared. They had a word for a winter like that, when the animals could not reach the grass, and they spoke it quietly. But this is not one of those winters. The snow on the slope is thin and dry, and the grass is there beneath it.

William of Rubruck, the friar we met earlier, watched these herders closely as he traveled, and he noticed that every member of the household had a part in this work. He wrote that the herders knew exactly which pastures belonged to them, and where to take their flocks in summer and in winter. In winter, he said, they come down to the warmer places in the south, and in summer they go up to the cooler places in the north. And he set down, one by one, the jobs of the women and the men. "It is the duty of the women to drive the carts, get the dwellings on and off them, milk the cows, make butter and gruit," he wrote. Gruit was his word for the hard, dried curd we will meet later in the evening. The men, he wrote, make bows and arrows, stirrups and bits, saddles, and the wooden frames of the houses and the carts, and they look after the horses. And the sheep and goats, he noticed, were tended by both men and women, sometimes by one and sometimes by the other.

So when you look at this valley tonight, you are looking at a household that works like a single body, each person with their own part, and the animals woven through it all.

You are standing at the edge of the fold now, beside Saran. The sun has turned the color of a ripe apricot, and it sits just above the line of the western hills. Long blue shadows stretch across the snow. Up on the slope, you can see the flock coming down, a great moving gray-white mass against the snow, and the sound of it reaches you before anything else. A soft, endless, overlapping bleating, high and low. The crunch of a few hundred small hooves on dry snow. And behind the flock, on his short shaggy horse, Temür, riding slowly from side to side, with a long pole in his hand and the dog Bars running low beside him.

The flock pours down the slope and flows around the ger like water around a stone. The smell reaches you, warm and thick and woolly, and a cloud of breath rises from all those animals at once and hangs over them in the cold air. Saran stands at the gap in the fold wall, counting with her eyes as they come through. She does not count aloud. She simply watches, and she knows. Her lips move once or twice. And when a ewe with a torn ear tries to slip past outside the wall, Saran steps forward with one hand out, and the ewe turns, and goes in with the others.

The fold fills. The sheep press close together, shoulder to shoulder, rump to rump, and their bleating slowly dies away into a low, comfortable murmur. You can feel the warmth rising off them. If you put your bare hand on the back of the nearest sheep, and sink your fingers into the wool, they disappear up to the knuckle, and the wool is warm and slightly greasy, and the cold of the air seems to vanish there.

In our own time, Mongolia still has many more animals than people. Its herders still keep the same five kinds, horses, cattle, camels, sheep and goats, and in some years the country has counted tens of millions of them, against a human population of only a few million.

Historians do not agree about how far families like this one actually traveled through the year. Some travelers of the thirteenth century, Rubruck among them, describe long, slow journeys between summer pastures far to the north and winter pastures far to the south, over hundreds of miles, especially on the great open steppes farther west. Other scholars point out that in the mountain valleys of Mongolia itself, many families probably moved much shorter distances, sometimes only a few miles, from a high summer valley down to a sheltered winter fold and back again. Both may well be true. The land decided, more than any rule.

Bolad rides in a little after the sheep, leading a string of three horses on a rope. The rest of the horses, nearly forty of them, he has left out on the slope above the ger, where the snow is thin and the dry grass is good, and where Temür will check on them again before dark. He swings down from the saddle stiffly, and stamps his boots, and blows on his hands. His face is red and raw with cold. The fur around the edge of his hat is white with frost from his own breath.

"The gray mare is limping," he says to Saran. "The left front. I think she struck it on the ice. I will look at it in the morning, when there is light."

Saran nods. "Gerel brought water. There is milk warming."

Bolad smiles at that, the first time all afternoon, and tugs the horses toward the hitching line, a long rope stretched between two posts on the south side of the ger. He ties them there, close to the door, where he can hear them in the night.

Little Toghan has come out to watch, bundled up so thickly in his small sheepskin coat that he can hardly bend his arms. He runs to his father and grabs him by the leg. Bolad lifts him up with one arm, and sets him on the back of the nearest horse, a calm old bay gelding, and Toghan sits there, grinning, holding the mane in both fists. He has been riding since before he could walk. Mongol children were often tied onto horses, or set in front of a parent, when they were only two or three years old, and by six, a boy like Toghan can already ride alone at a walk, and is longing to gallop. John of Plano Carpini, another friar, who traveled to the Mongol court a few years before Rubruck, in 1245 and 1246, was struck by the same thing. He wrote that the children began to ride when they were only two or three years old, and that the girls and the women rode as boldly and as well as the men.

"Tomorrow," Bolad tells him, "you can ride out with Temür to check the horses. Only to the ridge. Only if your grandmother says yes."

Toghan looks very solemn, and nods. He knows that his grandmother almost always says yes.

Up on the ridge, Temür has finished his circuit, and he sits for a moment on his horse, looking down. From up there, the ger is a small round pale shape against the snow, with the dark huddled mass of the fold beside it, and the line of horses at the door, and a single thin thread of smoke rising straight up from the roof ring into the still, cold air. Beyond it, the valley stretches away, white and wide and empty, to the frozen river, and beyond the river to more low hills, blue in the distance. There is not a tree anywhere. There is not another house in sight, though Temür knows that his uncle's family is camped just over the next fold of the hills to the east, and two more households beyond that. And there is not a sound, except the faint, far bleating of the sheep settling down, and the wind just beginning to whisper in the dry grass at his horse's feet.

He sits there a moment longer, breathing in the cold, feeling his horse shift and stamp beneath him, impatient for the warmth of the fold. Then he turns his horse and rides down toward the smoke.

Inside the ger, Mönggen hears the horses at the door, and the voices, and the stamping of boots. She leans forward and lays two more of the pale dry cakes on the fire, so that it will be bright and warm when the others come in. The flame catches them, and rises, and the light of it glows on the curved wooden poles of the roof, and on the round ring at the top, and on the patch of pale sky beyond.

## Part Three — Around Four O'Clock: The Lattice and the Roof Ring

By four o'clock the sun has touched the tops of the western hills, and the snow has turned from gold to rose to a cold, deepening blue. The family comes in from the cold, one by one, ducking under the felt flap of the door.

How do you build a house that can be packed onto a couple of carts, and raised again in about an hour, and still stand up to a steppe winter?

The secret is that the ger is not really one thing. It is a small number of clever parts, each one light enough to carry, and each one fitting into the others without a single nail.

The walls are made of a wooden lattice. Imagine thin, straight rods of willow, each one about as thick as your thumb, laid in two layers that cross each other at an angle, like the diamond pattern of a garden trellis. Where the rods cross, they are joined by small knots of rawhide, threaded through holes and tied off. And because they are joined only at those points, the whole lattice can open and close like a folding gate. Pushed together, a section of wall shrinks into a tight, narrow bundle that a man can carry under one arm. Pulled apart, it spreads into a broad, springy panel of diamonds, taller than a child. Several of these panels are stood up in a circle and tied end to end, and there are the walls.

The door is a separate frame of solid wood, a low, sturdy rectangle with a high sill at the bottom, set into the circle of lattice and tied to it on both sides. It faces south, always, toward the warmth and the light.

Then comes the roof. Dozens of long, slender wooden poles are laid from the top edge of the lattice upward, leaning inward, all of them pointing toward the center, like the spokes of a wheel. Their lower ends are tied to the lattice with loops of cord. Their upper ends fit into holes cut around the edge of a wooden ring, which sits at the very top of the house, in the middle, high above the hearth. This ring is called the toono. It holds all the roof poles together, and they hold it up, each pressing against all the others. It is the crown of the house, and its eye, and its chimney. The smoke goes out through it. The daylight comes in.

Then the whole frame is covered with felt, layer over layer, and bound round with ropes. And that is the ger.

This family raised this very ger here, in this fold of the hills, at the start of the winter, on a gray, still morning when the first snow had already come and gone. You can picture how it went. The carts creaked up the slope and stopped. Saran and Mönggen and Gerel unloaded the bundles. Bolad and Temür chose the spot, and scraped away the snow, and laid down a floor of old felts. Then the door frame was set up first, facing south, and the lattice panels were unfolded and stood up in a ring and tied to it. Bolad stood in the middle and lifted the roof ring high above his head on a pole, while the others pushed the roof poles up into its holes one by one, working their way around, until the ring could stand by itself. The felts went on, the ropes went round, and by the middle of the morning, Mönggen was kneeling at the new hearth, lighting the fire from a few embers she had carried from the last camp in a covered pot.

Behind the ger, against its north side, stand the family's two carts, their wheels sunk in drifted snow. On them are the things the family does not need every day: spare felts, the summer clothes, a store of dried meat, sacks of wool. Rubruck noticed carts like these too. He described chests made of split branches woven together, with lids like little roofs, covered with felt that had been rubbed with tallow or sheep's milk so that the rain would run off it. A rich family might own a great many of them, lined up behind the main house like a row of small cottages.

Here is something that may surprise you. There is no single main beam in this house, nothing that holds up everything else. Each wall panel leans a little outward under the weight of the roof, and each roof pole pushes a little inward on the ring, and the ring and the walls and the poles all hold each other in balance, the way the stones of an arch hold each other up. A band of rope wound tightly around the top of the lattice keeps the walls from spreading under the push of the roof. It is light, and it is strong, and when the wind comes hard across the steppe, the round shape lets the wind slide around it, instead of meeting a flat wall it can push against.

In later centuries, Mongol families came to treat the roof ring as the most precious part of the house. It might be carved and painted, and handed down from parents to children, while the lattice and poles around it were replaced as they wore out. A family could say, with some truth, that it was still living under the same roof ring as its grandparents.

John of Plano Carpini, the friar who came to the Mongol court in 1245, described these houses for the pope who had sent him. "Their dwelling-places are round like tents and are made of twigs and slender sticks," he wrote. "At the top in the middle there is a round opening which lets in the light, and is also to enable the smoke to escape, for they always make the fire in the middle."

A few years later, Rubruck looked at the same houses and saw beauty in them as well as cleverness. He noticed that the felt around the opening at the top was often decorated, and that over the doorway hung a felt curtain worked with colored designs. He wrote that the people made pictures on these felts of vines and trees, of birds and of beasts.

You are stepping inside now, behind Bolad. You duck your head under the low top of the door frame, and lift the felt flap with one hand, and the first thing you must do is step over the sill. It is high, about the height of your shin, and you step over it carefully, without touching it. Your boot comes down on a layer of felt rugs, soft and springy underfoot.

And then you straighten up, and the warmth reaches you.

It is not a hot room. It is not like walking into a kitchen with an oven. But after the air outside, it feels like stepping into a bath. The warmth wraps round your face and your hands, and your cheeks begin at once to burn and tingle as the blood comes back into them. Your eyes, which were narrowed against the glare of the snow, open wide in the dimness.

It is dim, but not dark. The only light comes from two places. Above you, high in the center of the roof, the round eye of the toono shows a disc of deep blue evening sky, and a single star has already appeared in it. And below that, directly under it, in the very middle of the floor, there is the fire. It burns in a low square hearth, framed by flat stones, with an iron stand over it to hold a big round-bottomed cauldron. The light of it is soft and orange and moving, and it glows upward over everything.

You look up. Dozens of slender roof poles, painted a faded red, rise from the walls all around you and converge on the ring above the fire, like the inside of a great umbrella, or the ribs of a flower. The smoke rises in a slow, thin, gray-blue column from the hearth, straight up, and slips out through the ring into the sky. Below the poles, the lattice of the walls makes a pattern of diamonds all the way round, half hidden behind hanging felts and rugs and leather bags.

The room is round, and perhaps eighteen feet across. Around the walls are low wooden chests, painted, and bundles of bedding rolled up, and leather bags hanging from the lattice. On the north side, the side directly opposite the door, there is a low wooden bed frame, piled with felts and furs. On the east side, to your right, are the cooking things, wooden bowls and buckets, the leather milk bags, the ladles and the churn. On the west side, to your left, hang a saddle, a bridle, a bow in its case, a coil of rope.

And the smell. Smoke, thin and grassy and faintly sour. Milk, warm milk, and the sharper smell of something fermenting. Wool and felt and leather. Old fat. Horse. People. It is a deep, rich, close, comfortable smell, the smell of a house that has been lived in for a long time.

In our own time, most Mongolian gers have an iron stove in the middle instead of an open hearth, with a metal stovepipe rising up through the roof ring. Many now have a small solar panel propped up outside the door, enough to charge a phone or run a light, and some have a satellite dish beside it. But the lattice, the roof poles, the ring and the felt are much as they were.

Historians do not agree on exactly how much the ger has changed since the thirteenth century. Rubruck described a dwelling whose roof poles met not at a flat ring but around a raised collar, which rose above the roof like a little chimney. Some scholars think the houses of his time had a taller, more pointed roof than the gers of today, especially the great ones that rode on carts. Others think the difference was mostly one of size and rank, and that an ordinary family's ger in 1254 would look quite familiar to a herder now. What everyone agrees is that the basic idea, the folding lattice, the radiating poles, the ring, the felt, is very old indeed.

Bolad has gone straight to the north side of the ger, and sits down on the edge of the bed there, with a long, contented sigh. He pulls off his outer coat, and his boots, and holds his bare hands out toward the fire. Temür comes in after him, stamping, his face red, and goes to the west side, the left side, and hangs his bow case and his whip on the lattice there. Gerel comes in with a last bucket of water, and goes to the east side, where her mother is, and sets the bucket down by the cauldron.

Toghan comes last. He trips at the door, the way small boys do, and his foot swings out toward the sill. And before it can touch, his grandmother has said his name. Not loudly. Just once, quietly, from her place by the fire. "Toghan."

He stops, and looks down, and lifts his foot, and steps over the sill with exaggerated care, high and slow, like a heron in a marsh. Then he looks at his grandmother. She nods, gravely. And he runs to her and burrows in beside her, against her warm sheepskin side.

"Why must I always step over it?" he asks.

"Because it is the threshold," says Mönggen. "It is the edge of the house. You do not stand on the edge of the house." And she strokes his hat, and that is all the answer he gets, for now.

Saran is ladling warm milk from a pot at the edge of the hearth into a wooden bowl. She hands it first to her husband, with both hands, and he takes it with both hands, and drinks. Then she fills another bowl for Temür, and another for Gerel, and another for her mother-in-law, and the last for Toghan. The milk is thin, because the cows give very little in winter, and it has been watered. But it is warm, and it steams in the bowls.

Gerel, sitting with her bowl beside her mother, tips her head back and looks up through the smoke at the roof ring. The star is still there, small and white and steady in the round of deep blue. As she watches, a second one appears beside it, fainter.

"There are two now," she says.

"Soon there will be too many to count," says her mother, "and then it will be time to close the ring." Every evening, as the cold deepens, the family draws a square of felt across the top of the toono with a long cord, to hold in the warmth, leaving only a gap at one side for the smoke. Every morning, they pull it open again to let in the day. Gerel likes to be the one who does it. She has already asked twice this afternoon whether it is time.

"Not yet," says Saran, before she can ask a third time. "Drink your milk."

For a while, nobody says anything. They simply drink, and sit, and let the warmth soak into them. The fire hisses softly. The roof poles glow red above. Outside, the dog Bars settles himself against the felt wall by the door, where he can feel a little of the warmth through it.

Mönggen lays another of the dry cakes on the hearth, and watches the flame curl up around its edges, and rise, toward the round dark eye of the roof.

## Part Four — Around Twenty to Five: Sunset, and the Secret of the Felt

At about twenty minutes to five, the sun slips down behind the western hills. For a little while the snow keeps a pale pink glow, as if it were lit from beneath. Then the glow fades, and the whole valley turns blue, and the cold begins to settle in for the night.

What is felt, and how do you make cloth with no loom, no spindle and no needle?

Felt is often called one of the oldest fabrics in the world, perhaps older than weaving, and it is made from almost nothing. You take raw wool, sheared from the sheep, and you beat it with sticks until the fibers are loose and fluffy and tangled. You spread it out in a thick, even layer on top of an old piece of felt, which serves as a kind of mother for the new one. You sprinkle it with water, sometimes hot water, until it is damp all through. Then you roll the whole thing up tightly, the old felt and the new wool together, often inside a hide, and you bind it with rope.

And then you roll it. For hours.

On the steppe, the roll was often tied behind a horse or a camel and dragged slowly back and forth across the grass, while the women and men of the camp walked beside it, or rode, and sang. Or a row of people knelt in a line and rolled it by hand, pushing it forward with their forearms, again and again, unrolling it to sprinkle more water, then rolling it up and going on. All that rolling and pressing and soaking works the wool fibers together. Each fiber of sheep's wool is covered in tiny overlapping scales, like the tiles of a roof, too small to see. When the wool is wet and pressed and rubbed, the fibers slide against each other, and the scales catch and lock, and the fibers cling and tangle and shrink together, tighter and tighter, until they can no longer be pulled apart. The loose fluff becomes a single, dense, matted sheet. Felt.

It was usually made in the autumn, after the sheep had been sheared and before the cold came, and it was work for the whole camp, neighbors helping neighbors, with food and drink and songs at the end of the day. A family might make several new felts a year, to replace the ones worn thin and gray by smoke and weather.

It took a great deal of wool. To cover a whole ger in winter, with all its layers, a family needed the fleeces of a large flock, gathered over more than one shearing. And not all wool was equal. The wool shorn in autumn, after a summer of good grazing, was thought best for felt, soft and long and full of grease. The coarser wool, and the hair of goats and camels, went into ropes and cords and the rougher kinds of felt.

Because felt was not only for walls. It was the great material of the whole household. It made the rugs on the floor and the covers on the beds. It made the pads under the saddles, and the lining of coats, and thick socks and the soles and uppers of winter boots. It made bags and cases, and the door flap, and the square that closed the roof ring at night. It even made the small sacred figures that hung on the walls of many gers, which we will meet much later tonight. A family without felt was a family without a home.

In later centuries, travelers among the Mongols watched the felt-making day and described it as something close to a festival. The first new felt of the season might be sprinkled with milk, for luck, the way a new house or a new journey was blessed. The old people would watch and give advice, and the young ones would do the heavy rolling, and when it was done there would be meat and drink for everyone who had helped. Whether every one of those customs was already followed in 1254, we cannot be sure. But the work itself, the beating and the wetting and the long patient rolling, was the same.

And so, at last, we come to the first of our three questions. How can a few layers of felt, no thicker than your hand, keep out the winter wind of the steppe?

The answer is that felt is full of air. Inside that dense matted sheet, the tangled fibers do not lie flat and close like the threads of a woven cloth. They curl and twist in every direction, and between them are countless tiny pockets of still air, trapped and held. And still air is one of the best things there is for keeping warmth in. Heat moves through it very slowly. So the warmth of the fire, and of the people, and their breath, stays inside the ger, held back by thousands upon thousands of those little pockets, as if the house were wrapped in a frozen breath of its own.

The wind cannot get through, either. A woven cloth, however fine, has tiny regular gaps between its threads, and a hard wind finds them and pushes through. But in felt, there are no straight paths. The fibers are matted so thickly, and so randomly, that the wind is stopped and broken up inside the felt before it can reach the other side.

And felt sheds the weather. Sheep's wool is naturally greasy, with a fat called lanolin that the sheep makes to keep its own fleece dry. Much of that grease stays in the felt, and snow and rain tend to bead up on it and slide off, rather than soaking through.

Then the family layers it. In summer, a single layer of felt over the lattice may be enough. But for winter, the walls get two or three layers, and the roof, where the warmth rises and tries to escape, often gets more. And under the felt, against the lattice, there may be an inner lining of cloth or skins. So the wind has to fight its way through several thicknesses of felt, each one full of trapped air, before it reaches anyone inside.

And the shape helps too. The ger is low, and round, with no corners and no flat walls. The wind does not batter against it. It flows around it, and over the gently sloping roof, and on across the steppe. Lashed down with its horsehair ropes, and banked around its base with snow and felt, the whole house sits on the ground like a stone in a stream.

That is the secret. Not a thick wall of stone, but a thin wall of trapped air, cleverly made, and laid on in layers.

Rubruck noticed that the felt was not only warm but carefully finished. "Frequently they coat the felt with chalk, or white clay, or powdered bone, to make it appear whiter," he wrote. A fresh white ger, gleaming on the snow, was a sign of a family that took care of its home, and a white felt was a little more resistant to the weather too. The people themselves seem to have thought of felt as almost the definition of who they were. In the Secret History of the Mongols, the great old chronicle of the Mongol people written down in the thirteenth century, the steppe peoples are called, again and again, the people of the felt-walled tents.

Here is something that may surprise you. Felt like this has survived from the steppe for more than two thousand years. In the frozen tombs of the Altai mountains, at a place called Pazyryk, archaeologists in the twentieth century found felts made around three hundred years before the birth of Christ, preserved in ice, still brightly colored, with cut-out felt patterns of animals and riders sewn onto them. And at Noin-Ula, in the hills of northern Mongolia, they found the tombs of the Xiongnu, a steppe people of around the first century, and in them, felt carpets with designs stitched into them. One of them was about five feet wide and eight and a half feet long, decorated with stitched scenes of animals. The felts our family sits on tonight belong to a craft that was already ancient when Genghis Khan was born.

You are standing at the north wall of the ger now, in the dim warm light, and you put out your hand and lay your palm flat against the felt. It is thick and soft and slightly rough, like the back of a very dense blanket. It gives a little under your hand. And it is not cold. It is cool, perhaps, but not cold, even though you know that just on the other side of it, a hand's breadth away, the night is falling at many degrees below zero. You press a little harder, and you can feel the lattice beneath it, the diamond pattern of the thin willow rods. And somewhere beyond, very faint, you hear the wind beginning to move. A low, soft, rushing sound, like the sea heard from far away. It touches the outside of the ger. The felt under your hand trembles, very slightly, the way a drum skin trembles when someone hums near it. And then it is still again, and the warmth of the room is just the same as before.

You look up. Around the roof ring, the smoke is curling and drifting. The sky in the round of the toono has gone from blue to a deep violet, and there are four stars now.

In our own time, our houses are insulated with fiberglass, and foam boards, and mineral wool spun from rock, and all of them work on the same principle as the felt. They are full of trapped, still air. And felt itself is still made on the Mongolian steppe today, in very much the old way, though now it is often pulled behind a truck or a motorcycle instead of a horse.

Historians do not agree on where felt was first invented, or when. Some think it began on the steppe itself, among the early herders of sheep, because it is so perfectly suited to the life of people who keep flocks and move with them. Others point to finds far to the west, in the ancient Near East and in Anatolia, and think the craft spread from there to the steppe very early indeed. Since felt rots away in most soils and survives only in rare, dry or frozen places, the oldest pieces may simply never have been found.

Saran has come to stand beside you, at the north wall. She runs her hand along the felt where the wall meets the roof, and frowns a little, and calls to her mother-in-law.

"Here, Mother. Feel this. It is thin here, at the top."

Mönggen gets up slowly from the hearth, and comes over, and feels the place with her old fingers. "The smoke has eaten it," she says. "That felt is from the year Temür was born. It has done well." She pats it, as if it were a horse that had carried her a long way. "In the spring, when we shear, we will make a new one for this side. Tonight it will hold."

"I want to help make it," says Gerel, from the hearth.

"You helped last year," says her mother.

"I helped carry the water. I want to roll it this time."

Mönggen smiles. "Then you will have arms like a wrestler by midsummer," she says. "And you will sleep well that night." She goes back to her place by the fire, and lowers herself onto the felt rug, and Toghan curls up against her again at once, like a cat finding the warm spot.

Temür is pulling on his coat again by the door. "I will go and look at the horses before it is dark," he says to his father. Bolad nods. Temür ducks out under the flap. For a moment a breath of icy air comes in with him, and the flame of the hearth leans sideways, and the smoke swirls. Then the flap falls back into place, and the warmth closes over the room again, and the smoke straightens, and rises.

Outside, Temür walks up the slope in the last of the light, his boots squeaking, toward the dark shapes of the horses. They are standing in a loose group on the open hillside, heads low, their backs already frosted white. One of them lifts its head as he comes near, and blows out a long plume of breath, and lowers its head again to the snow, scraping with one hoof. Temür walks among them slowly, talking to them under his breath, running a hand along a neck here and a flank there. He counts them without thinking, the way his mother counts the sheep. They are all here. The gray mare is standing on three legs, resting the sore one, but she is eating, and that is a good sign. He stands with her a while, his hand on her warm shaggy shoulder, and watches the last streak of green-gold light fade from the sky above the western hills. Then he turns and walks back down toward the small round shape of home, where a faint glow shows through the gap at the top of the roof, and a little smoke rises, gray against the stars.

Mönggen reaches for another of the dry pale cakes, and lays it on the hearth, and blows on it gently, once, and the fire glows up bright under the roof ring, red and gold against the deepening violet of the sky.

## Part Five — Around Half Past Five: The White Foods of Winter

By half past five, the last of the twilight has drained out of the sky. The round of the roof ring is black now, and full of stars, and the only light in the ger is the light of the fire. Saran kneels on the east side, among her bowls and bags, and begins to make the evening drink.

What does a family that lives on milk drink in the middle of winter, when its animals give almost none?

To understand that, you need to know that the year on the steppe had two great seasons of food. In summer and autumn, the family lived mostly on milk. The Mongols called these the white foods, and they made dozens of them. Fresh milk, from cows and yaks, ewes and goats, and even camels. Soured milk and yogurt. Cream skimmed off the top of the boiled milk and left to thicken. Butter. Soft curds and hard curds. And above all, from the end of spring to the autumn, the milk of mares, which was made into the most famous drink of the steppe, a sour, fizzy, faintly alcoholic drink that the Mongols called airag, and that foreign travelers came to know by its Turkic name, kumis.

Milk was more than food. It was the color of goodness itself. White was the color of purity and of luck, and milk was offered as well as drunk. Rubruck watched the ritual of it in the households he visited. Before the people of a house began to drink, he wrote, a little of the drink was sprinkled to the felt figures on the walls. And then a servant would go out of the door with a cup, and sprinkle a little of it three times toward the south, bending the knee each time, and then toward the east, and the west, and the north, honoring the fire and the air, the water and the dead. Only then would the master of the house take the cup in his hand and drink.

In winter, all of that changed. The mares went dry. The cows gave only a little thin milk, or none. The ewes and goats were carrying their lambs and kids for the spring. So the family turned to its second season of food, which was meat, and to the milk it had saved from the summer, in hard, dry, long-lasting forms that would keep through the cold.

It was a lean season, and the people of the steppe knew how to be lean. Carpini, who traveled in the winter of 1245 and 1246, noticed how little the ordinary people seemed to need. In the hungry months, he wrote, a person might drink a cup or two of thin broth or gruel in the morning, and eat nothing more all day, and then in the evening have a little meat, and that was enough. He also noticed that they could go without food for a day or two, and still sing and joke as if they had eaten well. To a friar used to the regular meals of a monastery, this seemed almost a kind of saintliness. To the herders, it was simply winter.

The most important of these was dried curd. In the summer and autumn, the women boiled great quantities of soured milk until it separated, and then pressed and strained the curds, and cut them into pieces, and laid them out on the roof of the ger or on boards in the sun and wind, until they were as hard as stone. They kept for many months. And in winter, when there was no fresh milk to be had, a handful of these hard pieces could be soaked and broken and stirred into warm water to make a sour, milky drink. Or children could simply suck on a piece, slowly, the way you might suck on a hard sweet.

Rubruck watched this done, and wrote it down carefully. He described how the women boiled the milk after the butter had been taken from it, and dried the curd in the sun until it was as hard as iron slag, and stored it in bags for the winter. And in winter, he wrote, when they had no milk, they put this sour curd into a skin, and poured water over it, and shook it violently until it dissolved, and then drank that sour liquid in place of milk. He used the word they used for it, which he wrote down as gruit. Mongols today still make it, and still call it by almost the same name, qurut.

And the butter, too, was made to last. "They store it in sheep's paunches which they keep for this purpose," Rubruck wrote; "they do not put salt into the butter." The butter was boiled first, for a long time, until all the water had gone out of it and only the clear golden fat was left. Poured into the cleaned, dried stomach of a sheep, and tied off, it would keep all winter without spoiling.

Here is something that may surprise you. Rubruck tasted the mare's milk drink, the airag, for the first time on his journey, in the summer of 1253, and he was rather nervous about it. But he found that he liked it. He described how it was made: the mare's milk was poured into a great bag of leather, and beaten with a heavy wooden club, hollowed out at its lower end, until it began to bubble and turn sour. And when you drank it, he said, it bit the tongue like a sharp young wine, and afterward it left behind a taste of almond milk, and it made a person feel warm inside. In later times, herders said that a good bag of airag needed to be stirred thousands of times a day, and anyone who walked past it in the ger was expected to give it a few strokes with the paddle.

You are kneeling beside Saran now, on the east side of the hearth, where the light is good. In front of her is a big wooden bowl, and in her lap a leather bag, and from the bag she takes a handful of what look like small, irregular lumps of pale gray-white stone. They clink together in her palm. She drops them into the bowl, and pours over them warm water from the cauldron, and leaves them to soak while she works at something else. After a while she reaches into the bowl and crumbles the softened pieces between her fingers, and stirs, and the water clouds and thickens into a thin, cool-looking, milky liquid.

All around her, within reach, are the things of her work. Leather bags of different sizes hang from the lattice, some fat and round, some flat and empty. A tall wooden churn stands against the wall, with its long plunger beside it, idle now until the spring. There are wooden bowls, some plain and some edged with thin bands of metal, and wooden ladles, and a pair of ox horns used as cups, and a small iron knife on a thong. Everything has its place, and everything is used, and nothing is wasted.

She hands you a little in a wooden cup. You hold it to your face. It smells sharp and sour and cheesy, like yogurt that has been left in the sun. You sip. It is strange at first, sour and a little salty, with a gritty texture at the bottom where the last crumbs of curd have not quite dissolved. But it is not unpleasant. It tastes clean, and alive, and somehow filling. You can feel it settling in your stomach. You take another sip, and it is better than the first.

Beside you, Toghan has taken one of the hard lumps out of the bag when his mother was not looking, and is sucking it, very slowly, with his cheeks puffed out, and a look of great contentment on his face.

In our own time, if you visit a Mongolian family, the first thing you will almost certainly be offered is a bowl of salty milk tea, with perhaps a little butter or roasted millet floating in it. But in 1254, that was not yet the drink of the steppe. Tea came later, in the centuries after this, carried in great bricks along the trade roads from China. The dried curds, though, you will still find, in every market in Mongolia, sold by the bagful, and still sucked by children.

Historians do not agree about how much airag the Mongols could actually drink in winter. Because the mares gave milk mainly from late spring to autumn, most scholars think that ordinary herders drank little or none of it in the coldest months, and relied on curd drinks and on whatever cow's milk they could get. But some of the great households and the khan's own court seem to have kept herds large enough to milk a few mares for longer, or to have stored airag through the cold. Rubruck himself was served drinks at Möngke's court in the winter, including a wine made from rice, and the kumis that was always the drink of honor. For a household like ours, though, winter is the season of the curd bowl and the meat pot.

Gerel kneels down on her mother's other side, and takes up a small wooden board and a knife, and begins to cut a lump of hard curd into thin slices, for her grandmother, whose teeth are not what they were. She works slowly and carefully, with her tongue between her teeth.

"Mother," she says, without looking up, "in the summer, can I milk the mares?"

"You can hold the foals," says Saran. "That is how it begins." In the summer, the mares were tied in a line near the ger, with their foals tethered nearby, because a mare will only let down her milk when her foal is close. A child's first job was to bring the foal to its mother's side for a moment, and then lead it away again, while a grown woman or man knelt with a bucket under the mare. It needed several milkings a day, and quick hands, and a great deal of patience. Gerel has watched it done every summer of her life.

"I held the foals last summer," she says.

"Then this summer you can hold the bucket."

Gerel smiles to herself, and goes on slicing. Over by the fire, Mönggen has been listening. "When I was your age," she says to her granddaughter, "my mother let me milk a mare once, and the mare kicked the bucket clean out of my hands, and I sat in the grass and cried, and everybody laughed. And then I got up and did it again." She holds out one gnarled hand for a slice of curd, and Gerel puts one in it. "That is the whole secret," says the old woman, and puts the slice in her mouth, and sucks it, thoughtfully, like her grandson.

Bolad laughs, a short warm laugh from the north side of the ger. "She tells that story every winter."

"It is a good story," says Mönggen. "It is worth telling every winter."

Saran pours the rest of the curd drink into a bigger bowl, and sets it on the north side of the hearth, near her husband. Then she reaches up to the lattice behind her, and unhooks one of the dried sheep's stomachs that hangs there, round and smooth and the color of old parchment. She unties the neck of it, and with a wooden spoon she scoops out a lump of hard, golden, pale butter, and drops it into the cauldron, where water is already heating for the meat. It melts at once, and spreads across the surface in little shining pools.

"Is the meat soon?" asks Toghan, with the lump of curd still in his cheek.

"When the water sings," says his mother. "Listen for it."

Toghan listens very hard. The cauldron is silent. The fire hisses softly beneath it. Somewhere outside, a sheep coughs. And then the door flap lifts, and Temür comes in, bringing a gust of cold air and the smell of snow and horse with him. He stamps on the felt by the door, and pulls off his gloves with his teeth.

"They are all there," he says to his father. "The gray mare is eating. She is standing on three legs, but she is eating."

"Good," says Bolad. "Then she will be well. Sit down. Get warm."

Temür hangs up his coat on the west side, and sits down there, near his father, and holds out his hands to the fire. His fingers are white and stiff at the tips. Gerel passes him a cup of the curd drink without being asked, and he takes it and drinks it all at once, and holds out the cup for more.

Gerel watches her brother drink, and then, without a word, fills his cup a second time, and he drinks that more slowly, holding it in both hands, letting the warmth of the bowl soak into his fingers.

Mönggen watches them all, from her place by the fire. Then she reaches out, and lays another piece of the dry pale fuel on the hearth, and watches the flame curl up around it. And the cauldron, very softly, begins to sing. Toghan sits up straight, and looks at his mother, and she laughs, and nods.

## Part Six — Around Half Past Six: Supper from the Iron Cauldron

At half past six, the cauldron is boiling hard, and the whole ger is full of the smell of mutton. The steam rises from the pot in a soft white cloud, mixing with the smoke, and drifts up together with it toward the black round of the roof ring.

How does a family keep meat fresh for months, with no salt, no cellar and no ice house?

The answer is outside the door. It is the winter itself.

At the beginning of winter, when the first hard frosts had come and the animals were at their fattest after the summer and autumn grazing, a family like this one would kill the animals it needed to see it through the cold months. A few sheep, perhaps a cow or a yak, sometimes a horse, depending on how large the family was and how rich. The meat was cut into joints, and the joints were left outside, or packed into the chests on the carts behind the ger, where they froze solid within a day. And there they stayed, frozen hard as wood, for the rest of the winter. Each day, or every few days, someone would go out to the cart with an axe, and chop off what was needed, and bring it in to thaw beside the fire. The steppe winter was a great natural cold store, and it would not thaw until the spring.

And for the journeys, and for the spring, when the cold store began to melt, there was dried meat. This was made by cutting meat into long, thin strips and hanging them up in the dry, cold wind, inside the ger or under the roof of a cart, until they were hard and dark and light as bark. Dried like this, a whole animal could shrink to a bundle small enough to carry in a single bag, and it would keep for a year or more. On the road, a herder could break off a piece and chew it, or drop a handful into hot water to make a quick broth.

And not every meat was thought to be the same. In later Mongol tradition, people spoke of some meats as warm and others as cool, rather the way some of us speak of foods that are good for a cold. Mutton and horse meat were the warm meats, the meats for winter, said to heat the body from the inside and help a person stand the cold. Goat meat was thought of as cooler, better for the summer. Whether a family in 1254 thought of it in exactly those words, we cannot know. But their winter pot, then as now, was full of mutton.

Rubruck noticed this too. He wrote that when an ox or a horse died, the people did not waste it, but cut the meat into narrow strips and hung them in the sun and the wind, where they dried without salt and without any smell. And the flesh of the animals that died, as well as those that were killed, all went into the pot. Nothing was thrown away.

The cauldron itself was one of the most valuable things a family owned. It was made of cast iron, or sometimes of bronze or copper, and it came from the smiths, who might be far away, in a town or in another camp, and it had to be bought or traded for with animals or skins or felt. A good cauldron could last for generations. It was the heart of the kitchen side of the ger, and the whole household ate from it. When people of the steppe wanted to speak of a family, a household that shared its food, they sometimes spoke of the people who shared one pot. It is no accident that a little boy named for the cauldron is a well-loved boy.

The cooking itself is simple. The meat is boiled, in the great iron cauldron, in water and nothing else, sometimes with a little salt if the family has any, until it is tender and the broth is rich and cloudy. Fat is prized above everything, because fat is warmth. The fat tail of the steppe sheep, a great soft cushion of white fat at the rump, is the best part of all, and it is given to the most honored person in the ger. When the meat is eaten, the broth is drunk afterward, hot, from the bowls, and the bones are cracked for their marrow.

Here is something that may surprise you. Wasting food was not just a bad habit among the Mongols of this time. It was something close to a sin. Carpini wrote down a long list of things the Mongols believed to be wrong, and among them was letting milk or any drink or food fall to the ground. He wrote that it was a great offense among them if any food or drink was allowed, in any way, to go to waste. And he and Rubruck both noticed, with the slight disapproval of men raised in tidy monasteries, that the Mongols did not wash their bowls with water after a meal, but wiped them clean, and that the broth from the meat was poured back into the pot, or drunk, never thrown out. In a land where every mouthful had been fought for, against the wind and the snow, that made perfect sense.

You are sitting on the west side of the ger now, near Temür, cross-legged on a felt rug, with a wooden bowl in your lap. Saran has lifted the meat out of the cauldron with a long iron fork, piece by piece, and laid it on a broad wooden board in the middle of the floor, near the fire. It steams in the firelight. There are ribs, and a shoulder, and a length of backbone, and pieces of the fat tail, white and soft and trembling. The smell is rich and warm and a little gamey, the smell of mutton and fat and smoke.

Bolad takes his knife from the sheath at his belt. It is a short, sturdy knife with a horn handle. He cuts a piece from the fat tail, and passes it, not to himself, but to his mother, Mönggen, holding it out on the flat of his blade. She takes it, and nods her thanks, and eats it slowly, with her eyes half closed. Then he cuts pieces for the others, one by one, and passes them round.

A piece comes to you. You take it in your fingers. It is hot, almost too hot to hold, and slippery with fat. You bite into it. It is very plain, boiled meat with no herbs and no sauce. But it is tender, and rich, and the fat melts in your mouth like warm butter. You can feel the warmth of it spreading into your chest, and then into your arms and legs. Around you, everyone is eating in silence, with great attention, the way people eat when they have been out in the cold all day. The only sounds are the hiss of the fire, and the soft wet sounds of eating, and once in a while the crack of a bone.

When you have finished, Saran fills your bowl with broth from the cauldron. It is hot and salty and golden, with little islands of fat floating on top. You hold the bowl in both hands and drink, and the warmth of it goes all the way down.

In our own time, Mongolian families still freeze their winter meat outdoors in just this way, and still make dried meat in long dark strips, and still eat the fat tail of the sheep as the best piece at a feast. But many now keep their meat in electric freezers as well, and in the cities, it comes from the supermarket in plastic trays.

Historians do not agree about how much the Mongols of this time lived on meat and milk alone. The friars who visited them described a diet that was almost entirely meat and dairy, with no bread, and they were astonished by it. But other written records show that the Mongols did trade for grain, especially millet, and that some households grew a little where they could. In recent years, scientists have studied the chemistry of bones from steppe graves to estimate what people actually ate, and some of these studies suggest that grain played a larger part in some steppe diets than the old travelers' accounts would lead us to believe. Our family, tonight, has a small sack of millet in the cart behind the ger, for the leanest weeks of spring.

Supper is also the time for talk, once the first hunger has gone. Temür has news from the ridge. From up there, he says, he could see smoke rising from his uncle's camp, over the next fold of the hills, and a rider leaving it, going east.

"That will be your cousin," says Bolad, "going to the river camp to fetch back the dog they lent." He thinks a moment, chewing. "Your uncle said he might come to us in a day or two. He wants to borrow the big saddle, the one with the new tree."

"He will want to stay and eat," says Saran, but she does not sound displeased.

"Then he will stay and eat," says Bolad. "And he will bring us news." On the steppe, where the nearest neighbors might be an hour's ride away and the next camp beyond them half a day, visitors were precious. Any traveler who came to the door of a ger, friend or stranger, could expect to be invited in, and given something to drink, and offered a place by the fire. The visitor brought news of the pastures, and of weddings and births, and of the weather on the far side of the hills, and in exchange took warmth and food and a night's shelter. It was an old and serious rule, older than anyone could remember. A family that turned a traveler away from its fire in winter would have been talked about for years.

"Will Uncle bring Ayan?" asks Toghan. Ayan is his cousin, a boy of seven, and his great rival in the shagai games.

"If he does," says his grandmother, "you will need all your bones."

Toghan considers this, very seriously, and goes back to his marrow.

When the meat has been eaten, and the broth drunk, Bolad picks up the shoulder blade of the sheep and turns it over in his hands, looking at it in the firelight. It is wide and flat and smooth. He does not do anything with it. He simply looks at it for a while, and then lays it down on the board. Many families of the steppe believed that the cracks in a burned shoulder blade could tell you what the future held, and Rubruck, a few weeks after this night, saw a servant carrying burned shoulder blades out of Möngke Khan's own dwelling. But Bolad is not a man who likes to know the future. He prefers to find it out when it comes.

Little Toghan has been given a leg bone, and is working at it with great determination, trying to get at the marrow inside with the point of a stick. His father takes it from him, and cracks it neatly with the back of his knife against a stone by the hearth, and gives it back, split open, and Toghan sucks the soft marrow out of it with a look of pure joy.

"And the bone, when you have finished," says Mönggen.

Toghan knows what she means. In the middle of every sheep's leg, at the joint, there is a small, square, knobbly bone, the ankle bone, which the Mongols call a shagai. Every child of the steppe keeps a bag of them. They are dice and counters and toy horses and toy camels, all at once, and there are dozens of games to play with them. Toghan's bag of shagai is already as big as his fist. He will have one more by the end of the night.

Gerel collects the bowls, and wipes them out with a twist of dry grass, and stacks them on the east side, and Saran pours what is left of the broth back into the cauldron, to be warmed for the morning. Nothing is wasted. Not a drop. Even the last smear of fat on the board is wiped up with a heel of curd and eaten by Toghan, who is never quite full.

The air in the ger has changed since the meal began. It is thicker now, and warmer, heavy with the smell of meat and fat and steam, and the walls seem closer and softer in the firelight. A thin film of moisture has gathered on the roof poles near the ring, and every now and then a single drop falls, glinting, and hisses on the hearth stones. Outside, the cold is deepening with every minute. But in here, for now, nobody is thinking about it.

Bolad wipes his knife on a scrap of felt, and slides it back into its sheath, and leans back against the north wall with a long sigh. Temür stretches his legs out toward the fire. The ger is warm now, warmer than it has been all day. The meal has done its work.

And Mönggen leans forward, and lays another piece of fuel on the fire, and the flame rises under the dark roof ring, and holds.

## Part Seven — Around Half Past Seven: What Feeds the Fire

By half past seven, supper has been cleared away, and the household has settled into the long, slow, comfortable part of the evening. And at the center of it all, as always, is the fire.

Why must nobody in this house ever poke the fire with a knife?

To the people of the steppe, the fire in the middle of the ger was not simply a way to keep warm and cook the meat. It was something alive, and something holy. It was the heart of the household, and in a sense it was the household itself, passed down from parents to children, carried from camp to camp. In later centuries, Mongols called the hearth by a word that also came to mean the family line, the continuing thread of a household from one generation to the next. A man who had no children to keep his fire burning after him was a man whose hearth would go cold. And a family spoke of a son's marriage, and his new ger, as the lighting of a new fire.

So the fire was treated with great respect, and there were many things you must never do to it. You must not step over it. You must not spit into it, or throw rubbish into it, or pour water on it to put it out. You must not point at it with your feet. You must not hold a sharp blade near it, or stir it with anything sharp. And when there was good fat from the meat, or a few drops of milk or airag, a little of it might be given to the fire first, before anyone else ate or drank, as you might offer the first and best of a meal to an honored elder.

John of Plano Carpini, the friar who came to the Mongols in 1245, wrote down a list of the things they considered to be wrong, the things that could bring bad fortune on a household, and the fire was near the top of it. He wrote that it was a sin among them to put a knife into the fire, or even to touch the fire with a knife in any way, or to take meat from the fire with a knife, or to cut with an axe near the fire. They believed that doing so would cut off the head of the fire. Among the same list, he noted that it was wrong to lean on a whip, or to strike a horse with its bridle, or to break one bone against another, or to pour milk or any food or drink onto the ground.

To a man from Italy, some of these rules seemed strange. But every one of them makes a kind of sense, in a round felt house in the middle of a winter steppe, where the fire is the thin line between the family and the cold. You do not stab the thing that keeps you alive. You do not make it angry. You treat it like a living guest, the most important one in the house.

You are kneeling on the east side of the hearth now, beside Mönggen, close to the fire. She has her basket of fuel beside her, and you look into it. It is full of flat, pale, roughly round cakes, a little bigger than your hand, light and dry and crumbly, gray-brown in color, with tiny pale threads of grass running all through them. They have no smell to speak of, only a faint, dry, dusty smell, like old hay. One of them is broken in half, and the inside is fibrous and loose, like dried peat, or pressed straw.

Mönggen picks one up, and hands it to you, and nods toward the fire.

You hold it in your hand. It weighs almost nothing. You lean forward and lay it carefully on the hearth, beside the glowing heap of embers. For a moment nothing happens. Then a thin wisp of blue-gray smoke curls up from one edge of it. A tiny tongue of flame appears, low and yellow, and spreads slowly along the rim, and then the whole cake begins to glow from within, red and orange, like a coal. It does not crackle. It does not spit or flare. It simply burns, steadily and quietly, with a low even heat, and a thin smoke that smells faintly sweet and faintly sharp, of grass and earth. You can feel the warmth of it on your face and your hands, strong and steady, much stronger than you expected from a thing so light.

Mönggen watches you, and nods again, satisfied. Then she takes up a short iron rod, blunt at the end, not sharp, and pushes the new cake gently into the heart of the fire with it, and the flame wraps around it, and the light rises up the red roof poles to the dark ring above.

Toghan is watching too. He has been given a small knife of his own this winter, a little blunt one in a leather sheath, for cutting meat, and he is very proud of it. He pulls it out now, and leans toward the hearth, to push a stray piece of fuel back into the fire with the point.

He does not get very far.

"Toghan," says his grandmother. Just once. Quietly. And he stops, with the knife in the air, and looks at her.

"Not the knife," she says. "Never the knife near the fire. Do you want to cut off its head?"

Toghan looks at the fire, and then at his knife, and then, rather guiltily, puts the knife back in its sheath. "Would it die?" he asks.

"It might," says Mönggen. "And then where would we be?" She hands him the blunt iron rod instead. "Here. Use this. Gently. Like you are stroking a dog."

And Toghan takes the rod, with both hands, and pushes the stray piece back into the fire, very gently, as if the fire might bite. The fire takes it, and glows a little brighter, and Toghan looks up at his grandmother with shining eyes.

"There," says Mönggen. "Now it knows you."

Gerel has been watching from the other side of the hearth. "Grandmother," she says, "is it true that our fire has never gone out? Not ever?"

Mönggen thinks for a while before she answers. "When we move," she says, "I carry the embers in the covered pot, with ash over them, on the cart, and I light the new fire with them, at the new camp. My mother did the same, and her mother. So this fire was lit from the fire of the last camp, and that one from the one before. I have never had to strike a new one with the steel, not since the winter your grandfather died." She is quiet for a moment. "That winter, the snow was very deep, and we lost a great many things. And the fire too. And I had to light a new one with the steel and the flint, with my hands shaking." She lays another piece on the hearth. "But that was a long time ago. This fire is a good fire. It has been with us a long time now."

Striking a new fire, when it had to be done, was a skill every grown person had. On his belt, Bolad carries a small leather pouch, and inside it is a curved strip of steel, a sharp piece of flint, and a twist of tinder, which might be dry grass rubbed fine, or the soft fluff of a dried plant, or a scrap of felt charred black at one end. Strike the steel against the edge of the flint, hard and glancing, and a shower of tiny sparks flies off. Catch one of them in the tinder, and blow on it, very gently, until it glows red, and then bursts into a little flame. Feed it dry grass, then thin twigs of wormwood, then the first broken pieces of fuel. A practiced hand can do it in less than a minute. A cold, tired, frightened hand, in the wind, at night, may take much longer. The pouch with the steel and flint was one of the things a herder of the steppe would never willingly leave behind, and in later centuries it became a thing of pride, worked with silver and hung from the belt beside the knife.

Saran, at the east side, has kept back a small piece of white fat from the sheep's tail. Now she reaches across, and drops it into the fire, without a word. It sizzles, and flares up bright and yellow for a moment, and gives off a rich smell of roasting fat, and then it is gone. Nobody comments on it. It is simply done, the way you might say a quiet grace before a meal, or touch a doorframe on the way out of a house. The fire has been given its share.

You notice now, too, the iron frame that stands over the hearth. It is a low ring of iron on short legs, blackened by years of smoke, and the cauldron sits on top of it, in the middle, with the fire burning underneath and around it. Its feet stand on the hearth stones. The whole thing is no bigger than a small table, and Bolad could lift it with one hand. But it is old, and dark, and somehow it seems as much a part of the house as the roof ring above it. In later times, Mongol families came to treat this iron frame almost as respectfully as the fire itself, and some would not let anyone set a foot on it, or lean anything against it, or move it without need.

Mönggen tells Gerel, as her own grandmother once told her, that the fire likes to be fed little and often, and not too much at once, like a baby. And she shows her again how to lay the cakes so that the air can get under them, leaning against each other in a little round tent of their own, with a gap at the bottom. Gerel has seen this a hundred times. But she watches again anyway, because one day it will be her fire.

Historians do not entirely agree on how strictly the steppe families of this time kept their hearth fires burning without a break. In later centuries, many Mongol families told of carrying embers from camp to camp, and of the hearth fire as a living thing passed down unbroken, and the idea of the hearth as the family line is very old. But the written sources of the thirteenth century say more about the respect paid to the fire, its taboos and offerings, than about whether every family managed to keep one flame alive for years on end. It may have been an ideal that families honored as best they could, and that some, like Mönggen, managed better than others.

And so, with the fire glowing under the roof ring, we come to our second question. What do you burn, night after night, all winter long, in a land where you could ride for a whole morning and not pass a single tree?

The pale cakes in Mönggen's basket are dried dung.

That is the answer, and it was the answer all across the steppe. The droppings of the family's own animals, collected from the pastures and the folds through the summer and autumn, left to dry in the sun and the wind until they were light and hard and odorless, and then stacked up beside the ger in a great pile, sometimes as tall as the ger itself, to see the family through the winter. Cow dung, and yak dung, made the best fuel of all, the flat round cakes that burn so slowly and so well. Horse and camel dung burned hotter and faster. And the small hard pellets from the sheep and goats, together with the packed dried blocks cut from the floor of the fold, burned hottest of all, and were saved for the coldest nights. Gathering it was a job for the women and the children, who walked the pastures with a basket on their backs and a little wooden fork or rake in one hand, picking up the dry cakes and tossing them over their shoulders into the basket without breaking stride.

Rubruck saw it burning even in the khan's own house. When he was led in to see Möngke Khan in January 1254, just a few days before this night, he looked around the great dwelling and set down what he saw. As one old English translation renders it, the whole house was hung with cloth of gold, "and on a hearth, in the middle, there was a fire of thorns, wormwood-roots, and cowdung." The roots of wormwood, a tough, gray, sweet-smelling shrub that grows all across the steppe, and the dry branches of thorny bushes, burned beside the dung, to help it catch and to make it blaze. Even the Great Khan, the ruler of an empire that already reached from China to the edge of Europe, warmed his hands in winter over a fire of cow dung, just as our family does tonight.

And the smell that you noticed in the afternoon, the thin, sweet, grassy smoke rising from the roof ring, is the smell of the steppe itself, of summer grass, eaten by the cattle and turned into fire.

Mönggen lays another of the pale cakes on the hearth, gently, with her blunt iron rod, and the fire takes it, and glows, and the warmth of a whole summer's grazing rises up into the round dark house.

## Part Eight — Around Half Past Eight: Firelight Work and Old Stories

By half past eight, the evening has found its shape. Outside, the stars have spread from one edge of the sky to the other, and the cold has sunk deep into the valley. Inside, everyone has found something to do with their hands, in the soft light of the fire.

What does a family do through a long winter evening, with no lamp but the hearth?

They work, mostly. Quietly, without hurry, and close to the fire, where the light is best. A winter evening on the steppe was the time for all the small, patient tasks that there was no time for in the busy summer, and that needed no daylight. The women sewed. They made and mended clothes, boots, hats, bags, covers. The men mended harness and saddles, twisted ropes, carved and shaped wood and horn and bone. Children played, and watched, and learned, by doing small parts of the work themselves. And all the while, people talked, and told stories, and sometimes sang.

There was no light to read by, and in any case very few people of the steppe could read. In 1254, the Mongols had had a written script for barely fifty years, borrowed from the Uighurs, and it was used mostly by scribes and officials at the courts. The life of a herding family was carried not in books but in memory and in speech: in the stories of the ancestors, the names of the pastures, the songs, the proverbs, the rules of the fire and the threshold, all passed from grandparents to grandchildren on evenings like this one.

Saran is sewing. On her lap is a piece of soft, supple goatskin, and beside her, a small pair of boots, half finished, for Toghan, whose feet have grown again since the autumn. Her needle is made of bone. Her thread is not wool or linen, but sinew, the long tough tendons from the legs and backs of the animals they eat, dried and then split into fine strands and twisted together between her fingers. It is stronger than any thread of plant fiber, and when it gets wet, it swells and fills the holes the needle has made, so that a seam sewn with sinew keeps out the water. Rubruck saw this kind of work going on in every household he visited, and among the women's tasks, he wrote, was the dressing and sewing of skins, with thread made from sinews.

Bolad, on the north side, is twisting rope. He has a bundle of long black and white horsehair, combed from the tails and manes of the horses, and he is twisting it into a cord, turning it between his palms and against his thigh, adding more hair as he goes, and the cord grows slowly and steadily, coiling up on the felt beside him. Horsehair rope was strong and springy and did not rot, and it was used for everything: binding the ger, tying the loads on the carts, tethering the horses, and making the long line of loops where the foals were tied in summer.

Temür, on the west side, is shaping arrow shafts for hunting, scraping at a straight birch stick with the edge of his knife, sighting along it in the firelight, scraping again. In a few days, if the weather holds, he and his father will ride out after hares and foxes on the hills. A fox skin will make a fine hat, and a hare is good meat.

And Gerel and Toghan are sitting on the floor between the fire and the door, with Toghan's bag of shagai, the little sheep ankle bones, spread out on the felt in front of them.

Here is something that may surprise you. One of the stories told on evenings like this one was later written down in the oldest surviving history the Mongols wrote about themselves, the book we know as the Secret History of the Mongols. It was composed in the thirteenth century, not long before this night, to tell the history of Genghis Khan's family and his ancestors. And many of its best stories are not about battles at all, but about homes, and mothers, and fires, and tents.

One of them is the story of Alan Qo'a, a woman who lived, the book says, many generations before Genghis Khan. She was a widow, and after her husband died, she had three more sons. Her two older sons began to whisper about this, wondering who the father could be. So one spring day, the Secret History says, Alan Qo'a cooked a meal, and called all five of her sons to sit together in a row. And she gave each of them a single arrow shaft, and told them to break it, and each of them snapped it easily. Then she bound five arrow shafts together in a bundle, and gave the bundle to each of them in turn, and told them to break it. And none of them could.

Then she told them where the three younger sons had come from. Every night, she said, a shining, yellow man had come into her tent, entering by the light of the smoke-hole at the top of the roof, or the gap above the door, and he had rubbed her belly, and his light had sunk into her. And when he left, he went out again on the beams of the sun or the moon, in the shape of a yellow dog. So her younger sons, she said, were the sons of heaven. And then she told them the lesson of the arrows. If they went each his own way, alone, they could be broken as easily as a single arrow shaft. But if they held together, like the bundle, nobody could break them.

The Secret History has other stories of home, too, and one of them is about a mother in a hard winter of her own. It tells how Hö'elün, the mother of the boy who would become Genghis Khan, was left a widow with small children, and how the people of their camp moved away and left the little family behind. And it tells how she did not give up. She tied her hat firmly on her head, the book says, and belted her robe up short, and ran up and down the banks of the Onon River, gathering wild pears and berries. With a digging stick of juniper wood, she dug up roots, and wild onions and wild garlic, and with these she fed her sons, until they grew up strong. It is a story about the courage of a mother, and about the steppe as a place that would feed you, if you knew where to look.

That smoke-hole, in the story, is the same opening that you can see above you now: the toono, the round ring at the top of the roof. To the people who told this story, it was not just a chimney. It was the place where the light of the sky came down into the house.

You are sitting just inside the door now, on the edge of the felt, watching. The fire is low and steady, and the light from it is warm and close and moving, so that the faces around the hearth seem to glow and fade and glow again. Saran's needle flashes, pale, in and out of the dark goatskin. The black horsehair cord coils slowly at Bolad's knee. Temür's knife whispers along the birch stick, and a curl of pale shaving drops to the felt, and then another. And in front of you, on the floor, the little bones click and rattle softly as Toghan scoops them up in his two cupped hands, and shakes them, and lets them fall.

On the curved felt walls all around you, the shadows of the family move and stretch. Saran's shadow bends over her sewing, huge and soft, reaching halfway up the roof poles. Bolad's shadow rocks gently as he twists his rope. When the fire flares, the shadows leap and shrink; when it sinks, they grow and lean together, until the whole round room seems full of quiet, slow-moving figures, larger than life, keeping company with the small real ones by the hearth. Toghan notices them, and holds up his hand to make a shadow horse on the wall, with two fingers for its ears, and makes it gallop round the lattice, until his sister tells him it is his turn to throw.

Each bone has four sides that it can land on, and each side has a name. One is the horse, one the camel, one the sheep, one the goat. When they fall, Toghan and Gerel bend over them, and count, and argue, and Toghan claims a horse that is clearly a camel, and Gerel laughs at him, and he laughs too.

The air smells of smoke, and leather, and warm wool, and faintly of the mutton broth still sitting in the cauldron. Above you, the poles of the roof rise into darkness, and at the very top, through the gap in the roof ring, you can see a little patch of sky, black and thick with stars. Somewhere outside, very faintly, one of the horses whinnies, and another answers, and then there is silence again.

After a while, Bolad begins to hum as he works. It is a slow song, with long, drawn-out notes that rise and fall and hang in the air, the kind of song that Mongols still call a long song. It has only a few words, about a horse and a far-off pasture and a river in the spring, and each word is stretched out over many notes, so that the song seems to wander like a rider across open country, in no hurry to arrive anywhere. Saran joins in, softly, for a line or two, and then goes quiet again, and lets him finish. Nobody claps. Nobody says anything. The song simply ends, and the work goes on, and the fire whispers.

Then Gerel, who loves riddles, asks one. "What has a round mouth, and eats all day, and is never full?" Toghan guesses the dog. Temür guesses Toghan, and Toghan throws a shagai at him, gently. Then Mönggen says, "The cauldron," and Gerel claps her hands, because that was the answer, and Toghan, whose name means cauldron, is indignant for a long moment, and then laughs harder than anyone. Riddles like these were one of the oldest games of the steppe, and a quick answer to a hard riddle was a mark of cleverness at any age.

Historians do not agree on exactly when the Secret History was written. At the end of the book, its author says that it was finished in a Year of the Rat, at a great gathering of the Mongol princes by the Kherlen River. But the Mongol calendar came round to the Year of the Rat once every twelve years, and different scholars have argued for different ones: 1228, the year after Genghis Khan died, or 1240, or even 1252, the year after Möngke became khan, just two winters before this night. Some think the book was written in stages, over many years, by more than one hand. What almost everyone agrees is that many of its stories were told aloud long before they were ever written down.

Mönggen has been watching the shagai game with half-closed eyes. Now she says, "Toghan. Gerel. Come here, and I will tell you about Alan Qo'a and the arrows."

"We know that one," says Toghan.

"Then you can tell me if I get it wrong," says his grandmother, and they come and sit beside her, one on each side, close against her sheepskins. And she tells it again, in her low, slow, cracked old voice, the story of the widow and her five sons, and the arrow shafts broken one by one, and the bundle that would not break. She tells it well. She has told it so many times that the words come out smooth and round, like the shagai in Toghan's hand, worn smooth by many fingers. Toghan mouths some of the words along with her. And when she gets to the shining yellow man coming down through the smoke-hole, both children look up, at the same moment, at the roof ring, and the dark gap in it, and the stars.

"Did he really come down through there?" whispers Toghan.

"Not through our smoke-hole," says Mönggen. "Through hers. A long time ago."

"How did he fit?"

"He was made of light," says his grandmother. "Light can fit anywhere."

Gerel, who is older, and thinks she is too old for this story, is nevertheless looking up at the roof ring too, with a strange, still look on her face. Her name means light. She has never thought about that before.

Toghan picks up one of the shagai, and holds it up toward the roof ring, one eye closed, as if he might see the yellow man up there. Then he yawns, enormously, and leans his head against his grandmother's shoulder.

Bolad looks up from his rope. "He will be asleep before the fire needs feeding," he says.

"He will not," says Mönggen, and puts her arm round the boy. "He is going to help me with the fire. Are you not, Toghan?"

And Toghan, without opening his eyes, nods.

Mönggen reaches out with her free hand, and lays another piece of fuel on the hearth, and the flame rises up gold toward the dark ring of the roof, through which, once upon a time, the light of heaven came down.

## Part Nine — Around Half Past Nine: Furs, Felt Boots and the Rising Moon

At half past nine, Bolad puts down his rope, and stretches, and reaches for his coat. It is time for the last look at the animals before the night. Over the eastern hills, the moon has risen, a little past full, and the snow outside has turned from black to a soft and silvery gray.

How do you dress to step out into a night so cold that your breath freezes on the fur of your collar?

You dress in layers, and almost all of them come from the animals. A herder of the steppe wore, closest to the skin, a long shirt or robe of soft cloth or thin leather. Over it went a heavier robe, long and loose, reaching below the knees, wrapped across the chest and fastened at the side, and tied round the waist with a long sash, so that it held the warmth in at the middle. In winter, that robe was lined with fur or with sheepskin, the wool turned inward. And over that, for going outdoors, went a great fur coat, long and heavy, with wide sleeves that could be pulled down over the hands like mittens.

On the feet, there were socks of felt, thick and soft, and over them boots of leather, often with thick soles of layered felt, roomy enough that there was space inside for warm air, and for a little dry grass stuffed around the foot on the coldest days. On the head, a fur hat, with flaps that came down over the ears and the back of the neck, and a brim that could be turned down over the brow. The face was the only part left open to the cold, and on the worst days, people pulled up the collar of the coat and turned their faces from the wind.

Rubruck, who arrived at the khan's camp in the dead of winter, was fascinated by these clothes, and also, it must be said, in great need of them. He wrote that the Mongols always made, in winter, at least two fur gowns, "one with the fur against the body, the other with the fur outside," facing the wind and the snow. The rich, he said, made their outer gowns from the skins of wolves and foxes and other fine furs. The poor made do with the skins of dogs and goats.

Here is something that may surprise you. Rubruck and his companions were Franciscan friars, and Franciscans of that time went barefoot, as a sign of their poverty, in all weathers. Rubruck had walked across half the world without shoes, or in simple sandals. But when he reached Möngke Khan's camp, at the end of December 1253, even his feet met their match. On the day he was called to court, he left his shoes behind, as was his custom. And as one old English translation puts it, "Next morning the ends of my toes were so frostbit by the extreme cold of the country, that I could no longer go barefooted."

The Mongols, it seems, took pity on him. Rubruck tells us that the court sent the friars fur gowns to wear, with the fur on the outside. His companion, a friar named Bartholomew of Cremona, found his heavy fur coat too tiring to wear, and they gave him lighter padded clothing instead. And Rubruck asked, and was given leave, to stay at the camp until the worst of the cold had passed, because Bartholomew was not strong enough to ride on through the winter. So this winter, somewhere to the south of our valley, a friar from Flanders who once went barefoot is living in a borrowed fur coat, among hearths of thorns and roots and cow dung.

There was another lesson waiting for the friars at court, and it was the same lesson that Toghan learned at the door this afternoon. One day early in that winter, as the friars were leaving one of the khan's dwellings, Bartholomew, turning to bow respectfully toward the khan as he went, did not watch his feet, and touched the threshold. At once the guards stopped him. He was taken away to be questioned by one of the khan's chief officials, a man Rubruck calls Bulgai, the chief secretary of the court. Rubruck, alarmed, explained that they were strangers, and had had no interpreter to warn them of the custom. And in the end, after a good deal of worry, Bartholomew was let go. To the Mongols, the threshold was the edge of the house, a line that belonged to the household and its fortune, and to tread on it was to tread on the house itself.

Bolad is pulling on his outer coat now, a great heavy coat of sheepskin, worn wool inward, with a broad collar of wolf fur. He ties his sash tight round his waist. He pulls on his felt boots, which have been standing by the fire, and his fur hat, and his thick leather gloves. And he nods to you, and lifts the felt flap of the door, and steps out over the threshold. You follow him.

The cold takes your breath away. It is not like the cold of the afternoon. That was sharp and clear. This is something deeper, heavier, a cold that seems to have weight and edges. It presses on your eyes. It finds the tiny gap between your hat and your collar at once, and slides down your neck like cold water. The first breath you take burns in your chest, and you cough, and your breath comes out in a great white cloud, and hangs, and does not seem to move. Within a minute, the fur around your collar, where your breath touches it, has turned white and stiff with frost. Your eyelashes begin to stick together when you blink.

And it is beautiful. The moon has risen over the hills to the east, large and bright and a little lopsided, and the whole valley is silver and gray and still. Every ridge of snow has a sharp blue shadow. The frozen river glints far off like a long dropped ribbon. Above the moon, the sky is deep, deep blue, and the stars are thinner here than they were an hour ago, washed pale by the moonlight, though overhead and to the west they are still thick. The snow squeaks under your boots, loud as anything in the silence.

In the light of the moon, the air itself seems to glitter. Tiny crystals of ice are drifting in it, so fine that you cannot feel them on your face, only see them, turning and sparkling as they catch the moonlight, like dust in a sunbeam. They hang over the snow, and over the fold, where the warm breath of the sheep rises and freezes at once. Your own breath joins them. When you breathe out slowly, you can watch your breath turn to glitter in front of you, and sink, and settle on the snow. The whole valley seems to be very gently, very quietly, sparkling.

Bolad walks round the ger, slowly, checking the ropes, pressing on the felt, as a sailor might check the rigging of a ship. He looks into the fold, where the sheep lie pressed together in one great warm woolly mass, steaming faintly in the moonlight, half asleep. He walks to the line of horses tied at the door, and runs his hand down the neck of each one, and murmurs to them. Then he stands for a while, looking up the slope toward the dark shapes of the other horses on the hillside. They are standing close together, tails to the north, still as statues, their backs white with frost. Out here, in this, all night. And they will be fine.

The dog Bars gets up from beside the door, and shakes himself, and comes over to Bolad, and pushes his big head under his hand. Bolad rubs his ears. Then the two of them stand together for a moment in the moonlight, a man and a dog, quite still, listening to the silence of the steppe.

Above them, the frost has begun to grow on the horsehair ropes of the ger, fine and white and feathery, so that the whole round house seems to have been dusted with sugar in the moonlight.

And then you go back in.

The warmth of the ger, when you lift the flap and step over the sill, is like a hand cupped around you. The fire is low and red, and the smoke rises softly. Your cheeks burn, and your eyes water, and the frost on your collar begins at once to melt into tiny drops.

The married women of the great households wore something even more remarkable on their heads, and Rubruck could hardly stop looking at it. He described a tall headdress, called a boqta, built on a frame of bark or light wood, rising high above the head, wider at the top than at the bottom, covered in silk, and crowned with a tuft of feathers or slender sticks. It was a sign of a married woman of rank, and the finest of all were worn by the wives of the khans and princes.. When a group of them rode out together, they looked from a distance like soldiers with helmets and lances. Saran does not own anything so grand. But she has a modest headdress of her own, kept wrapped in a cloth at the bottom of a chest, for weddings.

Historians do not agree on how much the fine clothes that the friars described at the khan's court reached ordinary herding families. Rubruck and Carpini both wrote of silks and gold brocades and rich furs at the great camps, brought from China and Persia as tribute and trade. Some scholars think that, in these years of empire, a little of that wealth flowed down even to ordinary households, a silk sash here, a cotton lining there. Others think that for most herders, life went on much as before, dressed almost entirely in the skins and wool of their own animals. A family like ours probably owned one or two good pieces of cloth, kept for weddings and feasts, and wore skins and felt for every other day of the year.

Around the hearth now, the household's boots stand in a ring, like a small patient herd, their soles turned toward the warmth. There are Bolad's big felt-lined boots, and Temür's, scuffed and stained at the toes, and Gerel's, and Mönggen's soft old ones, the leather worn smooth and dark as a chestnut. A faint steam rises from them as the last of the snow melts out of the felt. The smell of warm wet wool and leather mixes with the smoke. It is the smell of a winter evening in every ger on the steppe, and to the people in this room it is the smell of being safely home.

Saran has set Toghan's little felt boots among them, and is holding his felt socks out to the heat, one in each hand, turning them over and over. When she judges them warm enough, she calls him over, and he comes, yawning, and sits in front of her, and she pulls off the damp ones and puts the warm ones on his feet. He wriggles his toes in them, and holds his feet out toward the fire to show everyone, and sighs, as if he has been given a great gift.

"Now you will sleep warm," says his mother.

"I am not going to sleep yet," says Toghan. "I am helping Grandmother with the fire."

"Of course you are," says Saran, and kisses the top of his head.

Gerel has taken off her outer coat, and folded it carefully, the way her mother taught her, with the fur inward, and laid it on her bedding on the east side, ready to pull over her blankets later in the night. Temür has hung his bow case and quiver on the west wall, and is rubbing a little sheep fat into the leather of his boots, to keep it soft and keep the snow out.

Mönggen holds out her hands, and Saran passes her one of Bolad's gloves, which has split along the seam at the thumb. The old woman squints at it in the firelight, and finds a needle in the little bone case that hangs from her belt, and a length of sinew thread, and begins to stitch it, slowly, by feel more than sight, her lips pressed together. Her fingers are knotted and stiff, but they know the work. She has sewn gloves for this family for more than forty winters. When the seam is closed, she bites off the thread, and turns the glove the right way out, and pushes her own hand inside to test it, and nods.

And Bolad hangs up his coat, and comes back to his place on the north side, and sits down heavily, and holds out his hands to the fire.

"Clear," he says to his mother. "No wind. The moon is up. A cold night, but a quiet one."

"A quiet night is a good night," says Mönggen, and lays a piece of fuel on the low red fire, and watches it glow, and catch, and send its thin sweet smoke up into the ring of the roof, where the moonlight is now beginning to find its way in, pale and silver, from the east.

## Part Ten — Around Half Past Ten: Closing the Roof Ring

By half past ten, the work has been put away. The rope is coiled. The needle is back in its case. The arrow shafts lie in a neat bundle on the west side, and the little bones are back in their bag. The fire has sunk down to a low, red, glowing heap, and the ger is full of a soft, drowsy warmth. It is time for bed. Nobody needs to say so. Everyone simply knows, the way the sheep in the fold know, and begins, one by one, to move toward their furs.

How do you keep the warmth in through a long winter night, when the fire must be let down low?

There are several answers, and the family does all of them, the same way, every night of the winter.

The first is the roof ring. All evening, the smoke has been rising out through the toono, and with the smoke, a great deal of warm air has been escaping too, up into the cold sky. So at bedtime, someone pulls the square of felt that lies on the roof across the top of the ring, by a long cord that hangs down inside, near the door. The felt slides over the opening, and covers most of it, leaving only a narrow gap at one edge, enough to let the last thin smoke of the banked fire find its way out. And at once, the warmth of the room is held in, under the roof, like the warmth under a quilt.

The second is the fire itself. It is not left to burn bright all night, which would use up the fuel, and need constant tending. Instead it is banked. The glowing embers are drawn together into a heap at the center of the hearth, and a few good pieces of fuel are laid against them, and then the whole heap is covered with a thin layer of warm ash. Under the ash, the fire goes on burning, very slowly, all night long, giving off a gentle, steady warmth. In the morning, the ash can be brushed away, and the embers will still be glowing underneath, ready to be woken.

The third is the bedding. Felts are laid on the floor, layer on layer, and over them, sheepskins with the wool upward, soft and deep. The sleepers lie on these, and pull over themselves more felts, and furs, and their own heavy coats, until they are buried in wool. They sleep close together, often several to a bed, sharing their warmth, the way the sheep do outside in the fold.

And the fourth is the door. The felt flap that hangs over the doorway is pulled tight and tied down at the bottom, and a second felt may be hung inside it, so that no thread of cold air can creep in from the south.

Rubruck saw the sleeping places of the greatest household of all. When he was first brought before Möngke Khan, he found him not on a throne but on a couch, set on the north side of the great dwelling, in the place where Bolad sleeps tonight. As one old English translation describes it, "The khan sat upon a couch covered with a bright and shining spotted fur, like seal's skin." And Rubruck looked at the man himself, the ruler of so much of the world, sitting there in his furs, and described him simply. "He was a flat-nosed man, of middle stature, about forty-five years of age." Beside him sat one of his young wives, and further along, on another couch, a daughter, and some of the younger children. The khan of all the Mongols, in his felt house, in winter, sat with his family on a fur-covered bed by the fire, just as families did in every ger on the steppe.

Here is something that may surprise you. A family like this one had no clock, and no need of one. At night, they told the time by the sky. Mongols have long called the Pole Star the Golden Stake, the tethering post of the heavens, fixed in its place in the north while everything else turns around it. And the seven bright stars that we call the Big Dipper they called the Seven Old Men, walking slowly around that golden stake through the night, like herders circling a tethered horse. A herder stepping out of his door in the small hours could look up and see where the Seven Old Men had got to, and know at once how long it was until dawn. Even the roof ring, open to the sky, was a kind of window onto that great slow clock.

You are lying on the west side of the ger now, near the door, on a deep bed of felts and sheepskins that Saran has laid out for you, with a heavy fur coat over you. The wool is soft and deep and warm, and it smells of sheep and smoke and the faint sourness of milk. You can feel the warmth of the hearth on your face, and the cool, faint breath of the felt wall behind your head.

Gerel is standing by the door, with the cord of the roof ring in her hands. She has waited all evening for this. She looks up at the toono, where a faint silver light from the moon is coming in through the open ring and falling on the smoke. Then she pulls the cord, slowly and steadily, hand over hand. Above you, with a soft dry whispering sound, the square of felt slides across the top of the roof ring. The patch of stars and moonlight narrows, and narrows, and becomes a thin silver crescent at one edge, and then only a narrow line.

She ties off the cord, carefully, in the way her grandmother taught her. And she looks up once more at the thin line of moonlight in the roof, and then she goes back to her bed on the east side, and climbs in under her furs.

Through that narrow line, a single thin blade of moonlight comes down into the ger. It falls slantwise through the last of the smoke, so that you can see it hanging there in the air, faint and silver, a soft pale ribbon reaching from the roof toward the floor. It lands on the felt near the door, a small bright stripe, no wider than a finger. As the night goes on, and the moon climbs and swings across the sky, that little stripe of light will move, very slowly, across the floor of the ger, like the hand of a clock that nobody needs to read.

The room seems smaller now, and closer, and warmer. The roof poles disappear into darkness. The only light is the red glow of the hearth, and the faint silver thread at the top of the roof. You can hear everything. The soft ticking of the embers. The rustle of a sheepskin as someone turns over. Bolad yawning. The dog outside, shifting against the felt wall by the door, and sighing, a deep, long, doggy sigh.

Gerel lies on her back under her furs, with only her face showing, and looks up at that narrow line of silver. She is thinking about the yellow man in her grandmother's story, coming down on a beam of light. She is thinking that it would be a very cold journey, down from the sky on a night like this. And she is thinking about her own name, and wondering why her mother chose it. She decides she will ask in the morning. Then she decides she knows the answer already, and does not need to ask. She was born, her grandmother once told her, just as the sun came up over the hills, on a spring morning, and the first light came in through the roof ring and fell right across the bed. She smiles in the dark at that, and turns on her side, and pulls the furs up over her ear.

Historians do not agree on how families of different kinds shared their sleeping places. At the khan's court, Rubruck noticed that each of the wives had a dwelling of her own, with her own household and her own carts, standing in a long line beside the khan's. In ordinary herding families, it seems more often to have been one ger for a married couple and their children, and perhaps an elderly parent too, as in ours. But when a son married, he would often be given a new ger of his own, close beside his parents', and some scholars think that even quite modest families might have had two or three gers together in a winter camp. The pattern, it seems, stretched and shrank with the family.

Saran is putting Toghan to bed. Despite his promise to help with the fire, he has fallen asleep sitting up against his grandmother's side, with the bag of shagai still clutched in one fist. His mother lifts him gently, and he does not wake, and she carries him to the bed on the east side, where Gerel sleeps, and lays him down among the sheepskins, and pulls the furs up to his chin. He stirs, and mumbles something about a camel, and is still again. She tries to take the bag of bones from his hand, and his fist tightens on it, so she leaves it there.

Toghan's breathing slows, and deepens, and a faint small snore begins. Gerel, beside him, hears it, and smiles, and does not tease him.

Temür has already rolled himself up in his furs on the west side, near his father's bed, with his hat pulled down over his eyes. He works harder than any of them, out with the horses in the wind, and he is always the first asleep. His breathing is already slow and deep.

Bolad sits for a moment on the edge of his bed on the north side, pulling off his boots and setting them by the hearth, toes to the fire. He looks round the room, at his sleeping son, and his daughter's face peeping out of her furs, and the small round shape of Toghan, and his mother by the fire. Then he lies back, and pulls his coat over himself, and closes his eyes.

In the dark, from the north bed, Bolad speaks quietly, not opening his eyes. "When the spring comes," he says, "we will move early, to the river pasture. Before your brother's people. The grass there was good last year."

"After the lambs, I hope," says Saran, from the east side, where she is folding the last of the bowls into a basket.

"After the first lambs. When the snow goes from the south slopes." He is quiet for a moment. "Temür can take the horses by himself this year. He is ready."

"He has been ready since the autumn," says Saran. "He has only been waiting for you to say so."

Bolad laughs, very softly, so as not to wake anyone. "Then I will say so. In the morning."

Saran comes to the hearth, and kneels there for a moment beside her mother-in-law.

"Will you bank it, Mother, or shall I?"

"I will," says Mönggen. "Go to bed. You have been on your feet since before the light."

Saran touches the old woman's shoulder, once, and goes to lie down beside her husband, at the eastern end of the north bed, nearest her children.

And Mönggen, alone by the hearth now, takes up her blunt iron rod, and begins, very slowly and carefully, to draw the embers together into a heap at the very center of the hearth. She lays three of the best pale cakes of fuel against them, leaning together, the way she showed Gerel. And then, with a small flat wooden scoop, she sprinkles warm ash over the top, softly, softly, like snow falling on a roof, until the red glow is hidden, and there is only a low gray mound in the hearth, with here and there a faint red gleam showing through, like an eye half closed.

The warmth of it still rises. You can feel it on your face.

And you can feel the whole house settling around you, the way a horse settles when it lies down to sleep. The felt walls seem to draw in a little closer. The air is still, and thick, and soft, and warm, and smells of ash and wool and the faint, sweet, grassy smoke. Under your back, the layers of felt and sheepskin give gently, and hold you. Over you, the heavy fur coat lies like a warm animal. Your feet, in their felt socks, are warm right down to the toes. Somewhere beyond the wall, very faintly, a horse stamps once on the frozen ground, and then is still. And outside, all around the ger, for miles and miles in every direction, the steppe lies silent and white under the moon, and the cold goes on, and none of it can reach you here.

Mönggen stays kneeling there for a long moment, her hands resting on her knees, looking at the banked fire. Then she bows her head toward it, just slightly. And she lies down on her own bed, close beside the hearth, where she can reach the fire in the night, and pulls her furs around her.

Under its blanket of ash, at the center of the round dark house, beneath the closed eye of the roof, the fire breathes on, slow and red and warm.

## Part Eleven — Around Midnight: Everyone in Their Place

Around midnight, the house is asleep. All but one.

Who wakes in the night, when everyone else is sleeping, to look after the fire?

It is Mönggen. It is always Mönggen. Old people often sleep lightly, and she has slept lightly for years now, waking two or three times in a night, the way she once woke for her babies. She does not mind it. She lies for a moment in the dark, under her furs, listening. Then she slips out from under them, and kneels by the hearth, and with her blunt iron rod she lifts a little of the ash away from the banked fire, very gently. Underneath, the embers are glowing, deep red, warm and patient. She lays one more pale cake against them, and covers it all again with ash, and sits back on her heels.

The cold has deepened while the family slept. Mönggen can feel it on her face, a little sharper than at bedtime, and she can see it too. Near the door, where the felt flap meets the frame, a fine white fur of frost has grown along the inside edge, glittering faintly in the red light, where the warm breath of the house has met the cold of the night and frozen. On the lattice nearest the door, a few of the willow rods have a thin silver rime on them. But a pace or two in from the walls, the air is still mild, and around the hearth it is warm. The felt is holding, just as she said it would. Even the thin place on the north wall, above her son's bed, is holding.

She listens. There is no wind tonight. Only the small sounds: the tick of the embers, the breathing of the sleepers, the faint creak of the roof poles as the cold tightens the ropes outside. Once, from the fold, the soft cough of a sheep. Once, far away, so faint that she is not sure she heard it, the call of an owl down by the river. She knows every one of these sounds. She has been listening to them, on winter nights like this, for more than sixty years. They are the sounds of a house that is safe.

And then, before she lies down again, she looks around the ger, at her sleeping family. She does it every night. It is the last thing she does before she sleeps, and the first thing she does when she wakes.

You are lying on the west side of the ger, near the door, under your heavy fur coat. Your eyes are half closed. And you look around the ger too, with her, in the faint red glow of the banked fire and the thin silver stripe of moonlight from the roof.

On the north side, directly across from the door, on the low wooden bed with its furs, lies Bolad, on his back, one arm flung out, breathing slow and deep. Beside him, at the eastern end of the bed, lies Saran, curled toward her children, her hand under her cheek. On the east side, the right as you came in, near the kitchen things, the milk bags and the churn and the bowls, Gerel lies on her side under her furs, and Toghan beside her, still holding his bag of bones. On the west side, the left as you came in, near the saddle and the bow case and the coiled horsehair rope, lies Temür, rolled up like a caterpillar, with only the top of his hat showing. And there by the hearth, between the north and the east, kneels Mönggen, the keeper of the fire.

Everyone in their place. Every night the same. And every day too. Since this afternoon, you have watched each of them go, without thinking, to their own side of the house. Bolad to the north. Saran and Gerel to the east. Temür to the west, to hang up his bow. Even you, the visitor, were given a place on the west side, near the door.

And so we come to the last of our three questions. Why does every person who comes into this round house, the grandmother, the smallest child, even a stranger in from the snow, go to one fixed place, and sit there, and nowhere else?

The answer is that the ger is not just a house. It is a map. It is a small, round picture of the whole world, and of the family's place in it, and everyone who steps over the threshold knows how to read it.

The door faces south, toward the sun, toward the warmth and the light. The north side, at the back, opposite the door, is the place of honor. It is the side farthest from the cold air of the doorway, the side nearest the sheltering hill outside, the side where the most precious things of the house were kept. And that is where the master of the house sits, and sleeps, and where the most honored guests are invited to sit beside him.

The east side, on the master's left as he sits facing the door, is the women's side. That is where the cooking things are kept, and the milk, and the butter, and the bowls, and the cradle, all the things of the household's food and its children. The west side, on the master's right, is the men's side. That is where the saddles and the bridles hang, and the bows, and the tools, and the things of the horses and the herds. And the space near the door, at the south, is the humblest place, the place for children, for servants, and for strangers who have not yet been invited further in.

William of Rubruck saw exactly this, in every dwelling he entered, and he described it carefully, so that the people at home in France would understand. He wrote that when they set up a dwelling, they always turned its door to the south. The master's couch, he wrote, was always on the north side. The women's place was always on the east, that is, on the left of the master as he sat on his couch looking south. The men's place was on the west, on his right. And he noticed that a man coming into the house would never hang his bow on the women's side. Above the master's head, he saw a small figure made of felt, hanging on the wall, which the people called the master's brother, and another above the mistress, and others beside the door, one on each side, to watch over the household and its herds. Everything in the round house, Rubruck saw, had its proper place, and so did everyone.

And so, when a visitor lifts the felt flap of a stranger's ger, he does not need to be told where to go. He steps over the threshold, without touching it, and turns to the west, to the men's side, and waits there to be invited further in. The family does not need to give orders, or to argue about who sits where. A small house, with six people and a fire and a winter's worth of work in it, becomes a peaceful one, because everybody already knows their place in it, and everybody's place is respected. The fixed places are a kind of quiet courtesy, built into the felt and the wood of the house itself.

Historians do not agree on how strictly these places were kept in every ordinary home. Some think that a small, busy family, in a small ger, must often have sat wherever there was room, and kept the order of places mostly for guests and feast days. Others think the arrangement was so deep a part of life that people kept it without even thinking, every day, as Rubruck seems to have seen. In our household tonight, it is simply how things are.

Mönggen looks round once more at her sleeping family, each one in their place. Bolad on the north. Saran beside him. Gerel and Toghan on the east. Temür on the west. And you, near the door.

Then she lies down again, beside the hearth, and pulls her furs around her, and closes her eyes.

The house is very quiet now. Quieter than it has been all day. So quiet that you can hear the faint whisper of the felt as the roof poles shift, very slightly, in the cold, and then settle again.

The banked fire breathes under its ash. Every so often, a tiny sound comes from it, a soft tick, a faint settling, like someone turning over in their sleep. A thin thread of smoke rises from the gray mound and drifts up, slowly, toward the narrow gap at the top of the roof ring, and slips out into the night.

The silver stripe of moonlight has moved a little across the floor since bedtime. It lies now across the edge of the felt near the hearth, pale and soft.

Toghan sighs in his sleep, and murmurs, and his fingers loosen a little on the bag of bones. Gerel turns over, and her hand finds her brother's shoulder in the dark, and rests there.

Mönggen's breathing, by the hearth, has grown slow and soft and even, like the breathing of the fire beside her.

Temür sleeps on, without moving, the way only a boy of fifteen can sleep, after a long day in the cold with the horses.

On the north bed, Bolad's breathing is slow and deep and even. Saran has drawn the edge of the fur up under her chin. Her face, in the faint red light of the hearth, is calm and smooth, the lines of the day's work gone from it.

You can hear all of them breathing. Six people breathing, slow and soft, in a round felt house, in the middle of the winter steppe. And the breathing of the fire under its ash.

Outside, the dog Bars lies curled against the felt wall by the door, his nose under his tail, his thick coat frosted white. Every now and then his ear twitches. But he does not wake.

The sheep in the fold lie pressed together, in one great soft, warm, woolly mass. The horses at the hitching line stand with their heads low, dozing on their feet, their breath rising faintly in the moonlight. And up on the slope, the herd stands close together, tails to the north, still and quiet, under the stars.

There is no wind. The moon is high now, and small, and very bright, and the snow is silver all the way to the frozen river, and beyond it, to the hills.

And in here, under the felt, it is warm.

Somewhere, a long way off, in other valleys, under the same moon, other round houses are sleeping too. The uncle's ger, over the next fold of the hills, with its own thread of smoke and its own banked fire. The households beyond that, and beyond those, scattered across the white steppe, each one in its own sheltered fold, each one with its door turned to the south and its fire glowing in the middle. And somewhere far to the south, in the great camp of the khan, a friar from Flanders is spending this same winter night a long way from home, where fires of thorns and roots and cow dung burn low in the hearths.

All of them under felt. All of them warm.

The silver stripe from the roof ring creeps on, a little further across the floor. It touches the edge of Mönggen's furs now, and lies there, pale and still, across the old woman's hand, where it rests on the sheepskin. She does not feel it. She is already asleep.

Let your breathing slow down now, with theirs. In, and out. Let the weight of the fur coat settle on your shoulders. Let your hands lie loose. Feel the deep soft felt beneath you, holding you up. Feel the faint warmth of the hearth on your face, and the cool quiet dark of the round room all around you. You are safe here. You are warm. Everyone is in their place, and the fire is keeping watch.

There is nothing more to do tonight. The animals are in. The roof ring is closed. The door is tied. The boots are drying by the hearth, and the socks are warm, and the bowls are stacked on the east side, ready for the morning. The broth is waiting in the cauldron. The felt is holding. Everyone is home.

So you can let go of the day now, the way they have. Let go of the cold of the afternoon, and the long walk through the snow, and the bright hard glitter of the moonlit valley. Let it all drift away, like the smoke through the roof ring. Let the warmth come in its place. Slow, and soft, and heavy.

Breathe in, and smell the faint sweet smoke, and the wool, and the felt. Breathe out, slowly, and feel your shoulders sink a little deeper into the sheepskins. Breathe in again. And out.

Somewhere close by, Toghan murmurs again, something soft and sleepy and without any words at all. Then he is quiet.

The fire under the ash ticks, softly. The smoke rises. The house sleeps.

And in the middle of it all, under the roof ring, the little heap of embers glows on, red and patient, beneath its gray blanket, keeping the whole house warm.

## Part Twelve — Around Five in the Morning: The Small Hours, and the First Light in the Roof Ring

The night goes on, slow and quiet. One o'clock. Two. Three. Four. The moon swings across the sky, and the thin silver stripe that comes down through the roof ring moves with it, inch by inch, across the floor of the ger, from the door, past the hearth, toward the west wall.

How does a house of felt pass the darkest hours of a winter night?

Very gently. That is all. Nothing happens. Nothing needs to happen. The fire breathes under its ash. The family breathes under their furs. The felt holds the warmth in, and the cold out, just as it has held it every night this winter, and every winter before.

Outside, the moon is sinking now, toward the western hills, and growing larger and more golden as it sinks. The shadows on the snow have turned around since midnight, and stretch long and blue toward the east. The stars, which were washed pale by the moonlight, are coming back, one by one, brighter and thicker, as the moon goes down. Up on the hill, the horses stand together, quiet and still. The Seven Old Men have walked a long way round the Golden Stake since bedtime, and they are tipped now, low in the north, the way they always are when the morning is near.

Around five o'clock, Mönggen wakes again, as she always does.

She lies for a moment, listening. All is well. Then she slips out from under her furs, and kneels by the hearth, and with her blunt iron rod she lifts away a little of the ash. The embers are still there, red and warm. She lays one more pale cake against them, the last of the night, and covers it gently with ash again, and watches for a moment as a faint thread of smoke rises from the gray mound, and drifts up toward the gap in the roof ring.

She looks round at her family. Everyone in their place.

Then she lies down again, but she does not sleep this time. She lies with her eyes open, warm under her furs, and waits for the light. It is the best part of her night, this quiet hour before the others wake. She likes to be the first to see the morning come.

You are lying on the west side, near the door, warm and drowsy, half awake and half asleep. You can feel the deep soft felt under you, and the weight of the fur coat over you, and the faint warmth of the hearth on your face. Your feet are warm. Your hands are warm. Everything is soft and dim and still.

And you can hear the house breathing.

Bolad, on the north bed, slow and deep and even, with a faint rumble at the end of each breath. Saran beside him, softer, lighter, almost silent. Gerel, on the east side, breathing quick and small, like a bird. Toghan beside her, with a little whistle in his nose. Temür, on the west side, so quiet that you can hardly hear him at all. And Mönggen, by the hearth, awake, breathing slowly, watching the roof.

Outside, the dog Bars stirs, and stretches, and settles again.

Let yourself listen to it, the way Mönggen does. There is nothing to watch for, and nothing to wait for. There is only the sound of people sleeping, safe and warm, in a house of felt, and the soft breath of the fire.

Think back, if you like, over the long day. The white afternoon, and the smoke rising straight up from the roof ring. The sheep pouring down the slope, and Saran counting them with her eyes. The warmth that met you like a bath when you first stepped over the high sill. The felt wall, cool and soft under your hand, trembling faintly when the wind touched it, and holding. The little sour cup of curd. The plain, rich mutton, and the hot golden broth. The pale dry cakes in Mönggen's basket, and the quiet, steady way they burned. The click of the shagai on the felt. The old story of the widow and her five sons, and the yellow man of light coming down through the smoke-hole. The moonlit valley, glittering. Gerel pulling the cord, hand over hand, and the stars narrowing to a thread.

And every one of them, now, at rest.

The fire has burned all night, under its ash, without a flame. That is the quiet magic of it. It does not need to blaze. It only needs to be fed a little, and covered, and trusted. A single small heart of red embers, in the middle of a round house, in the middle of a frozen valley, in the middle of the great white steppe. And it is enough.

Let your breathing slow, the way theirs has. In, and out. Let the blanket be heavy and warm on your shoulders. Let your hands lie loose. Feel the faint, steady warmth of the hearth on your face, and the soft dark of the round room all around you, and the deep quiet of the whole sleeping valley beyond the felt. You are safe here. You are warm. You can stay as long as you like.

The embers tick. The roof poles creak, very faintly, as the cold outside reaches its deepest point. And then even that sound fades, and there is only the breathing again, slow and soft, six people and a fire.

The hours go on. Six. Then nearly seven.

Long ago, the friar Carpini, who came to the steppe in 1245, looked up at the roof of a house like this one, and wrote that at the top in the middle there is a round opening "which lets in the light." And that is what it is waiting to do now.

Somewhere around seven o'clock, very slowly, something begins to change.

It is very slight at first. Through the narrow gap at the top of the roof ring, where all night there has been only black sky and stars, the sky is no longer quite black. It is a very deep blue. And then a little paler. The stars in that small gap fade, one by one, until only one is left, small and white. Then that one goes too.

Outside, along the line of the eastern hills, a thin band of light is spreading. Gray at first, and then the palest, coldest green, and then a faint wash of pink, very soft, above the snow. The whole valley begins to come out of the darkness, shape by shape. The ridge to the north. The fold, with its sleeping sheep. The line of horses at the door, frosted white. The long bare slope. The frozen river, far off, a thin pale line.

And inside the ger, the light comes down through the gap in the roof ring, gray and soft, and touches the top of the roof poles, so that for the first time since bedtime you can see their faded red, and the round dark wood of the ring itself, and the gray mound of ash in the hearth beneath it, with its one thin thread of smoke rising.

Mönggen watches it come. She smiles to herself, in the gray light, under her furs.

It is the same light that came down through this roof ring on the morning Gerel was born. The same light, her grandmother likes to think, that came down through another smoke-hole, a long time ago, in the story of Alan Qo'a. The same light that has come down through the roof rings of the steppe, every winter morning, for longer than anyone can remember. And it touches the ash in the hearth, softly, and the thread of smoke rising from it, and the faded red of the poles.

Outside, the light is growing. The pink along the eastern hills deepens to rose, and then to a pale clear gold. The snow on the long slope catches it, and begins, very faintly, to shine. The frost on the backs of the horses glitters. In the fold, a ewe lifts her head, and looks toward the east, and lowers it again. The dog Bars opens one eye, and closes it. The thin thread of smoke from the roof ring rises straight up into the still, cold, brightening air, the first thing in the whole valley to be touched by the sun.

But inside, it is still dim, and still warm, and still quiet. The light has only just begun.

In a little while, she will get up, and kneel at the hearth, and brush the ash away from the embers, and give them a little air, and lay on fresh fuel, and blow on it gently, once, and the fire will wake, and glow, and rise up bright and warm under the roof ring, for the new day.

In a little while after that, Gerel will wake, and remember, and jump up to pull the cord, and the felt will slide back from the top of the roof ring, and the whole round of the morning sky will be there, pale and clear and full of light. Saran will set the cauldron on the fire to warm last night's broth. Bolad will pull on his boots, and go out to look at the gray mare's foot. Temür will go up the slope to the horses, his breath white in the cold, and count them, and find them all there. And Toghan will wake last of all, and find the bag of bones still in his hand, and remember that today, if his grandmother says yes, he can ride out with his brother to the ridge.

And she will say yes. She always does. And the two brothers will ride out together across the bright snow, under the clear sky, the big one and the small one, and come home again at midday with red cheeks and enormous appetites, to a warm ger and a full cauldron and their grandmother's fire.

But not yet. Not yet.

For now, everyone is still asleep.

The light in the roof ring grows a little, and a little more, but it is gentle light, gray and soft, and it does not wake anyone. It lies on the furs, and on the felt, and on the sleeping faces, like a second, thinner blanket. Toghan's cheek is pink with warmth. Gerel's hair has come loose from its braid and lies across the sheepskin. Saran's hand has slipped down from under her cheek, and lies open on the fur. Bolad has turned on his side toward the hearth. Temür has not moved at all.

The house is still holding its breath of warmth, the warmth of the whole night, gathered under the felt. In a little while, when the roof ring opens and the door flap lifts, some of it will escape into the morning. But not yet. For now, it is all still here, wrapped around the sleepers, soft and close and deep.

And you can stay in it a little longer. There is no need to get up. There is no need to do anything at all.

Bolad is asleep on the north bed, his big hands loose on the fur, his boots warm by the hearth. Saran is asleep beside him, curled toward her children, her face calm and smooth. Temür is asleep on the west side, rolled up in his furs, dreaming perhaps of the horses, and of riding out alone in the spring. Gerel is asleep on the east side, with her hand on her brother's shoulder, her name the name of the light that is coming. And Toghan is asleep beside her, small and warm, with his bag of little bones held tight in one fist, and his felt socks on his feet.

Outside, Bars is asleep by the door, frosted white, his nose under his tail. The sheep are asleep in the fold. The horses doze on their feet at the hitching line, and up on the hill.

And Mönggen, the keeper of the fire, lies awake and warm in the first gray light, watching over all of them, as she has watched over them all night long.

The house is quiet. The house is warm. The night has passed safely, every hour of it.

And there, in the very center of the round felt house, beneath the roof ring, where the first pale light of morning comes down through the gap and touches the hearth, the little fire is still glowing under its blanket of ash, red and patient and alive, just as it was when we found it yesterday afternoon. It has kept the family warm all night. It has kept its small red heart burning in the dark, through the coldest hours of a steppe winter. And now, as the light comes down to meet it through the eye of the roof, it is waiting, quietly, for the morning.

If you enjoyed spending this winter night with Mönggen and her family, a like or a subscription would mean a great deal, but only if you truly enjoyed it. And if there is a time or a place you would love to spend a night in, leave it in the comments, because your ideas truly help.

Next time, we will visit a very different kind of house, on a very different kind of night, somewhere far from the open steppe.

Sleep well, friend. Good night.
