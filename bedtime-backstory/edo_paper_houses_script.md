It is a January afternoon in Edo, the great city of the shoguns, in the year 1830. A dry wind comes off the bay and runs along a narrow street of wooden shops, rattling their cloth curtains. The sky over the rooftiles is the pale color of rice water. Inside one small house, behind walls of paper as thin as a moth's wing, a grandmother lifts the edge of a heavy quilt and looks underneath. There, in a little bed of gray ash, a few coals of charcoal glow orange, quiet and patient, like a sleeping animal breathing.

So tonight, I would like you to carry three small questions with you. The first is this. Why would anyone build a house with paper walls, in a city where snow falls? The second. How do six people sleep warm, all night long, around one small pot of charcoal? And the third. Why does a whole street, dozens of families, fear one single spark? These are not three separate questions, really. They are one question, asked three ways, and the answer is the house itself. We will open the first one before supper is cleared, the second at bedtime, and the third when the house is nearly asleep.

For now, let your head grow heavy on the pillow. Let your shoulders loosen and drop, the way a quilt settles when it is laid down. Your eyes may close whenever they like. You do not need to remember anything. So let us begin.

The street is called a *machi*, a ward, and it lies in the district of Nihonbashi, a few minutes' walk north of the great wooden bridge that gives the district its name. The great highways of the country are all measured from that bridge. Along both sides of the street stand shops, shoulder to shoulder, each one a narrow face of dark wood and pale paper, each one with a short cloth curtain hanging in its doorway, dyed with the shop's name. There is a shop for tea, a shop for lacquered combs, a shop for cotton cloth, and a shop for soy sauce that you can smell from four doors away.

And here, in the middle of the row, is our shop. Its curtain is deep indigo, and on it, in white, is written a single word, *Minoya*. It means, roughly, the house of Mino. Mino is a province far to the west, in the mountains, famous for one thing above all. Its paper. The Minoya sells paper.

This family never quite existed under one name, but every detail of their night is real.

The master of the shop is Kihei. He is forty-four years old, a paper dealer, a careful, square-handed man with a quiet voice and a habit of rubbing a sheet of paper between his thumb and finger before he says a word about it. He buys paper from wholesalers who bring it east by boat and by packhorse, and he sells it to the whole neighborhood: to the schoolmaster, to the shrine, to the woodblock printers, to the man who makes paper lanterns, and to every family who needs to mend a screen.

His wife is O-Tami. She is thirty-nine, and she runs the kitchen, the money box and, in a quiet way, the master. The little "O" in front of her name is a polite way of speaking of a woman, a little like saying "Miss" or "Mrs." She keeps the accounts at night with a wooden abacus, and the click of its beads is one of the sounds of this house.

Kihei's mother is O-Sei. She is sixty-eight, a widow, small and straight-backed, with gray hair pulled into a neat knot. She has lived in Edo her whole life and has seen more winters than anyone on the street. Her job, which nobody gave her and nobody would dare take away, is the fire.

Their son is Shinkichi, fifteen, the heir. One day the indigo curtain will be his. For now he sits behind the counter learning prices, learning the abacus, learning how to bow to a customer just deeply enough.

Their daughter is O-Fumi, eight years old, who is learning to write her letters at a small temple school down the street, and who is very proud of her brush.

And there is Chōkichi, twelve, the apprentice. He came to Edo two years ago from a farming village across the bay, sent by his parents to learn a trade. He sweeps, he runs errands, he carries bundles of paper on his back, and he sleeps in the shop. He will not be paid real wages for years. But he is fed and clothed, and he is part of the house.

Six people. One narrow house. And one small fire.

That fire lives in the back room, and it is our light for the whole of tonight. It is called the *kotatsu*. In the floor of the back room, a small square pit has been cut and lined with clay. In the pit, in a bed of fine ash, sit a few pieces of glowing charcoal. Over the pit stands a low wooden frame, like a little table with open sides. And over the frame lies a thick cotton quilt, big enough to spread out across the floor on every side. You slide your legs under the quilt, toward the warm center, and the quilt holds the heat around you like a cupped hand. That is all it is. And it is enough.

Here is a small question to start us off, one we will answer before this part is done. What kind of house is this, exactly?

The common answer is that it is a *machiya*, a town house, the ordinary home of a shopkeeping family in a Japanese city. The front of the house is the shop, open to the street in the daytime. Behind the shop are the living rooms, one after another, like beads on a string. Running down one side of the house, from the street all the way to the back, is a strip of bare earthen floor, packed hard and swept smooth. You keep your sandals on there. You step up from it, out of your sandals, onto the raised wooden floor of the rooms. The kitchen is on that earthen strip, toward the back, with its clay stove and its big water jar. At the very back is a scrap of garden, a well shared with the neighbors, and a small storehouse.

The front of a house like this is narrow, often not much wider than a living room in a modern apartment, but it runs deep, a long way back from the street. In Kyoto people joked that such houses were the beds of eels. The rooms are floored with *tatami*, thick mats of tightly packed rice straw covered with woven rush. Each mat is about six feet long and three feet wide, and rooms are measured by how many mats they hold. The back room of the Minoya is a six-mat room. That is about the size of a small bedroom today.

And the walls. Some of the walls are plaster, smoothed over bamboo lath. But many of the walls are not really walls at all. They are sliding screens. The inside ones, called *fusuma*, are thick, covered in heavy paper on both sides. The ones that face the light, called *shōji*, are a lattice of thin wooden bars with a single sheet of white paper pasted across them. When the sun falls on a shōji screen, the whole wall glows. It does not let you see out. But it lets the daylight in, soft and even, like the light inside a cloud.

So that is the answer. A machiya is a deep, narrow wooden house of mats and sliding paper, with a shop at the front and a fire at the heart.

Now for something you might not expect. This house is new. It is only a few months old. The tatami still smell sweetly of fresh rush, a green, hay-like smell. The cedar posts are still pale. There is a reason for that.

In the spring of last year, in 1829, on the twenty-first day of the third month by the Japanese calendar, which fell in late April by our own, a fire broke out to the north of here, in the district of Kanda, near a place called Sakuma-chō. A strong wind was blowing. The fire ran south across the merchant quarters, through street after street, through Nihonbashi itself and beyond. When it was over, a great part of the commoners' city was ash. The Minoya was ash.

So the house you are standing in was built on the same ground, to almost the same plan, in a matter of months. The carpenters of Edo were very good at this. They had a great deal of practice. And this is part of the answer to one of our questions, though we will come back to it properly later, after supper.

There is a book written by an American who came to Japan almost fifty years after our night, and who loved these houses more than almost any foreigner before him. His name was Edward Sylvester Morse. He was a zoologist from Massachusetts, an expert on shellfish, who came to Japan in 1877 to study sea creatures and stayed to study everything. In 1886 he published a book called *Japanese Homes and Their Surroundings*, full of his own careful drawings of rooms, screens, roofs, gates and kitchens.

He did not miss the little fire pots that warmed every room. He wrote, "An inseparable accompaniment of every Japanese home, from the most exalted to the very humblest, is the hibachi." A hibachi is a fire bowl, a cousin of the kotatsu: a box or jar of fine ash, with charcoal glowing inside. Morse described it exactly, as "a vessel partially filled with fine ashes, containing when in use a few bits of burning charcoal." He was writing about Tokyo in the 1880s. But in Edo in 1830, it was just the same.

Let us step inside. You come in from the street, ducking under the indigo curtain. The shop floor is raised a little above the earth, and on it, in neat stacks, lie bundles of paper wrapped in plain brown covers and tied with straw cord. Some sheets are thick and creamy, for letters and account books. Some are so fine you could read through them. The air smells of paper, which is a clean, faintly sweet smell, like dry grass and flour.

Behind the counter sits a hibachi, a square wooden box lined with copper, filled with ash. In the ash, a few coals are buried, and an iron kettle sits on a trivet above them, ticking softly as it warms. Kihei holds his hands over it while he talks to a customer. You hold your hands over it too, and the warmth rises into your palms, gentle, like holding a cup of tea without the cup.

You notice your breath. It is not quite white indoors, but almost. It is cold in here. Not freezing, not dangerous, but cold, in the way a stone floor is cold. The paper screens let in the light, and they also let in the air. There is no glass in any window. There is no chimney. There is no stove in the middle of the room roaring with heat.

You walk down the earthen passage in your sandals, past the kitchen, where a pot of something with ginger in it is beginning to steam, and you step up into the back room. And here, at last, is the kotatsu. O-Sei sits with her legs tucked under its quilt, her back very straight, a little sewing in her lap. She looks up at you, then nods at the empty side of the quilt, as if to say, well, sit down, before you catch your death.

You kneel, and slide your legs under. And there it is. The warmth wraps around your shins and knees and feet all at once, a deep, dry, toasting warmth that seems to go straight into the bone. Your shoulders are still cold. Your nose is cold. But from the waist down you are in summer.

Today, most of us heat the air of a whole house, every room, all day, and we think of that as what being warm means. In 1830 Edo, almost nobody heated the air of a room. They heated people. A bowl of coals for the hands, a quilt for the legs, padded clothes for the body, and the rest of the house could be as cold as it liked.

Historians are not of one mind about how people felt about this. Some, reading the complaints of foreign visitors, argue that Japanese houses were simply cold in winter, and that people endured it because good timber and paper were what they had. Others point out that the people who lived in these houses rarely wrote about the cold as a hardship at all. To them, a cold room with a warm kotatsu was not a failure of heating. It was how winter felt, and winter was a season to be lived in, like any other.

O-Sei lifts the edge of the quilt again, peers under, and with a pair of long iron chopsticks she nudges one coal a little closer to another. The two coals touch, and brighten.

"It is going to be a cold one," she says, to nobody in particular.

From the shop, Kihei calls a polite farewell to his customer. Shinkichi's abacus clicks. Out in the kitchen O-Tami lifts a lid, and a breath of steam rolls along the earthen floor. Somewhere toward the back of the house, little O-Fumi is practicing her characters with a brush, her tongue at the corner of her mouth, and Chōkichi has just come in from an errand, cheeks red from the wind, stamping his feet on the beaten earth.

The light through the shōji is going from white to gold. It is the middle of the afternoon, and the short winter day has perhaps an hour and a half left in it. Somewhere down the street a woman is calling her children. Somewhere farther off, a dog barks once and stops.

O-Fumi comes in on her stockinged feet, holding up her practice sheet for her grandmother to see. Four characters, wobbly but brave. O-Sei looks at them for a long moment, very seriously, and then says that the third one is very good. O-Fumi beams, and slides her legs under the quilt beside her, and leans against her grandmother's shoulder.

Under the quilt, in the little square pit, the charcoal glows on, orange and quiet, the warm heart of the paper house.

## Part Two — Around Half Past Four in the Afternoon: Closing the Paper House

The gold light has slid down the screens to the floor now, and the shadows of the lattice bars lie long across the mats. Out in the shop, Kihei looks at the street, looks at the sky, and nods once to the boys. It is time to close the house.

Here is a small question for this part. How do you lock up a house whose walls are made of paper?

The answer begins with a sound, the one sound that, more than any other, means evening in a Japanese town. It is a low wooden rumble, a long sliding roll, followed by a soft knock. Then another. Then another, all along the street, house after house, like a slow drumroll passing from door to door. It is the sound of the *amado*, the rain doors.

Amado are wooden shutters, tall, thin panels of plain board, each one about as wide as a person and as tall as a door. In the daytime they are not to be seen at all. They are stacked, one against the next, inside a narrow wooden box built onto the end of the house, a kind of cupboard for doors. At dusk you pull them out, one at a time, and slide them along a groove that runs across the outside edge of the house, in front of the paper screens. Each one pushes the one before it along, until the whole side of the house is sealed behind a wall of wood. Then a small bolt or a wooden peg goes in at the end, and the house is shut.

At the front of a shop, the closing is a little different. The whole shop front, which has been open to the street all day, is closed with heavy boards that fit into slots, one beside the next, with a little door left in one of them, low enough that you must stoop to pass through. The shop curtain with its name is taken in and folded. After dark, if a customer knocks, the master can still sell a sheet of paper through that little door.

So the answer to our small question is that you do not lock the paper. You lock the wood that covers it. By night, the paper house becomes a wooden box, and the paper screens stand inside it like a second, inner skin.

And between the two skins, the outer wood and the inner paper, there is a narrow layer of still air. Still air is one of the best friends a cold house can have. It does not carry warmth away the way a moving draft does. The amado stop the wind. The shōji stop the drafts that creep around the edges of the amado. And the people inside sit in the stillness between.

Look up at the front of the Minoya from the street, before the light goes, and you will see that the paper house is not all paper. Above the shop there is a low second floor, so low under its roof that a grown man can barely stand up straight in it. Its front is not screens but thick plaster, with a narrow window made of plastered bars set close together, like the ribs of a basket. The roof is gray clay tile, not wood shingle and not thatch. The plaster and the tile are new, like everything else, but they are not new ideas. For a hundred years, ever since the 1720s, the city's magistrates had been urging the merchants of the main streets to build their fronts this way, with tile above and plaster on the outside, so that a spark blowing down the street would find less to catch on. Many could not afford it. The ones who could, did.

So the merchant house of Edo is a kind of compromise, and you can read the compromise in its skin. Toward the street, where the danger is, it wears tile and mud. Toward the inside, where the family lives, it is light timber, mats and paper, open to the light and easy to rebuild.

Now for the paper itself, because this is a paper merchant's house, and Kihei would not forgive us for skipping it.

Japanese paper, *washi*, is not made from wood pulp the way most of our paper is today. It is made mostly from the inner bark of a shrub called *kōzo*, the paper mulberry. Farmers cut the long stems in late autumn, steam them, and strip off the bark. Then they scrape away the dark outer layer, soak the pale inner bark, boil it with ash to soften it, pick it clean by hand, fiber by fiber, and beat it with wooden mallets until it falls apart into a soft, white, cloudy mass. That mass is stirred into a vat of cold water, along with a slippery liquid made from the root of a plant, and the papermaker dips a fine bamboo screen into the vat, again and again, rocking it gently, so that the long fibers lie down and cross over one another in thin layers.

And here is the surprising part. The finest paper of the year is made in the dead of winter. The papermakers of Mino, and of the other paper villages, believed that paper made in the coldest weeks, with water so cold it made their hands ache, came out the strongest, the whitest and the cleanest of all. The cold water kept the fibers firm and kept the vat fresh. They even had a name for it, *kanzuki*, paper made in the season of cold.

So while you sit in a cold house in Edo, in some mountain village far to the west, a woman is standing at a vat of icy water, rocking a screen, making the paper that will one day glow in somebody's window.

Because the fibers are so long, and so well crossed, a sheet of good washi is astonishingly strong for its thickness. You can crumple it and smooth it out again. A child's finger can poke a hole in it, certainly, and children did, constantly. But it does not shred the way modern paper does. And it lets through a great deal of light while it stops the eye. Mino paper was especially prized for exactly this, for shōji, and Kihei's customers come to him for it by name.

There is a custom tied to that paper, too. At the end of the year, before the New Year arrives, many families strip the old paper from their shōji, wash the wooden lattice, and paste on fresh white sheets, so that the house can begin the year clean and bright. Kihei has been busy all month.

Edward Morse, our zoologist from Massachusetts, was charmed by the rain doors. In his book of 1886, he described how the outer side of a Japanese house is closed at night by these wooden sliding shutters, and how by day they vanish into a small closet at the end of the veranda, so that a whole side of the house can stand open to the garden and the air. He drew the little door-closets carefully, and the grooves the shutters ran in.

And then there is the noise. Every evening, and again every morning, the rolling and knocking of the shutters goes up and down the street. It is one of the characteristic sounds of a Japanese town. A traveler arriving at an inn after dark would hear the house being shut up around him, panel by panel, as if it were folding its hands for the night.

Now it is your turn. You step down into your sandals on the earthen floor and go out through the little door at the front of the shop, into the street. The cold meets you at once. The wind has teeth now that the sun is low, and it smells of the river and of woodsmoke and, faintly, of snow somewhere far away.

Chōkichi is already at the box at the end of the house. He reaches in and grips the edge of the first shutter, and you take the next. The wood is cold and smooth, worn by years of hands, though this shutter is new, and still smells of fresh-cut cedar. You lift it, settle its bottom edge into the groove, and push. It rolls along with that low wooden rumble you have been hearing all down the street, and knocks gently against the one before.

Another. Another. Your fingers are going numb. Chōkichi grins at you and blows on his hands. He is twelve, and he has done this every evening for two years, and he is quick. With each panel, a slice of the lit house disappears behind wood. The last gold glow through the paper goes out of sight. Then there is the peg, pushed home, and the side of the house is a plain dark wall.

At the front, Shinkichi and Kihei fit the heavy boards into their slots, one after another. The indigo curtain comes down. Kihei rolls it, carefully, so that the white letters of the shop's name are not creased, and carries it inside like something precious.

You stoop through the little door last of all, and slide it shut behind you. And the difference is immediate. The wind is gone. The rattle of the street is gone. The air inside is still cold, but it is quiet cold, the cold of a closed cupboard rather than an open field. You can hear the kettle on the hibachi ticking. You can hear your own breathing.

A modern house keeps its warmth behind thick walls packed with insulation, and windows of double glass with a sealed gap between the panes. A house in Edo had no glass and almost no insulation, but it had the same idea. Wood, then a gap of still air, then paper. It was a layered house, the way a person in winter is a layered person.

Historians differ on how much this really helped. Some argue that paper and thin boards hold almost no heat at all, and that the layered house mostly kept out wind, not cold, so that a room in Edo in January could be nearly as chilly as the street at dawn. Others answer that keeping out the wind is most of the battle, and that a house only needs to be calm, not warm, if the people inside are dressed for the season and have a kotatsu to put their legs under.

Inside, O-Tami has lit a small lamp in the kitchen, and its light makes the earthen floor look soft and brown. She is cutting a long white daikon radish into thick rounds with steady strokes of her knife. A pot of water steams on the clay stove.

Kihei comes through with the rolled curtain and lays it on its shelf. He looks along the inside of the closed front boards, checking each one with his hand, the way his father did, and his father's father.

"The wind is from the northwest," he says.

O-Tami does not look up from her radish. "Then it will be dry."

"It will be dry," he agrees.

Kihei goes to the paper stacks at the back of the shop and lays his palm flat on the top bundle, then on the one below, feeling for damp. Paper hates damp almost as much as it hates fire. In summer, in the long wet season, he has to air the stock and watch for spots of mold. In winter the danger is the opposite. The air is so dry that the sheets crackle and curl at their edges, and the room fills with a fine paper dust that tickles the nose. He nods to himself. The stock is fine.

Neither of them says anything more about it. They do not need to. In this city, a dry northwest wind in winter is something every grown person notices, the way a farmer notices rain. It is the season of fires, and the wind decides how far a fire runs. But that is a thought for a little later. For now it simply means that tonight, everyone in the house will be a little more careful with the coals.

In the back room, O-Fumi has wriggled further under the kotatsu quilt, so that only her head and shoulders stick out, and she is telling her grandmother a long story about a girl at school who has a new comb. O-Sei listens, nodding, her needle going in and out. Chōkichi comes in rubbing his hands, kneels at the edge of the quilt, and looks at it with naked longing.

"Hands first," O-Sei tells him, and lifts the edge of the quilt for him to slide his frozen fingers under. He does, and closes his eyes, and a small, involuntary sound of happiness comes out of him.

Shinkichi, who at fifteen is too dignified to make such sounds, sits down on the other side with his abacus, and slides his own hands under the quilt as well, very casually, as if it were an accident.

Under the quilt, all those hands and feet gather in toward the center, toward the little square pit where the charcoal glows in its ash, and the house, shut and quiet now, settles around its one warm heart.

## Part Three — Around Half Past Five in the Evening: The Bell at Dusk

The sun goes down over the western hills at about ten past five, and the cold comes up out of the ground to meet the dark. Inside the shut house, the last gray light fades slowly from the paper, and then, when it is nearly gone, from somewhere over the rooftops, a bell begins to ring.

Here is a small question for this part. In a city with almost no clocks, how does anyone know when evening has begun?

The answer is that bell. Edo tells the time by temple bells, great bronze bells hung in wooden towers, struck from the outside with a swinging log. There are a number of them scattered across the city, and each one rings the hours for its own neighborhood. The oldest and most famous of them hangs in a tower at a place called Hon-ishichō, here in Nihonbashi, only a few streets from the Minoya. The bell that hangs there tonight was cast in 1711, and its deep voice carries over thousands of rooftops.

The way it rings is particular. First come three single strokes, spaced apart, a warning, as if the bell were clearing its throat to say, listen now. Then comes the count of the hour itself, each stroke left to hum and fade before the next.

But the count is strange to our ears. The Japanese of 1830 do not divide the day into twenty-four equal hours. They divide the daylight into six hours, and the night into six hours, and they count them in an odd way, downward: six at dawn, then five, then four, then nine at midday, then eight, seven, and six again at dusk. The night goes the same way: six at dusk, five, four, nine at midnight, eight, seven, and six at dawn. Nobody hears one, two or three strokes for an hour. The numbers come from an old way of counting in multiples of nine, borrowed long ago from China, and only the last figure of each was rung.

So the bell you are hearing now, after its three warning strokes, rings six times. It is the sixth hour of evening, the hour of dusk. The last shops close. Lamps are lit. Workmen go home. Evening has officially begun.

And here is the surprising thing about those hours. Because day and night are each split into six, the hours change length with the seasons. In summer, the daytime hours are long and the night hours short. In winter it is the other way around. On this January night, with dusk coming early and dawn coming late, each hour of the night runs a little over two of our hours. Each daytime hour is shorter, well under two. A night hour in January is a long, slow thing.

To make the system work, Japanese clockmakers had to invent a remarkable kind of clock. The few mechanical clocks in Japan, found in great houses and temples, had to be adjusted through the year, with movable hour markers on their faces, or with weights shifted along a bar every couple of weeks, so that the hours would stretch and shrink with the seasons. Ordinary families had no such thing. They had the bell, and the sky, and the habits of a lifetime.

The bell did not ring for free. Somebody had to climb the tower and swing the log, every hour of every day and every night, in rain and in snow. The bell keeper at Hon-ishichō was a hereditary job, passed down in one family, and the households within hearing of the bell paid him a small fee, a few copper coins a month, for the service of the hour. So when you hear the bell from the Minoya's back room, you are hearing something the Minoya has paid for, like water or oil. It is one of the oldest public services in the city.

And the bell keeper did not guess. He kept time with a slow-burning stick of incense, marked along its length, or with a water clock, a vessel that dripped at a steady pace, and he checked them against the sky at dawn and dusk. If you want to understand how an Edo night feels, imagine that every hour of it is being measured out somewhere, very patiently, by one man and a burning stick.

The bell is also a reminder of something else. A time bell in a crowded city is a fire bell's quieter cousin. Edo, by 1830, has lived for two centuries with the memory of fire, and its people speak of it with a dark, wry humor. There is a saying in the city, very old by now, that fires and quarrels are the flowers of Edo. They bloom often, and they bloom bright.

The greatest of those flowers is long in the past tonight, nearly two lifetimes back. In the first month of 1657, in the era called Meireki, after a long, dry winter, fires broke out on three days running and joined together under a strong wind. When they were finally out, somewhere between sixty and seventy parts in every hundred of the city had burned. The great central tower of the shogun's own castle was gone. It had been the tallest such tower in Japan, and it was never built again. The number of the dead is not known exactly. Estimates run from thirty thousand to as many as one hundred thousand people.

That was nearly two hundred years before tonight. But Edo was rebuilt in the shape the fire had taught it. Firebreaks were cleared. Some temples and great houses were moved farther out. Wider roads were laid. And the city never quite stopped listening for the bell.

One of the first Europeans to see Edo for himself was a German doctor named Engelbert Kaempfer. He worked for the Dutch East India Company at their little trading post on the island of Dejima in Nagasaki harbor from 1690 to 1692, and twice he traveled east with the Dutch delegation all the way to Edo, to pay respects to the shogun. Afterward he wrote a long account of Japan, which was published in English in London in 1727, and for more than a century it was what most Europeans knew of the country.

He found Edo enormous, a sea of low wooden roofs stretching away from the castle, crowded with people. And during his very first stay in the city, in 1691, he lived through both a fire and an earthquake. He set them down in his account as part of the ordinary life of the place, not as a strange disaster. A wooden city, he understood, lived with both. The houses were built light because the ground moved. And because they were light and wooden, they burned.

Kaempfer was a tireless observer. He was a doctor and a naturalist, curious about everything, and he took notes on plants and roads and prices and temples and the manners of innkeepers, often in secret, because foreigners were not supposed to gather so much knowledge of the country. Much of what Europe later believed about Japan came from those notes. And among the things he set down was this ordinary fact of the wooden city: that it burned, and shook, and rebuilt itself, and went on.

Now, in the back room of the Minoya, it is getting dark enough that you can no longer see the pattern of the quilt. O-Sei sets down her sewing and reaches for a lamp.

It is called an *andon*. It is a small wooden frame, about as tall as your forearm, standing on the floor, with paper pasted around all four sides. Inside, on a little shelf, sits a shallow dish of oil, and in the oil lies a wick made from the soft white pith of a rush. O-Sei takes a thin cedar splint, touches it to a coal at the edge of the kotatsu pit, and blows on it gently until it catches. Then she lifts the paper door of the lamp and lights the wick.

A small flame stands up, the size of a fingernail. She closes the paper door. And the lamp begins to glow, not brightly, but softly, the whole paper box lit from inside like a lantern made of moonlight. The light it gives is weak. You could just about read by it if you held the page close. But it turns the back room warm and yellow, and the shadows of the family loom large and gentle on the fusuma screens.

The oil in the dish is rapeseed oil, pressed from the seeds of the same plant whose yellow flowers fill the fields in spring. It burns clean, but it is not cheap. Poorer families in the back alleys burn fish oil, pressed from sardines, which is cheaper but smokes, and smells, unmistakably, of fish. In the Minoya, they can afford rapeseed oil, but O-Sei still trims the wick short. Light is a thing you spend.

There are candles in the house too, but they stay in their box. A Japanese candle is a fine thing, thick and tapering, its wick a hollow tube of paper and rush, its wax pressed from the berries of a kind of sumac tree. It burns with a tall, bright, wavering flame, and it costs a great deal. Candles are for guests, for the household shrine on festival nights, and for walking out in a paper lantern. A family evening at home runs on oil.

You sit with your legs under the quilt and watch the little flame through the paper. The bell is still humming in the air. Then a second, more distant bell begins, from another quarter of the city, a little behind the first, and then a third, very faint, from far away, so that for a while the evening is full of overlapping bronze voices, all saying the same thing, each in its own time.

You feel the cold on the back of your neck. You pull your padded jacket closer. Under the quilt, your knees are warm.

Today, you know the time to the minute, any moment you like, from the screen in your pocket, and light comes on at the touch of a switch, filling a whole room at once. In Edo, time came over the rooftops, slowly, from a bell you could not see, and light came one small flame at a time.

Historians still argue about how the great Meireki fire began. A famous story, told for generations, says it started at a temple called Honmyō-ji, where priests were burning a kimono that was said to be cursed, and a gust of wind carried the burning cloth into the roof. That is why it is sometimes called the Long-Sleeves Fire. Other historians think the cursed kimono is a legend grown up afterward, and some suspect that the temple quietly accepted blame for a fire that really began at a powerful neighbor's mansion nearby. The truth may never be fully known.

Out in the shop, Shinkichi lights a third lamp, a tall one on a stand, and sets it behind the counter, in case anyone knocks at the little door. Its light falls on the stacks of paper and turns them the color of cream. Chōkichi kneels beside it with a small pair of scissors and trims the wick, very carefully, because a wick cut too long smokes and blackens the paper of the lamp, and a blackened lamp means a scolding. He has learned this the way apprentices learn everything, once, and well.

Beside the lamp, on a hook, hangs a folding paper lantern, a cylinder of oiled paper on a frame of split bamboo rings that collapses flat like a paper fan pressed shut. On its side is painted the shop's name. If anyone in the house has to go out after dark tonight, they will take it, lit with a short candle, and the whole street will know by its writing whose lantern is passing.

O-Tami comes in from the kitchen with a second, smaller lamp in her hand, a little dish of oil on a stand, and sets it on the shelf by the door. Then she kneels for a moment at the edge of the kotatsu and slides her hands under the quilt, and closes her eyes, just as Chōkichi did.

"Supper soon," she says. "The radish needs a little longer."

Kihei comes in after her, ducking under the low lintel. He has been out to the back, to look at the storehouse door and the well, as he does every evening. His sleeves are cold. He sits, and counts the strokes of the farthest bell under his breath.

"Six," he says.

"It is always six at this time," says O-Fumi.

"It is always six at this time," Kihei agrees gravely. "But it is good to make sure."

O-Fumi thinks about this. "Who rings it?"

"An old man in a tower," says her grandmother. "And his father before him, and his grandfather before that. All night long."

"Does he not get cold?"

"Very cold," says O-Sei. "That is why we listen properly, when he rings. It would be rude not to."

O-Fumi accepts this, and settles, and the next time the far bell hums she holds very still, listening properly, out of courtesy to the old man in the tower.

O-Sei smiles at that, the small, private smile of a mother whose son has turned out like his father. She remembers her husband saying the very same thing, in another house, on this very ground, before the fire of last spring. She does not say so. She lifts the edge of the quilt, and looks under, and with the iron chopsticks she pulls a little more ash over one corner of the coals, so that they will not burn too fast before bedtime.

The little flame stands in its paper lamp, and under the quilt the charcoal answers it with its own deep orange glow.

## Part Four — Around Six in the Evening: Supper Beside the Kotatsu

The radish has softened, the soup is ready, and the warm smell of miso has crept down the earthen passage and into every room. In the back room, O-Tami sets down the first of the little supper trays, and the evening's real business begins.

Here is a small question for this part. What does a merchant family in Edo eat for supper on a winter night?

Begin with the trays. Nobody in this house eats at a big shared table. Each person has a small lacquered box, about the size of a shoebox, called a *hakozen*. Inside the box live that person's own bowls, chopsticks and a little plate, all their own. At mealtimes you take out your bowls, turn the lid of the box upside down, set it on top, and the lid becomes a tray. After the meal you pour a little hot tea into your bowls, swirl it round, drink it, wipe the bowls with a cloth, and put them back in the box until next time. The bowls are washed properly only now and then.

Tonight, because it is cold, O-Tami sets the trays on the floor all around the kotatsu, so that everyone can keep their legs under the quilt while they eat. That is not quite proper, and O-Sei pretends not to notice.

On each tray is a bowl of rice. But it is not hot rice fresh from the pot. In Edo, families cook their rice once a day, in the morning, in a big iron pot over the kitchen stove, when the fire is already going. The rice is eaten hot at breakfast, kept in a wooden tub through the day, and eaten cool at midday and in the evening. This was such a settled habit that a man named Kitagawa Morisada, who a few years after our night began writing down the differences between Edo and the old cities of Kyoto and Osaka, noted it as one of them. In Edo, rice is cooked in the morning. In Kyoto and Osaka, he noted, it is cooked at midday.

So on a winter night, the cool rice is warmed by a trick. You pour hot green tea over it, straight from the kettle, and eat it as a kind of soup, with a pickle or two on top. It is called *chazuke*, rice in tea, and it is one of the most comforting things in Japan.

Beside the rice is the soup, made from fermented soybean paste, *miso*, with thin slices of fried bean curd floating in it and a scatter of chopped green onion. There is the radish, simmered in a broth of dried fish flakes and kelp until it is soft right through, with a dab of sweet miso on top. There is a little plate of dried sardines, crisp from the grill, each one no longer than your finger. And there is a dish of pickles, yellow rounds of radish pickled in rice bran, salty and crunchy and faintly sour.

Every one of these things came into the city from somewhere close by. The radish is a long white Nerima radish, from the farming villages a few hours' walk to the west of Edo, where the soil is light and deep and the radishes grow as long as a man's forearm. Farmers bring them into the city in carts and on their backs, and on the way home, the same carts carry something else back out to the fields. They carry the city's night soil, bought from landlords and households, to spread on next year's radishes. Edo feeds its farms, and its farms feed Edo.

The little sardines have a stranger story. They come from the long sandy beaches east of the city, across the bay, where fishermen haul in sardines by the hundred thousand with great nets. Only a small part of the catch is eaten. Most of it is spread on the sand to dry and then packed into straw bales and shipped west, to be sold as fertilizer for the cotton fields. So the same small silver fish on O-Fumi's plate tonight helped to grow the cotton in her own padded jacket.

No meat, of course. The family is Buddhist, and the eating of four-legged animals is frowned on, though in the back streets of Edo there are certain shops that sell wild boar and venison in winter, under polite names, as medicine for the cold. Fish, beans, rice, radish, seaweed and pickles. That is supper. It is not a feast. But it is warm, and salty, and there is enough.

So the answer to our small question is a simple one. Cold rice made hot with tea, a bowl of miso soup, a simmered radish, a few small fish and a pickle.

Now, while everyone eats, it is time to open the first of our three questions. Why would anyone build a house with paper walls, in a city where snow falls?

The oldest answer comes from a Buddhist monk named Yoshida Kenkō, who lived in Kyoto about five hundred years before our night. Around the year 1330 he wrote a little book of thoughts called *Essays in Idleness*, and in it, among reflections on the moon, and on old age, and on the right way to drink wine, he wrote a few lines about houses. In one English translation, they begin, "A house should be built with the summer in mind. In winter, one can live anywhere."

That is the heart of it. Summer in Japan is the hard season. It is long, and hot, and so humid that clothes do not dry and mold creeps over everything left in a dark corner. Wood that cannot breathe will rot. Mats that cannot breathe will spoil. People who cannot feel a breeze cannot sleep. So the Japanese house was designed, above all, to let air move. Its floors were raised off the damp ground. Its walls could slide away entirely. In August you could open a house from front to back and let the wind from the river pass straight through it.

Winter, by comparison, was short and bearable. Edo is no Siberia. On an ordinary January day the air is a few degrees above freezing, and at night it often drops below. There is frost, and some snow most winters, but rarely the bitter, killing cold of the northern countries. Kenkō's point was that a person can endure winter in almost any house, by wearing more clothes and staying near a fire. But a badly made house in summer is simply unbearable.

Look up, if the light allows, at the deep eaves that overhang the house. In summer, when the sun climbs high, those eaves cast a deep shade over the screens and keep the hot light off the floor. In winter, when the sun stays low in the south, its light slides in under the same eaves and falls straight onto the paper. And then the paper does its winter work. It cannot hold in much heat, but it turns the thin winter sun into a broad, soft glow that fills the room. On a bright January morning, a shōji screen facing south can make a cold room feel like the inside of a lantern. A house built for summer has its winter gifts, too.

And there were other reasons too, which the people of Edo knew in their bones. The ground moves. A heavy house of stone and brick can crack and fall in an earthquake, while a light frame of posts and beams, joined with wooden pegs and covered with paper and thin boards, sways and settles back. And a house that will probably burn, sooner or later, should be cheap and quick to rebuild. Paper and timber were cheap. And every house was built to the same measure, the measure of the tatami mat, so that screens, doors and mats came in standard sizes. You could buy them ready made, the way you buy a door or a window today. After the fire of last spring, Kihei rebuilt the Minoya in months, not years.

So the paper wall was not a mistake, and it was not poverty. It was a choice. A house built for summer, for earthquakes and for fires, in a city where winter could be met with a quilt and a little pot of coals.

You sit with your tray on the floor and your legs under the quilt, and you lift the bowl of rice in both hands. Kihei, across from you, pours hot tea from the kettle over his own rice, and then over yours, and the steam rises into your face, carrying the grassy smell of green tea and the sweetness of the grain.

You eat in the Edo way, raising the bowl close to your mouth and pushing the rice in with your chopsticks, slurping the hot tea a little, because slurping is not rude here. It is how you cool a mouthful that is too hot. The warmth goes straight down into your chest. You had not realized how cold your middle was until now.

The radish is so soft you can cut it with the edge of your chopsticks. It tastes of the sea, from the kelp and the dried fish flakes, and of the earth, from the miso. The little sardines crunch, bitter and salty. The pickle snaps between your teeth. Around you, the family eats quietly. Talk at meals is not forbidden, but it is not encouraged, either. There is the clink of bowls, the soft sounds of eating, the hum of the lamp, and somewhere outside, very faint, the wind pushing at the rain doors.

The lamp makes a small gold island in the dark room. Your legs are warm. Your hands, wrapped around the bowl, are warm. Your back is cold, and you do not mind.

Today, most of us eat supper at a table, in a heated room, with the food kept cold in a refrigerator until it is cooked. In Edo, the kitchen fire was lit twice a day at most, the rice was cooked once, and a winter meal was made warm at the last moment, by tea from a kettle that had been sitting on the coals all evening.

Historians do not all accept Kenkō's line as the whole explanation. Some argue that it is a beautiful piece of writing but a poor piece of history, and that Japanese houses were built the way they were mostly because of what was cheap and plentiful, which was wood, bamboo, straw, clay and paper, and because of earthquakes and fire. Others answer that the summer climate really did shape the house, and that you can see it in the raised floors, the deep eaves and the sliding walls, all of which make sense in August and very little sense in January.

At the edge of the earthen floor, Chōkichi eats from his own tray, sitting a little apart. In many shops the apprentices eat after the family, in the kitchen, in a hurry. O-Tami lets him eat at the same time, as long as he sits at the edge, which is a small kindness and a common one, and he knows it.

He eats very fast, the way boys who have been hungry all day eat, and then stops himself, and eats a little slower, glancing at Kihei. Kihei, who was an apprentice himself once, in another shop, does not look up.

O-Fumi has been given the crispest sardine, the one with the tail curled up, and she is eating it in tiny bites from the head down. Shinkichi is trying to eat his radish and work out a sum in his head at the same time, and his lips are moving.

"What is it?" asks his father.

"The Yamashiroya account," says Shinkichi. "They have not paid since the ninth month."

"They will pay at the end of the year," says Kihei. "Everyone pays at the end of the year."

"Or they run away," says O-Tami.

"Or they run away," Kihei agrees, and for a moment the two of them look at each other over the lamp, the long look of two people who have kept a shop through good years and bad.

O-Fumi, who has no interest in accounts, wants to know when the rice-cake men are coming. In a few days, at the very end of the year, teams of men will walk the streets of Edo carrying a great wooden mortar, heavy mallets and a portable stove for steaming rice. Families who have no mortar of their own hire them to come to the door, and there in the street, in clouds of steam, the men pound hot sticky rice into the soft white cakes called *mochi*, shouting in rhythm as they swing, while a woman of the house turns the rice between the blows with wet hands. Every child in the ward comes out to watch.

"Five days," says her mother. "Perhaps six."

O-Fumi counts on her fingers under the quilt, and seems satisfied.

O-Sei says nothing. She finishes her tea, wipes her bowl carefully, and puts it back in its little box. Then she lifts the edge of the kotatsu quilt, peers in at the coals, and sets one fresh stick of charcoal at the edge of the glowing bed, to catch slowly, from the side, for the long night ahead.

## Part Five — Around Seven in the Evening: To the Bathhouse

The trays are back in their boxes, the kettle has been filled again, and the bell of the fifth hour has not yet rung. O-Tami takes down a small wooden bucket and a cotton towel, and looks at her husband, and at the children. In winter, after supper, there is one place every family in the ward is going.

Here is a small question for this part. Why does almost nobody in Edo have a bath at home?

The answer is fire, and fuel, and water. A bath of hot water deep enough to sit in needs a great deal of firewood to heat. Firewood in Edo is expensive, brought down the rivers and across the bay from distant forests. A bath needs a great deal of water too, and in this part of the city, water comes from shared wells and from wooden pipes fed by the city's aqueducts, carried home bucket by bucket. And a wood fire burning hot every evening under a tub, inside a wooden house, packed in among other wooden houses, is exactly what the city fears. A few rich families have their own baths. Almost everyone else goes out.

So the answer to our small question is that a bath at home costs too much wood, too much water and too much risk, while the bathhouse at the end of the street costs a few copper coins.

The water itself is something the people of Edo are proud of. Since the 1650s, the city has drawn its drinking water from rivers far to the west, through open channels and then through a hidden network of wooden pipes and troughs laid under the streets. In the ward, the pipes rise into square wooden wells, where you lower a bucket and draw up water that has traveled many miles underground. A true son of Edo likes to boast that he was washed on his first day of life in water from the shogun's own aqueduct. He means it as the highest kind of city pride, the way someone today might boast of being born within the sound of a famous bell.

But carrying enough of that water home, bucket by bucket, to fill a deep wooden tub every night, and then burning enough wood to heat it, is another matter entirely. It is far easier to share.

The public bathhouse is called a *sentō*. There is one in nearly every neighborhood of Edo, and some neighborhoods have several. You can recognize it by the bow and arrow that sometimes hangs over the door, a little joke. To shoot an arrow is *yumi iru* in Japanese, and that sounds almost exactly like *yu ni iru*, to go into the hot water.

Inside the curtained doorway, you step up onto a raised wooden floor and leave your sandals. A clerk sits on a high platform, a kind of tall wooden desk, where he can see everything, collect the coins, and keep an eye on the clothes. You undress in an open room, folding your clothes into a basket or onto a shelf. Then you walk across to a wide floor of wooden boards, sloping slightly so the water runs away, where people kneel and wash themselves with small buckets of hot water and little bags of rice bran used as soap. You wash first, always, completely, before you ever go near the bath itself. The bath is for soaking, not for washing.

And the bath itself is hidden. At the back of the washing floor there is a low wall, painted and decorated, often with a picture of a mountain or the sea, and in it, a low opening, so low that you must bend nearly double to pass through. Behind it, in a small, hot, dim room full of steam, is the tub. The low opening keeps the steam and the heat in. It has a lovely name, too. It is called the *zakuro-guchi*, the pomegranate entrance. Some say that is because old metal mirrors were polished with pomegranate juice, and the words for polishing a mirror and for bending to go in sounded alike.

There is more to the bathhouse than the bath. In many Edo bathhouses, a steep wooden staircase leads up from the men's side to a room on the upper floor, where, for a few more coins, men can sit after their soak, wrapped in a cotton robe, drinking tea, nibbling sweets, playing board games and arguing about the news. It is a kind of club, the nearest thing the ward has to a coffee house. Kihei likes to go up there now and then, to hear what the other shopkeepers are saying about prices.

Now for the surprising part. Not so very long before our night, men and women in many Edo bathhouses bathed together, in the same steamy room. In 1791, the shogun's government, in one of its periodic fits of tightening public morals, ordered that men and women must bathe separately. Many bathhouses obeyed by building a partition down the middle, or by setting aside different days or hours for men and for women. Others obeyed less carefully. By 1830, the Minoya's neighborhood bathhouse has two sides, a men's side and a women's side, divided by a wooden wall with a gap at the top, so that the steam, and the gossip, can drift freely between them.

And the gossip of the bathhouse is so famous that it became a book. Between 1809 and 1813, a writer named Shikitei Sanba published a comic book called *Ukiyoburo*, which means something like *The Bathhouse of the Floating World*. It is almost entirely made of conversations overheard in an Edo bathhouse, through a single day: old men complaining about their knees, young men boasting, mothers scolding children, servants trading news about their masters, drunks singing, and everyone talking at once.

In his opening pages, Sanba plays at being solemn about it. The bathhouse, he says, teaches a lesson, because in the bath there is no rank. The samurai and the shopkeeper, the rich man and the porter, all strip off the clothes that show who they are, and sit in the same hot water as they were born. In one modern rendering of his preface, he puts it with a straight face: "Public baths are the shortest route there is to moral and spiritual enlightenment."

You step out into the street with the family, a paper lantern swinging from Kihei's hand. The cold hits your face at once, dry and sharp, smelling of charcoal smoke from a hundred kotatsu pits. The street is very dark. Here and there a little light leaks out around the edges of a shop's front boards. Somewhere a dog is gnawing something. Your sandals crunch on frozen grit.

The bathhouse is at the corner, and you can see it before you reach it, because steam is pouring out of its roof vents into the dark like a small cloud. You duck under its curtain into a wall of warm, wet air that smells of wood smoke and hot cedar and rice bran. After the black street, the lamps inside seem very bright. The clerk on his high seat takes your coins. You undress, folding your padded clothes, and shiver for a moment, naked in the open room.

Around you the room is full of voices. An old man is telling a young one that the winters were colder when he was a boy. A father is scrubbing his small son's back while the boy wriggles. Two porters are laughing about something that happened at the fish market this morning. From over the partition, from the women's side, comes the high bright sound of a baby being bathed, and a woman's voice telling it to hush. Nobody here is a stranger for long.

Then you kneel on the wet boards, and pour a bucket of hot water over your shoulders, and the shiver turns into a long sigh. You scrub with the little bag of bran until your skin is pink. Then you bend low and go through the pomegranate entrance, into the steam.

It is almost dark in there, and very hot. Shapes of heads float in the haze. Someone is humming. You find the edge of the tub with your hand and lower yourself into the water, slowly, because it is hotter than you would ever run a bath at home, hot enough to make your skin prickle and your breath catch. You sink to your chin. And then something happens that the people of Edo understood perfectly. The heat goes all the way in. Not just into your skin. Into your muscles, your joints, your bones. In a few minutes, every bit of cold you have gathered since the afternoon is gone.

Not so long ago, on the shortest day of the year, which fell about three weeks before tonight, the bathhouses of Edo floated whole yellow citrons in their tubs, the knobbly, fragrant fruit called *yuzu*. It was said that a citron bath on the winter solstice kept you from catching cold all winter. The fruit are gone from the water now. But if you breathe in deeply, you might imagine there is still a trace of their sharp, sweet smell, caught in the old cedar of the tub.

You will carry this heat home. That is the point of it.

Today, most of us bathe or shower at home, alone, whenever we like, with hot water that comes out of a pipe. In Edo, a winter bath was a nightly outing, a crowd, a conversation, and a store of warmth you carried back through the cold streets like a coal wrapped in cloth.

Historians are not of one mind about the bathhouses. Some argue that the government's orders about separate bathing were mostly ignored, and that mixed bathing remained common in Edo until much later, which is what many foreign visitors in the 1850s and 1860s reported with astonishment. Others point to bathhouse plans and pictures showing divided rooms, and argue that by the early nineteenth century separate bathing was usual in the city center, even if it was not universal.

On the women's side, O-Tami kneels behind her daughter on the wet boards and scrubs her back with the bag of bran, while O-Fumi chatters about the girl with the new comb, who is, it turns out, sitting three places away. O-Tami does not wash her own hair tonight. A married woman's hair in Edo is a careful sculpture, oiled and combed and tied up over pads and pins, and it is washed only now and then, perhaps once or twice a month, on a warm afternoon, with plenty of time to dry. In January, few women would wash their hair at night and then walk home through the cold. So O-Tami keeps her head well above the water in the tub, and talks with the neighbor beside her about the price of charcoal, which has gone up, and the price of rice, which has gone up, and the neighbor's mother-in-law, who has not changed at all.

You come out again into the cold, and for the first few minutes you do not feel it at all. You are steaming. Little wisps rise from O-Fumi's hair under the lantern light. Shinkichi's face is pink. Kihei, who soaked longest, walks slowly, contentedly, swinging the lantern.

At the corner there is a small stall with a clay oven, and a smell drifting from it that stops O-Fumi in her tracks. Sweet potatoes, roasting. A paper sign hangs over the stall, and Shinkichi reads it out for his sister, grinning. It says something like, "Better than chestnuts. Thirteen *ri*."

"What does that mean?" O-Fumi asks.

So Shinkichi explains, proudly, because he has only just understood it himself. The word for chestnut, *kuri*, sounds like *ku-ri*, nine *ri*, and a *ri* is a measure of distance, about two and a half miles. Roasted chestnuts are a great winter treat. Roasted sweet potatoes taste a little like chestnuts. And the best sweet potatoes come from a town called Kawagoe, which is said to be about thirteen *ri* from Edo. So, nine *ri* is good, but thirteen *ri* is better. Better than chestnuts, thirteen *ri*.

O-Fumi thinks about this for a while, and then says that she would like a sweet potato. Kihei, warm and indulgent from the bath, buys two, wrapped in paper. One for her, and one, he says, for her grandmother, who stayed home to keep the fire.

Kihei carries the wet towels and the little bucket wrapped in a square of cloth, knotted at the corners. Such cloths are everywhere in Edo, used for carrying everything from books to lunch boxes, and they owe their name to the bathhouse. People once spread them on the floor to stand on while they undressed, and bundled their clothes in them, and the word for them still carries the word for bath inside it.

They walk back down the dark street, steaming gently, the hot potatoes held against their chests inside their jackets. At home, O-Sei has kept everything just as it was. The lamp glows in its paper box. The kettle ticks. Chōkichi slips out with a coin to take his own turn at the bath, and the family slides back under the kotatsu quilt, warm through and through, around the little square of coals that waited for them.

## Part Six — Around Eight in the Evening: Ledgers by Lamplight

The fifth hour has rung, with its three slow warning strokes and its five strokes after, and the evening has settled into its working rhythm. Chōkichi is back from the bath, pink and steaming. The sweet potato has been shared and eaten. And O-Tami has brought out the books.

Here is a small question for this part. What does a careful merchant family in Edo do every single night, just in case the street burns?

Start with the books themselves, because they are the most precious things in the house. A shop like the Minoya runs on credit. Customers take paper now and pay later, and the debts are written down in long, thick account books, bound with cord, their covers marked with big bold characters wishing the shop good fortune. The books are written in black ink, made of soot and glue, on strong Japanese paper. If the books are lost, the debts are lost. The money owed to the shop simply disappears.

So there is a story that Edo merchants told one another, and that Kihei believes with all his heart. When fire comes, you throw the account books down the well. Good washi, made from long mulberry fibers, does not fall apart in water the way cheap paper does. And good black ink, once it has dried on such paper, does not run. After the fire, so the story goes, you fish the books up out of the well, dry them page by page in the sun, and go on collecting what you are owed. Whether every merchant really did this, nobody can say. But Kihei keeps his books in a box near the back door, a few steps from the well, and every night they go back into that box.

Then there is the storehouse, the *kura*. Behind the Minoya, across the scrap of garden, stands a small building that looks quite different from the house. Its walls are thick earth and clay, plastered white, very likely more than a foot thick. Its roof is heavy tile. Its door is a massive thing, plastered too, with edges cut in steps like a staircase, so that when it shuts, the steps lock into each other and leave no straight crack for flame to slip through. Inside, Kihei keeps his best paper stock, his family's good clothes, the lacquer trays for New Year, and anything else he cannot bear to lose.

And beside the storehouse door, under a little wooden roof, sits a tub of wet clay, kept ready. If the alarm bell rings close by, the last thing the family does before they leave is close the storehouse door and slap that clay into every gap around its edges, sealing it shut. A good storehouse, well sealed, could stand in the middle of a burning street and come through with everything inside it safe.

So the answer to our small question is the nightly ritual of a careful shop. The books go into their box by the well. The storehouse door is closed and checked. The tub of clay is wet. Then the family can sleep.

Nor is this only a matter of private prudence. In Edo, the ward watches the ward. Every household belongs to a small group of neighbors, usually five households, who are held responsible together for one another's conduct. If one house in the group breaks the law, the others can be called to account for not stopping it. And a fire that starts through carelessness is treated as a wrong done to the whole street. A household found to have let a fire escape could be ordered by the magistrate to stay shut inside its own house for days, as a punishment and a public shame. So when Kihei checks his coals before bed, he is not only protecting his own house. He is keeping faith with his neighbors.

The surprising part is who would come if the fire bell rang. In 1720, under the eighth shogun, a city magistrate named Ōoka Tadasuke organized the townspeople of Edo into their own fire brigades, paid for and manned by the wards themselves. There were nearly fifty companies in the central city alone, each named after one of the syllables of the Japanese alphabet. Each one had its own banner, called a *matoi*, a tall pole topped with a great carved emblem and hung with long paper or leather strips that whirled when it was spun.

The firemen were mostly carpenters and scaffolders, men used to running along roof beams high above the street. Their method was not water. Hand pumps and buckets could do little against a fire driven by a winter wind. Their method was to pull down houses. They would choose a line ahead of the fire, climb onto a roof, plant the company's banner there, and then tear down the houses below it with hooks and poles and their own hands, to leave the fire nothing to eat. The man holding the banner on the roof was announcing to the whole city that his company would stop the fire right here.

And in every ward, high above the roofs, stood a fire watchtower, a tall ladder or wooden tower with a bronze bell hung at the top. A watchman climbed it in the season of fires and looked out over the dark. The number of strokes, and their speed, told the city how near the danger was.

There is an older book on Kihei's shelf, one that every merchant of his generation knows and laughs at. It was written in 1692 by a writer from Osaka named Ihara Saikaku, and its title means something like *The Way the World Reckons Its Accounts*. Every one of its stories takes place on a single day, the last day of the year, when by custom all the year's debts must be settled. Saikaku describes, with merciless good humor, the shopkeepers rushing about collecting what they are owed, and the debtors hiding from them, pretending to be ill, slipping out the back door, inventing dead relatives, sending their wives to the door to weep. The last day of the year, in his telling, is a long, comic battle between the people who are owed and the people who owe.

That day is coming for the Minoya, too. It is nine days off. That is why O-Tami's abacus is clicking tonight.

You sit with her at the kotatsu, your legs warm under the quilt, and watch. The account book lies open on the low frame of the kotatsu, where the quilt has been folded back a little to make a flat place. Beside it is the abacus, a wooden frame with rows of small beads strung on bamboo rods. O-Tami runs her finger down a column of black characters in the book, and her other hand moves on the beads, flick, flick, click, so fast you cannot follow it.

The lamp is close beside her, and its yellow light falls across the page. You can smell the ink, faintly, a smell like burnt pine. You can feel the warmth of the coals under the quilt rising against your knees. O-Tami's lips move, silently. Then she stops, writes a small figure in the margin with a fine brush, and starts again.

Shinkichi kneels beside her with his own abacus, doing the same column, to see whether he gets the same answer. He does not, the first time. His mother says nothing. She simply waits, and he goes back to the top and starts again, his face red. The second time, he gets it right. She nods, once. That is all the praise he will get, and it is plenty.

The abacus itself came to Japan from China perhaps three centuries before our night, and by 1830 it is in every shop in the land. Children of shopkeeping families learn it at the little temple schools, along with reading and writing, and a good merchant's son can add a long column on the beads faster than you could read it aloud. Shinkichi is not yet a good merchant's son. But he is getting there.

Today, a fire in a city is met by trained crews, with engines and hoses and water under pressure, and by houses built so that a fire in one room cannot easily spread to the next. In Edo, a fire was met by neighbors with hooks and banners, by storehouses sealed with clay, and by account books thrown down a well.

Historians disagree about how well the townsmen's brigades really worked. Some argue that pulling down houses in front of a wind-driven fire was the only sensible method in a wooden city, and that the brigades saved countless streets. Others point out that the companies were fiercely proud and fiercely rival, that they sometimes quarreled over who had the right to fight a fire, and that great fires still swept the city again and again, including the one that burned the Minoya.

Kihei is going over the paper stock list. He looks up from it, and says to nobody in particular, "Mino paper went up again."

"Everything went up again," says O-Tami, flicking her beads.

Kihei's list is a map of the city's needs. Thick, glossy sheets for the woodblock printers, who print the work of artists like the old master Hokusai, now seventy years old and still drawing every day, who within a year or two will begin publishing a famous series of views of Mount Fuji. Soft, plain sheets for the temple school, where O-Fumi and forty other children practice their letters. Mino paper for screens. Heavy paper for umbrellas and lanterns. And a great deal of thin, soft tissue paper, which the people of Edo carry folded in their sleeves and use to blow their noses, and then throw away. When a Japanese embassy passed through the south of France in 1615, the townspeople there were so astonished to see these elegant visitors blow their noses on soft paper and toss it aside that they scrambled to pick the sheets up as souvenirs. In Edo, nobody thinks twice about it. It is simply what paper is for.

O-Sei is watching the two children. O-Fumi is under the quilt up to her chin, sleepy from the bath. Chōkichi is mending a straw sandal, his tongue between his teeth. And O-Sei begins, very quietly, to talk about last spring.

She does not talk about the fire itself. She talks about the morning after. About how the wind had dropped and the sky was very blue. About how the whole family, and the whole ward, had walked together to the open ground by the river and sat there on their bundles, and how a neighbor had shared rice balls, and another had shared tea. About how nobody from this street had been hurt, not one. About how, before the ash was even cold, the carpenters had come, and how Kihei had stood on the empty ground with a stick and drawn the new house in the dirt, exactly where the old one had been.

"And here it is," she says, and pats the tatami beside her, the way you would pat a good horse.

Chōkichi, who was here for it, nods. O-Fumi, who was only seven, does not remember much except the rice balls. Kihei, who drew the house in the dirt, keeps his eyes on his list.

You go out with Kihei to the storehouse. The cold is very still in the little garden, and the stars are hard and bright above the roofline. The lantern throws a small circle of light on the white plaster wall of the kura, which seems to glow faintly on its own. Kihei puts his palm flat on the heavy door, as he did with the paper stacks, as if he were taking its temperature. He lifts the little wooden cover of the clay tub and presses a finger into the clay, to make sure it has not dried. It is wet and cold, and it holds the print of his finger.

Then he stands for a moment, looking up at the sky, at the stars, at the smoke from the neighbors' kotatsu rising straight up now, because the wind has dropped a little. He seems satisfied. He takes you back inside.

Chōkichi has stopped mending his sandal. He is thinking, as he often does at this hour, of home. In his village across the bay, there are no kotatsu pits with little frames and quilts. There is a big square hearth in the floor of the main room, open, with a wood fire burning in it and a pot hanging over it on a hook from the rafters, and the smoke finds its own way out through the thatch. In winter his whole family sits around that hearth in the evening, his grandfather in the place of honor facing the door, his mother at the side nearest the kitchen, the children wherever they can squeeze in, their faces hot and their backs freezing. The smoke stings your eyes. The fire roars and spits. It is nothing like the polite, quiet little coals of Edo.

He does not say any of this. But O-Sei, who has been watching him, says, "In the country, the fires are bigger."

"Yes," says Chōkichi.

"Bigger is not always warmer," says O-Sei, and lifts the quilt a little for him, and he slides his legs a little further in.

That is the last that anyone in the Minoya will say about fire tonight. The books are closed. O-Tami ties their cord and carries them out to their box by the back door, as she does every night. Kihei takes the lantern and goes out to look at the storehouse door and the tub of clay, as he does every night. And O-Sei lifts the quilt and looks in at the coals, bedded in their ash in the little clay-lined pit, glowing steadily, safely, exactly where a fire belongs.

## Part Seven — Around Nine in the Evening: Making the Beds of Cotton

The lamp has burned a little lower in its dish, and O-Fumi's head has begun to nod against her grandmother's arm. O-Sei looks at her, and then at the rest of them, and says one word that every child in Japan knows. It is time to lay out the bedding.

Here is a small question for this part. Where, in this house, are the beds?

You might look around the back room and see none. No bed frame, no mattress, no headboard. There is no bedroom at all, in the sense we would mean it. There are rooms, floored with mats, and any room can become a bedroom in a few minutes, and become a sitting room again in the morning.

The beds live in a cupboard. Along one wall of the back room is a pair of sliding doors, papered like the fusuma, and behind them is a deep closet, divided by a single wooden shelf into an upper and a lower half. It is called the *oshiire*, literally the place you push things into. Folded on the shelves, in soft square stacks, is the family's bedding. Every night it comes out and goes down on the mats. Every morning it is folded and pushed back in, and on fine days it is hung out over a railing or a pole in the sun to air, which is the single best way to keep cotton bedding dry and sweet.

So the answer to our small question is that the beds are in the cupboard, waiting, and the bedroom is wherever you put them.

The bedding has three parts. First, laid directly on the tatami, comes the *shikibuton*, a thick, flat mattress of cotton cloth stuffed with cotton wadding, about as thick as your hand is long. Tatami are firm and springy, and they hold a little warmth of their own, being made of tightly packed straw. The mattress lies on them like a soft second floor.

Then comes the cover. The poorest families have simple cotton quilts. But many households in Edo, and the Minoya among them, have something better, and quite wonderful. It is a quilt made in the shape of an enormous kimono, with a collar and wide sleeves, padded thickly with cotton wadding from top to bottom. You do not wear it, exactly. You lie down, and it is laid over you, and you can slide your arms into its sleeves if you like, and its thick collar comes up around your neck and shoulders, where cold air would otherwise creep in. It is bigger than you are, so it covers your feet and tucks in around your sides. It is like being tucked into a giant, soft, padded robe.

And then the pillow. For the men and children it is a small, firm cushion, stuffed with buckwheat husks that rustle when you move your head. For the women it is something stranger. It is a small wooden box, about as high as your hand is wide, with a little cushion tied on top, and you rest just the nape of your neck on it, so that your elaborate hair, oiled and pinned, does not touch the floor and is not crushed. O-Sei, at sixty-eight, has used one every night of her grown life, and says she cannot sleep any other way.

Now here is something surprising, especially in a paper merchant's house. Not everyone in Japan sleeps under cotton. Cotton has only become common for ordinary people in the last two hundred years or so. Before that, and still among the poor and in the mountain villages, people slept under quilts of hemp or straw, or under paper. Yes, paper. There was a kind of clothing called *kamiko*, made of sheets of strong washi, rubbed soft, and treated with the bitter juice of unripe persimmons, which made it tough and resistant to water. Paper does not let wind through. A paper robe worn under your other clothes, or a paper quilt laid over you, is surprisingly warm. Monks wore paper robes in winter. The great poet Matsuo Bashō, more than a century before our night, wore one on his long journeys on foot. And Kihei, every winter, sells a few bundles of the special thick paper used to make them.

Edward Morse noticed all this. In his book of 1886, he wrote that a Japanese house has no special room for sleeping, and that the thick quilts are brought out from the closets at night and spread on the mats wherever the family wishes to sleep, and put away again in the morning, so that the same room serves for every purpose of the day. To an American used to bedsteads and bedrooms, it was one of the most striking things about the Japanese house. He found it sensible. A room that is a bedroom only at night is a room you can use all day.

You help O-Tami pull the bedding from the closet. The cotton is cool to the touch, and heavy, and it smells of sunshine, faintly, from its last airing on the railing behind the house, two days ago, when the weather was fine. You lay the first mattress down on the mats, close to the edge of the kotatsu, with its foot end just under the kotatsu quilt.

That is the secret of the arrangement, and you see it now as the beds go down one by one. They do not lie in a row. They lie around the kotatsu, on all four sides, like the spokes of a wheel, each one with its foot end tucked in under the edge of the big kotatsu quilt, toward the warm square pit in the middle. Everyone will sleep with their head out in the cool room, and their feet in the warmth.

O-Sei and O-Fumi share one side, the old woman and the child, as they always do. Kihei and O-Tami take the side across from them. On the third side, Shinkichi lays his mattress. And on the fourth side, tonight, there is a second mattress, a thin one, carried in from the shop. Kihei looked at the sky earlier, and at the frost, and said that the boy should sleep in the back room tonight, with the family. Chōkichi brought his bedding in with the speed of someone afraid the offer will be taken back.

Good cotton bedding is one of the most valuable things a family owns, worth more, sometimes, than everything else in the room put together. The cotton itself is grown far to the west, around Osaka, and shipped east to Edo in great bales. In the back alleys, where the poorer families live in long, thin row houses with one room each, it was a common joke that people pawned their winter quilts every spring, when it grew warm, and bought them back from the pawnshop in autumn, when it grew cold again. The pawnbroker, the joke went, had the best-kept bedding in the ward.

You sit on your own mattress for a moment, before you lie down, and feel the house around you. Every screen is shut. The fusuma to the middle room are closed. The rain doors are bolted outside the paper. The lamp is a small gold glow. The quilts are thick and soft and smell of sun. Beneath you, the mats give a little under your weight, and you can feel, very faintly, the warmth that has soaked into them all evening from the kotatsu pit. You can hear the others settling, the whisper of cotton, the creak of a joint, a long sigh.

Before she lies down, O-Sei kneels for a moment in front of a small black cabinet on a shelf in the corner, the family's Buddhist altar. Its little doors are open, and inside, in the dim light, are a small gilt figure, a few wooden tablets bearing the names of the family's dead, among them her husband's, and a tiny bowl of rice set out this morning. She lights a single stick of incense from the lamp, sets it upright in a little bowl of ash, and strikes a small bronze bowl with a wooden stick. It rings, clear and thin, a sound like a drop of water. She presses her palms together and bows her head, and her lips move. Then she closes the little doors, very gently, for the night. The thread of incense smoke rises straight up in the still air, and the room smells, for a while, of sandalwood.

So now we come to the second of our questions, and you can see the answer lying on the floor in front of you. How do six people sleep warm, all night long, around one small pot of charcoal?

The first part of the answer is that the charcoal was never meant to warm the room. It warms the people, at their coldest point, which is their feet. Warm feet send warmth back up through the whole body, and a person whose feet are warm can sleep in a room cold enough to see their breath. Six pairs of feet, all pointing in toward one small square of coals, all under one heavy quilt that joins with their own quilts, share one warm center between them.

The second part is how the charcoal is managed. O-Sei will not leave a bright fire in the pit. Before bed, she buries the coals in ash, leaving only a little of their glow exposed. Ash is a blanket for fire. It slows the burning almost to a standstill, so that a few pieces of charcoal give off a low, steady heat for hours instead of a fierce heat for one. Edward Morse saw the same trick with the hibachi, and he wrote it down exactly. "A single stick of coal buried vertically in the ashes is burned for several hours." One small pot, carefully banked, can last until the small hours of the morning, and then it can be woken again with a breath.

The third part is everything else the family has already done tonight. They shut the rain doors, so the wind cannot reach the screens. They closed the fusuma between the rooms, so that the six-mat back room is a small, still box of air. They went to the bath, and soaked until the heat reached their bones, and they will carry that heat into bed. They will sleep in padded cotton gowns, with cotton socks on their feet. They will lie under quilts shaped like robes, with collars to keep out the drafts. And six people breathing in one small closed room add their own gentle warmth to it all night long.

And there is one more trick, for anyone whose feet are still cold. O-Sei has set two smooth, flat stones on the edge of the kotatsu pit all evening, warming slowly. Now she wraps each one in an old cloth and slips one into O-Fumi's bedding and one into her own. They will stay warm for a long while. Warmed stones like these have been used in Japan for centuries, in bedding and inside clothing. People still remembered that monks in their cold temples had once held them against their stomachs through the long winter nights.

So the answer to our second question is not one trick, but many small ones, laid on top of each other like quilts. Warm feet, banked coals, still air, a hot bath, padded robes, warm stones and each other.

Historians who study daily life in Edo do not entirely agree about the kotatsu at night. Some point out that advice books and doctors of the period warned against sleeping all night with your legs in the kotatsu, because the heat could burn the skin, dry the body, and give you a headache from the charcoal fumes, and because a kicked quilt could catch on the coals. Others note that pictures, stories and verses of the time show families doing exactly that, again and again, on the coldest nights. Probably both are true. People were warned, and on the coldest nights they did it anyway, carefully.

And this family is careful. Before she lies down, O-Sei slides the shōji screen that leads toward the kitchen open by just the width of two fingers. A little fresh air will come in through that gap all night, enough to keep the charcoal's breath from gathering in the room, not enough to make a draft you would feel.

Then the lamp. O-Tami trims the wick very low, so that the flame is a tiny bead of light inside its paper box, giving off a glow no brighter than a glowworm. It will burn like that, low and patient, for a good part of the night, so that anyone who wakes can see their way.

Chōkichi is first into bed, because he is twelve and has been on his feet since before dawn. He slides under his quilt, pushes his feet in under the kotatsu quilt, finds the warmth, and lets out the same small involuntary sound of happiness he made earlier when he warmed his hands. Shinkichi pretends not to hear it. Then Shinkichi slides his own feet in, and makes the same sound, a little more quietly.

O-Fumi lies against her grandmother, the warm stone at her feet, her small wooden-handled brush placed carefully on the mat beside her pillow, where she can reach it in the morning. O-Sei settles her head on her little wooden pillow, very straight, her hands folded on the collar of her robe-shaped quilt.

Kihei and O-Tami are the last to lie down. They lie side by side, feet to the warmth. For a little while O-Tami's fingers still move on the quilt, flick, click, flick, doing sums in the air without an abacus. Then they stop.

In the middle of them all, under the heavy quilt, in the little clay-lined pit, the charcoal sits buried in its ash, glowing faintly, warming six pairs of feet.

## Part Eight — Around Ten at Night: The Gates of the Ward Swing Shut

The bell comes again over the rooftops, three warning strokes and then four, slow and round, each one hanging in the cold air before the next. It is the fourth hour of the night. In the back room of the Minoya, everyone is lying down, and the lamp is a bead of gold in its paper box. Out in the street, the ward is closing its gates.

Here is a small question for this part. Who keeps watch over a sleeping street in Edo?

Begin with the gates themselves. Every ward of the commoners' city, every block of streets like this one, has a wooden gate at each end, called a *kido*. In the daytime the gates stand open and nobody notices them. But at the fourth hour of the night, about ten o'clock by our clocks, they are swung shut and barred. After that, anyone who wants to pass from one ward to the next must knock at a small side door and explain themselves.

Beside each gate stands a tiny hut, hardly bigger than a cupboard, and in it lives the gatekeeper. He is usually an older man, paid a small wage by the ward. He opens and closes the gate, he lets through the midwife and the doctor and the man running to fetch them, and he keeps an eye on strangers. To make ends meet, he also runs a little shop out of his hut by day, selling straw sandals, paper, candles, cheap sweets for children and odds and ends. O-Fumi spends many of her small coins at the gatekeeper's hut.

And across the street from the gate, in a slightly larger hut, is the ward's own watch post. It is staffed in turns by the landlords and the men of the ward, and by a hired man or two. It is where you go if there is trouble in the night, if someone is ill, if a quarrel breaks out, if a lost child is found wandering. On its roof, in many wards, stands a short ladder with a small bell hung at the top.

Inside the watch post, hung neatly on the walls, are the things a ward might need in the night. Long poles with iron hooks at the end. Wooden buckets with the ward's mark painted on them. A ladder. A paper lantern or two. A brazier where the men on duty can warm their hands, and a kettle for tea. It is a small, cramped, smoky room, and the men who sit there on winter nights pass the hours talking, dozing and listening.

The most important of those men is the landlord. In Edo, most commoners do not own the ground they live on. The owners of the land, who are often rich and live elsewhere, hire a manager for each property, a kind of resident landlord, who collects the rents, settles quarrels, keeps the register of who lives where, and answers for his tenants to the authorities. He witnesses their marriages, helps arrange their funerals, scolds their sons and finds work for their daughters. There was a saying in the city that a landlord is as good as a parent, and a tenant is as good as a child. It was half a joke and half the truth.

So the answer to our small question is that the street watches itself. The gatekeeper at the gate, the men of the ward at the watch post, and, as we will hear very soon, the neighbors themselves, walking.

Here is the surprising part. When the gatekeeper lets a late traveler through the small side door, he does not simply wave him on. He strikes two pieces of hard wood together, a sharp, ringing clack, once or twice. The sound carries down the dark street to the gatekeeper at the far end, and tells him that someone has been let through and is coming his way. When the traveler reaches the far gate, that gatekeeper lets him through, and strikes his own clappers in turn, passing the message on. So on a winter night in Edo, a single late walker can be followed across the city by a little trail of wooden knocks, gate to gate to gate, like a rumor traveling through the dark.

The clappers are called *hyōshigi*. They are two short, square bars of hard, dense wood, often joined by a cord, and when they are struck together they make a sound quite unlike anything else, a hard, bright, dry crack that seems to go straight through paper walls. And in the winter, the season of dry winds, they are carried by someone else as well.

Listen. Far down the street, beyond the gate, something is coming. Two cracks of wood, close together. A pause. Then a man's voice, long and drawn out, calling. Then the two cracks again, a little closer.

It is the fire patrol. In the winter months, the men of each ward take turns walking the streets at night, in pairs or small groups, carrying a lantern and a pair of clappers. Every few steps they strike the clappers and call out, in a long, sing-song voice, a single phrase. It means, take care with fire. Mind your fires. Be careful of your fires tonight. It is not an alarm. It is a reminder, a kind of spoken lullaby with a purpose, sung to houses full of sleeping people and banked coals.

Not everyone loved the sound. Half a century after our night, in 1878, an English traveler named Isabella Bird made a long journey through the north of Japan, sleeping in inns and farmhouses, and she wrote about it in letters that became a famous book, *Unbeaten Tracks in Japan*, published in 1880. She was a hardy, observant, often admiring traveler. But she was a light sleeper. And among the many noises of a Japanese night that kept her awake, she singled out the clapping of the watchmen on their rounds, which she found perfectly maddening. To a foreign guest trying to sleep, it was a torment. To the people inside the houses, who had heard it every winter night since they were babies, it was simply what a safe night sounded like.

Now it is your turn to lie still and listen. You are on your mattress in the back room, your feet tucked under the kotatsu quilt, warm to the ankles, the robe-shaped quilt drawn up to your chin. Your nose is cold. The tip of each ear is cold. The rest of you is warm.

The clappers come nearer. Crack, crack. The voice, long and soft and a little hoarse from the cold: take care with fire. Crack, crack. It is right outside now, just beyond the rain doors, just beyond the paper. You can hear the footsteps of the two men on the frozen street, the creak of their straw sandals, the swing of their lantern. One of them coughs. Then crack, crack, and the voice again, and they are passing, moving on down the row, house after house, the sound growing smaller.

You find you have been holding your breath, a little, the way you do when someone walks past your door at night. You let it out. Somewhere under the quilt, O-Fumi's foot finds yours and stays there, warm.

Now that the clappers have passed, you notice how much else you can hear. The Minoya shares its side walls with the houses on either side. On the left is the tea dealer's shop, and through the plaster and the thin boards you can hear the old tea dealer cough, twice, and his wife murmur something, and then the creak of someone turning over. On the right is the comb maker, whose youngest child is only a few months old, and you hear it fuss for a moment in the dark, and its mother hum to it, a low, wordless tune, until it stops. They are so close. A few inches of wood and plaster away. In a row of houses like this, nobody is ever really alone at night. You lie in the dark and listen to your neighbors sleeping, and they, if they are awake, are listening to you.

Then, from even farther off, so faint you are not sure you heard it, comes a different sound. A tiny, silvery tinkle, like a wind chime. It is the noodle seller. He walks the streets on cold nights with a wooden stand slung from a pole over his shoulder, a little stove at one end and a box of bowls and noodles and broth at the other, and from the pole hangs a small bell, which rings as he walks. Anyone still awake who wants a bowl of hot buckwheat noodles, at this hour, only has to step out and call to him. Nobody in the Minoya calls. But the sound of that little bell, passing in the dark, is somehow one of the coziest sounds in the city. Somebody, out there in the cold, is carrying a hot fire and good food from street to street, in case anyone needs it.

These noodle sellers are often called by a curious name, which means something like "two-eight noodles." Nobody in Edo quite agrees on why. Some say it is the price: two times eight, sixteen copper coins a bowl. Others say it is the recipe: two parts wheat flour to eight parts buckwheat, which holds the noodles together. Either way, a bowl of hot noodles in broth, handed over in the cold by lantern light, is one of the small luxuries of the Edo night.

Historians are not all of one mind about how strict the ward gates really were by 1830. Some see them as part of a carefully watched city, in which every street was closed off at night and every movement known. Others argue that by this late in the Edo period the gates were often left ajar, the gatekeepers were old men selling sweets, and anyone with a lantern and a believable story could pass through, so that the gates were more a habit, and a comfort, than a wall.

Kihei is not asleep yet. He is lying on his back with his eyes open, listening to the clappers fade away down the street, the way he does every winter night. When he was a boy, he walked the fire patrol himself with his father. When Shinkichi turned fourteen, he went out with him, for the first time, on a cold night like this, and taught him the call. In a few nights it will be the Minoya's turn again.

He remembers those nights with his father very clearly. The cold so sharp it made his teeth ache. The weight of the lantern, swinging on its pole. The way his father's breath smoked in its light as he called. The way the houses on either side, dark and shut, seemed to be listening. And the way his father, at the end of the round, stopped outside their own house, and struck the clappers one last time, softly, just for them, before they went in to the warmth.

"Next week," he murmurs.

"I know," says Shinkichi, from his mattress. He is not asleep either. "I will take the clappers."

"You will take the lantern," says Kihei. "I will take the clappers."

There is a silence, and then a small sound that might be Shinkichi laughing into his quilt.

O-Tami is asleep already, her face toward her husband. She went from sums to sleep in the time it takes to put down an abacus, as mothers of busy households do. Chōkichi is asleep too, his mouth a little open, one arm flung out of his quilt. O-Sei's eyes are closed, but nobody who knows her would assume she is sleeping. She is listening.

And now, because she has heard the clappers pass, and because it is the fourth hour, O-Sei sits up, slowly, on her mattress, and reaches across to the kotatsu. She lifts the edge of the heavy quilt and looks in. The coals are buried, only a few small points of orange showing through the gray ash, like stars seen through cloud. She takes the iron chopsticks from their place on the edge of the pit and moves one coal, very slightly, so that it lies closer to the others and will not go out too soon. She pulls a little more ash over another, so that it will not burn too fast. Then she lowers the quilt.

"Good," she says, very quietly, to the coals, or to herself.

Then she lies down again, settles her neck on her little wooden pillow, and folds her hands on her collar.

Kihei watches her do this, as he has watched her do it every winter night of his life. When she is settled, he closes his own eyes.

Outside, very faint now, at the far end of the ward, the clappers crack once more, and the long voice calls its reminder to the last houses on the street. And here, at the center of the Minoya, the charcoal sits quiet under its ash and its quilt, warm and safe, exactly as the voice asked.

## Part Nine — Around Eleven at Night: Snow on the Rain Doors

The clappers have gone, and the noodle seller's little bell has gone, and the long night hour stretches on in the dark. The wind that rattled the shop curtains in the afternoon has died away entirely. And sometime in the stillness, without anyone in the Minoya noticing the moment it began, the snow has started to fall.

Here is a small question for this part. Why does a house grow so quiet when it snows?

You notice it first as an absence. A little while ago there were faint sounds from the city all around, a dog somewhere, a door, a far-off voice, the creak of a cart. Now there is almost nothing. The silence has a soft, thick quality, as if the whole house had been wrapped in cotton.

The answer is the snow itself. Fresh, falling snow is mostly air, caught between countless tiny crystals, and that loose, airy blanket swallows sound the way a heavy quilt does. It settles on the roof tiles, on the wooden rain doors, on the street, on the shutters of every shop, and every surface it covers stops echoing. Noises that would bounce down a hard frozen street simply sink into the white and vanish. So a snowy night is a hushed night, everywhere in the world, and it is especially hushed in a city of wood and paper and tile.

And the snow does one more quiet kindness. Lying on the roof, it makes a thin, soft blanket over the tiles. Snow is cold, but it is very still, and a layer of it on a roof keeps the wind off the tiles and holds the cold air above it apart from the slightly less cold air below. A roof with snow on it loses its warmth a little more slowly than a roof swept bare by the wind.

So the answer to our small question is that the snow wraps the whole house in a quilt of its own, and the quilt is soft enough to swallow sound.

Here is the surprising part. In Edo, snow is not a hardship to be endured. It is a pleasure to be enjoyed, almost a festival. It does not come every week, and it rarely lies deep, so when it does come, people go out to look at it. They call it *yukimi*, snow viewing, the winter cousin of the springtime outings to look at cherry blossoms and the autumn evenings spent looking at the moon. On the morning after a good snowfall, people walk out to the riverbanks and the temple hills and the open places along the bay, to see the city transformed, every roof and pine branch and stone lantern frosted white. Tea houses open their screens to the view. Friends write verses. Artists sketch.

One of the best-loved snow-viewing verses in Japanese was written in the winter of 1688, by the poet Matsuo Bashō, the same wandering poet who wore a paper robe on his journeys. He was staying with friends, and snow had fallen, and he wrote a verse that has been translated many ways. In spirit it says, come then, let us go out snow viewing, as far as we can go, until we stumble and tumble down in the snow. There is laughter in it, and a kind of happy recklessness. The snow is so beautiful that you want to keep walking out into it, further and further, even if you fall.

Children have their own way of viewing the snow. In the morning, all over Edo, they will scoop it up from the garden and the edges of the street and press it into small smooth mounds, the size and shape of a rabbit crouching. Then they will find two red berries from the bushes in the garden for its eyes, and two long green leaves for its ears, and set it on a tray or a stone by the door, where it will sit, looking out at the world with its red eyes, until it melts. A snow rabbit. O-Fumi has made one every snowy morning of her life that she can remember.

And from the great bridge of Nihonbashi, on a clear morning after snow, when the air has been washed clean, you can sometimes see something wonderful. Far away to the west, beyond all the gray roofs of the city, beyond the castle and its pines, standing alone above the hills, is Mount Fuji, perfectly white from its summit almost to its feet. The people of Edo love that view more than any other. They build their streets, in some places, so that the mountain stands at the end of them. They paint it, and print it, and write verses to it, and on mornings like the one that is coming, they stop on the bridge on their way to work and simply look.

In the Minoya, nobody is going snow viewing tonight. But there will be a morning.

Now lie quietly and feel the house. You are under your quilt in the back room, feet to the warmth, and the silence is so deep that you can hear the very small sounds of the house itself. A faint tick from the cedar posts as they cool. A whisper from the kotatsu quilt as someone shifts. The soft breathing of five other people, each with its own rhythm. O-Tami's long and slow. Chōkichi's a little snuffly, from his cold nose. O-Sei's so quiet you can barely hear it at all.

There is a smell of charcoal, faint and dry and clean. A smell of cotton. A smell of new tatami, still green and grassy. A last thread of sandalwood from the altar in the corner.

And then you notice the light. The paper of the shōji toward the kitchen, the one O-Sei left open by two fingers, has a faint glow along its gap that was not there before. It is not lamplight. It is cooler than that, bluish, silvery. Out in the garden behind the house, beyond the screens, the moon has risen. It is an old moon, past full, lopsided, coming up late over the eastern roofs. And its light is falling on new snow, which throws it back up, brighter, into every crack and corner. The narrow gap in the screen is a line of pale silver. The paper beside it glows the faintest gray-blue, like the sky an hour before dawn.

It is beautiful. You lie and watch the silver line for a long time. The snow goes on falling. You cannot hear it. You only know it is there because the light keeps changing, very slightly, as the flakes drift between the moon and the garden.

Somewhere above you, on the roof, a little slab of snow slides down the smooth tiles and drops off the eaves into the garden with a soft, muffled thump. Then silence again. A moment later, another, farther along the row. It is one of the most peaceful sounds there is, the sound of snow settling itself into place.

You let your eyes close for a while, and the silver line in the screen stays with you behind your eyelids, a pale glow in the dark. You breathe in, slowly, and the cold air of the room is clean and sharp in your nose. You breathe out, slowly, and the quilt rises and falls with you.

The quilt over you is heavy and soft, and it smells faintly of the sun it soaked up two days ago on the railing behind the house. You can feel each layer of the night around you. The cotton of the quilt. The still air of the little room. The paper of the screens. The narrow gap of quiet between the paper and the rain doors. The wood of the rain doors themselves. And now, outside all of them, a new layer, the softest of all, the snow. You are at the very center of it, like the warm stone at the bottom of O-Fumi's bedding, wrapped in cloth after cloth after cloth.

Historians who study the climate of the past do not entirely agree about how cold these winters were. Some argue that the early part of the nineteenth century, around our night, was a notably cold time across much of the northern world, with harsher winters and poor harvests in some years, and that the people of Edo knew more frost and snow than the people of Tokyo know now. Others caution that a single winter, in a single city, can be warm or cold for its own reasons, and that it is easy to read too much into a few cold years. What is certain is that tonight, in this ward, it is snowing.

O-Fumi wakes. She does not know what woke her. Perhaps the silence. She lifts her head from her pillow and looks at the silver line in the screen, and her eyes go very wide.

"Grandmother," she whispers. "Is it snow?"

O-Sei, who of course was not asleep, opens her eyes. "It is snow."

"Can I see it?"

"In the morning," says O-Sei. "In the morning it will still be there, and it will be even more beautiful, and you will have had your sleep. Tonight it is only for the moon."

O-Fumi considers this. It is a hard thing to accept when you are eight and there is snow on the other side of a sheet of paper. But her feet are warm, and the stone wrapped in cloth at the bottom of her bedding is still holding a little of its heat, and her eyes are heavy. She lies back down and looks at the silver line in the screen.

"Will you wake me early?"

"I will wake you early," says O-Sei. "We will go and see it on the bridge."

That seems to settle everything. O-Fumi turns on her side, toward her grandmother, and pushes her feet a little deeper under the kotatsu quilt, where they find the warmth again, and she is asleep before her next breath has finished.

O-Sei lies awake a little longer, looking at the line of moonlight. She is thinking of other snowfalls. Of a snowfall when she was a girl, when her own grandmother took her out to see the snow on the temple roofs at dawn. Of the snowfall the winter after she was married, when her young husband came home from the shop with snow on his shoulders and laughed at her for making such a fuss of brushing it off. She smiles in the dark.

She thinks, too, of how many houses she has lived in, on this same small piece of ground. The house she came to as a bride. The house that was built after the fire when Kihei was a little boy. And now this one, new and fragrant, the third. Each one a little different, each one the same. The same narrow front, the same long passage, the same back room, the same square pit in the floor, in the same place, with the same kind of little fire in it. A house of wood and paper does not last forever. But the shape of it lasts, and the people in it, and the fire.

Then she lifts the edge of the kotatsu quilt, just a little, and peers in. The coals under their ash are still showing a few faint points of orange, steady and small. She does not move them this time. They do not need it. She lets the quilt fall, and closes her eyes.

Across the kotatsu, Chōkichi is dreaming. In his dream he is home, in his village across the bay, and it is snowing there too, on the thatched roofs and the bare rice fields and the pine trees on the hill. His mother is calling him in. His little brother is building something lopsided out of snow by the door. The big fire in the hearth is roaring, and the smoke is drifting up into the dark rafters, and there is a pot of something hot hanging over the flames. In his sleep, he smiles.

Shinkichi is asleep now, at last, on his back, his face peaceful. Chōkichi has pulled his arm back in under his quilt in his sleep and curled up small. O-Tami has not moved. Kihei, beside her, breathes slowly and deeply, one hand resting on the quilt above the kotatsu, as if, even sleeping, he were keeping a hand on the warm heart of his house.

Out in the dark shop, the stacks of paper sit in their neat bundles, cold and still, waiting for the morning. The tall lamp behind the counter was put out long ago. The little door in the front boards is barred. The hibachi behind the counter has been emptied of its coals, which were carried back and added to the kotatsu pit at bedtime, so that no fire at all is left in the front of the house. Everything there is dark, and quiet, and snow is settling softly on the roof above it.

Outside, the snow falls on the rain doors, and on the tiled roof, and on the indigo curtain's empty pole above the shop front, and on the storehouse with its white walls, and on the whole quiet ward. And inside, under the quilt, the charcoal glows on in its bed of ash, a small, steady warmth beneath a city of snow.

## Part Ten — Around a Quarter Past Midnight: The Ninth Bell in the Snow

The snow is still falling when the bell comes again, and tonight it sounds different. The three warning strokes arrive softened, rounded, as if the bronze had been wrapped in wool. Then the count begins, nine slow strokes, each one sinking into the white silence. It is the ninth hour. It is the middle of the night.

Here is a small question for this part. Why do the people of Edo call this hour the hour of the Rat?

The answer is an old one, borrowed from China long ago, like the abacus and the way of counting the bell. Besides their numbers, the twelve hours of the day and night each have the name of an animal, taken from the twelve animals that also name the years. The first of them is the Rat, and the Rat's hour is the one that holds midnight. After the Rat comes the Ox, in the small hours. Then the Tiger, before dawn. Then the Rabbit, which brings the sunrise. The Horse has midday, and so on, all the way around the clock, until the Boar, the last of them, brings the night back to the Rat again.

So when O-Sei says, as she sometimes does, that she woke in the hour of the Ox, everyone knows she means the deepest, quietest part of the night, long after midnight and long before the first birds. And when a child is told that the rice-cake men will come in the hour of the Dragon, she knows she will be up and dressed by then.

So the answer to our small question is simple. The Rat is the first of the twelve animals, and the first of the hours belongs to it, and that first hour holds the middle of the night.

Here is something gentle and surprising about this particular midnight. It falls very near the end of the year, in the last month, and the last month in Japan has a special name. It is called *shiwasu*. The usual story, told to children, is that the name means something like "teachers running," because at the end of the year everyone is so busy, paying debts and cleaning houses and preparing for the New Year, that even the Buddhist priests, the most dignified and unhurried of people, have to hurry from house to house to say their prayers. O-Fumi likes this story very much. She imagines the old priest from the temple school hitching up his robes and trotting down the street.

Scholars who study old words are not sure it is true. Some think the meaning of "teachers running" was attached to the name much later, as a kind of playful guess, and that the real roots of the word are older and plainer, something closer to "the year comes to its end." Others think the playful story may be old enough to deserve respect in its own right. Either way, the name has stuck, and in nine days' time, shiwasu will be over.

Edward Morse, our American who loved these houses, spent a great deal of time looking at tatami mats. In his book of 1886, he described how they cover the floor from wall to wall, fitted together so closely that the joins can barely be seen, and how they are kept so clean that no one would ever think of stepping on them in footwear from the street. To him, a Japanese room was really a floor, a soft, clean, fragrant floor, on which you could sit, eat, work, write, and lie down to sleep. He thought there was great wisdom in that. You are lying on such a floor now.

Lie still, and let yourself half wake, the way you sometimes do in the very middle of a long night, not quite asleep and not quite awake. The ninth stroke of the bell is fading somewhere far above the snow. Your feet are warm. They have been warm all night, tucked under the kotatsu quilt toward the buried coals. Your shoulders are warm, inside the thick collar of the robe-shaped quilt. Only the tip of your nose knows that the room is cold.

The darkness is not quite dark. The lamp in its paper box has burned down to the tiniest bead of flame, and it gives the room a faint, warm, honey-colored glow, just enough to see the shapes of the sleepers around you. Along the gap in the screen, the moonlight on the snow makes its thin silver line. Between them, the gold and the silver, the room floats in a soft half-light.

You can smell the cotton, and the charcoal, and the rush of the mats. You can feel the gentle firmness of the tatami under your mattress, springy and alive, like a meadow of dry grass. You can hear the others breathing. Nothing else. The city has gone completely quiet under the snow.

You think, drowsily, of everything you have learned tonight. The rain doors, rolling shut. The bell keeper in his tower. The papermakers at their icy vats in the mountains. The cool rice warmed with hot tea. The steam pouring from the bathhouse roof. The account books in their box by the well. The sweet potato that was better than chestnuts. And all of it, the whole of it, gathered around this one small square of coals.

Breathe in, slowly, and feel the cold, clean air of the room come into your chest. Breathe out, slowly, and feel the warmth of the quilt settle around you again, a little closer, a little heavier. Your arms are heavy. Your legs are heavy and warm. Your head is sinking into the soft rustle of the pillow. There is nothing at all that you need to do.

And you think of the sounds. The rumble of the rain doors at dusk, rolling shut all down the street. The three slow warning strokes of the bell, and the count that followed. The click of the abacus. The slurp of hot tea over cool rice. The hollow knock of the bathhouse buckets. The bright crack of the clappers, and the long soft voice that went with them. The tiny silver bell of the noodle seller, passing in the dark. And now, beneath all of them, this deep white silence, and six people breathing.

You do not need to remember any of it. You can let it go. It will still be here.

The quilt around your shoulders is so heavy and so soft that it feels as if the whole night were resting on you, gently, keeping you in place. You could not get up now if you wanted to. And you do not want to.

In the dim light, Chōkichi stirs. In his sleep, he has pulled one foot out from under the kotatsu quilt, and kicked the edge of his own quilt down, and his shoulder is bare to the cold. He does not wake. He only curls tighter, frowning a little in his dream.

Kihei wakes. Perhaps it was the bell. Perhaps it is the habit of a master who has slept with apprentices under his roof for twenty years. He lifts his head, sees the bare shoulder, and without a word, reaches across and draws the boy's quilt back up to his chin, and tucks it in. Then, very gently, he lifts the edge of the kotatsu quilt and guides the boy's cold foot back in under it, toward the warmth.

Chōkichi's frown smooths out. He sighs, deeply, and is still.

Kihei lies back down. He looks for a while at the silver line of moonlight in the screen. He is thinking, as he often thinks at this hour, about the shop. About the New Year, and the debts that will be paid and the debts that will not. About the price of Mino paper. About Shinkichi, who is going to be a good merchant, in time, and who walked so proudly beside him on the fire patrol last winter. About the house, which he drew in the dirt with a stick last spring, and which is here now, around him, shut and safe and warm.

He finds he is not worried about any of it. Not tonight. He closes his eyes.

Across the kotatsu, O-Tami murmurs something in her sleep. It sounds like a number. Then she is quiet.

O-Tami wakes for a moment too, as mothers do, and listens to the breathing of her household, counting them, without meaning to, the way she counts coins. Six. All six. Then her thoughts drift, the way thoughts do at midnight, toward the New Year. In a few days, pine branches and lengths of green bamboo will stand on either side of the shop door, to welcome the god of the new year into the house. A rope of twisted straw will hang over the entrance. There will be rice cakes, and sweet black beans, and new clothes for the children. On the first night of the year, everyone will hope for a lucky first dream. The luckiest of all, people say, is to dream of Mount Fuji, and after that, a hawk, and after that, of all things, an eggplant. O-Tami has never once dreamed of Mount Fuji. She smiles at the thought, and closes her eyes.

O-Sei is awake again. She is always awake again at the ninth bell. She sits up, slowly, the way old people do on a cold night, pulling her quilt around her shoulders like a cloak. She lifts the edge of the kotatsu quilt, and the faint orange glow from the pit lights her face from below, softening every line.

The coals are lower now. Most of what was charcoal at bedtime is pale ash. But in the center, under a thin gray crust, two pieces are still alive, glowing steadily. O-Sei takes one fresh stick of charcoal from the little basket beside the pit, and lays it gently, carefully, across the two glowing pieces, so that it touches them both. Then, with the iron chopsticks, she draws a soft blanket of ash up around it, not covering it entirely, leaving a little of its dark side open to the glow. It will catch slowly, from below, over the next hour, and give its warmth until the morning.

She watches it for a moment, to be sure. A faint thread of orange creeps along the underside of the new stick. It has taken.

"There," she whispers.

She lets the quilt fall. She lies down again, and draws her own quilt up, and settles her neck on her little wooden pillow. Beside her, O-Fumi sleeps on, her face turned toward her grandmother, her breath slow and soft.

Shinkichi turns over in his sleep, and pushes his feet a little deeper under the kotatsu quilt, and goes still.

Under the quilt, the kotatsu pit gives up its warmth in a slow, even breath, through the cotton, into six pairs of feet, into the tatami around it, into the still air of the little room. It is not much heat. It is not meant to be. It is exactly enough.

The lamp flickers once in its paper box, and steadies. The silver line of moonlight in the gap of the screen has moved a little along the mat, as the old moon climbs. The snow, out in the garden, has covered the stepping stones, and the edge of the well, and the little roof over the tub of clay by the storehouse door, so that everything out there is soft and white and round.

Shinkichi is dreaming too. In his dream he is older, and the indigo curtain hangs over the door with his name on it, and he is standing behind the counter, and a customer is asking for Mino paper, and he rubs a sheet between his thumb and finger, just the way his father does, before he says a word. In his sleep, his thumb and finger move a little on the edge of his quilt.

And O-Fumi, the smallest of them, is not dreaming at all. She is simply asleep, deeply, completely, the way only children sleep, her cheek on her hand, her breath soft and even, her feet warm under the quilt beside her grandmother's.

You lie among them, in the half-light, and you feel that same deep heaviness coming over you. Your thoughts are growing slow and soft, like snow drifting down past a window. There is no hurry. The night is long, and the coals will last.

All six are lying now around the warm square in the middle of the floor, their heads in the cool dim room and their feet in the warmth, like the spokes of a wheel around its hub. Outside, the snow falls on the ward, on the gates, on the bell tower, on the whole sleeping city. And under the quilt, the new stick of charcoal glows on the old coals, catching slowly, keeping the heart of the paper house warm.

## Part Eleven — Around Half Past Two in the Morning: The Hour of the Ox

Far away over the snow, the bell speaks again, so softly now that it is hardly more than a hum in the air. Three warning strokes, then eight. It is the eighth hour of the night, the hour of the Ox. The city of Edo is as deeply asleep as it will ever be.

Here is a small question for this part. Which is the very quietest moment of an Edo night?

The people of Edo had an answer, and a name for it. Each of the long hours of the night was divided, in the mind if not on any clock, into four parts. And the third part of the hour of the Ox, a little after two in the morning by our reckoning, was held to be the stillest, deepest point of the whole night. Everyone was asleep by then. The noodle seller had gone home. The fire patrol had finished its rounds. The gatekeepers dozed in their little huts. Even the bell keeper in his tower, people said, was only half awake between his strokes. It was the bottom of the night, the moment furthest from both dusk and dawn.

So the answer to our small question is the third part of the hour of the Ox. And it is now.

You are lying in it, in the back room of the Minoya, in the dark. The snow has stopped, or nearly. Once in a while you think you hear a last flake tick against the rain doors, but perhaps you only imagine it. The moon is higher. Its light along the gap in the screen is a little brighter and a little whiter than before.

Chōkichi turns over in his sleep, slowly, like a seal rolling in the water, and settles on his other side with a long sigh. The quilt rustles and is still. Across from him, Shinkichi mutters a word that might be a price, and does not wake. The house creaks once, very softly, as a beam somewhere shifts a hair's breadth in the cold, and then it too is silent.

You feel the weight of the quilts on you, soft and heavy, like a hand resting on your shoulder. You feel the warmth around your feet, steady and patient. You feel the cool air on your face, clean and still. There is nowhere you need to be but here.

Kenkō, the old monk who wrote his little book of thoughts around the year 1330, said that a house should be built with the summer in mind, because in winter, one can live anywhere. Lying here, at the bottom of the night, in a house built for summer, you can feel what he meant. It is cold in this room. Your breath, if you blew it out into the moonlight, would show. And yet you are warm, and safe, and so comfortable you could stay here forever. In winter, one can live anywhere. Anywhere, that is, with a thick quilt, and warm feet, and somebody who knows how to bank a fire.

O-Sei knows. She is lying very still, with her eyes closed, and for once she may truly be asleep. But her hand lies on the edge of the kotatsu quilt, as Kihei's did earlier, touching it, as if even in her sleep she wants to know where the fire is.

And that brings us to the last of our three questions. It has been waiting for us all night, outside the rain doors, ever since the dry wind blew down the street in the afternoon. Why does a whole street, dozens of families, fear one single spark?

You already know the answer. You have been hearing it all night.

You heard it in the wind. Kihei said it was from the northwest, and O-Tami said that meant it would be dry, and neither of them said any more. In the winter, Edo is dry, and the wind blows hard off the plains, and a spark carried on that wind can travel a long way.

You heard it through the walls. On one side, the old tea dealer coughing. On the other, the comb maker's baby, and its mother humming. They are a few inches of plaster and wood away. The houses of this street stand shoulder to shoulder, roof against roof, wall against wall. What touches one touches all.

You heard it in the old stories. The great fire of long ago that took the castle tower. The fire of last spring that took this very house, and every house around it, so that the whole street walked together to the river and sat on their bundles and shared their rice balls.

And you heard it in the law of the ward. Five households, answering for each other. A careless fire treated not as one family's bad luck, but as a wrong done to all the neighbors.

So the whole street fears one spark because, in a city of wood and paper, there is no such thing as one family's fire. Every coal in every kotatsu on this street belongs, in a way, to everyone. And that is the other half of the answer, the gentle half. Because the fear is shared, the care is shared too. That is why the neighbors walk the patrol in turns, with their lanterns and their clappers, calling softly to every house. That is why the landlord keeps the hooks and buckets in the watch post. That is why O-Sei wakes at the bell and looks under the quilt. She is not only keeping her own family warm. She is keeping faith with the whole street.

The fear of one spark is really a kind of promise. Every family on the street, every night, banks its coals and checks its ashes for all the others. And every other family does the same for them.

Historians have long wondered how the people of Edo felt, living with all this. Some read the old saying about fires being the flowers of Edo as a kind of bravado, a way of laughing at something too large to fear every day. Others read it as something closer to acceptance, even pride, in a city that burned and rebuilt and burned and rebuilt and never stopped being itself. Perhaps, on a night like this one, it was simply something nobody needed to think about at all, because the coals were banked and the patrol had passed.

You lie quietly in the dark, and let it go. There is nothing to fear tonight. The coals are buried deep and safe in their clay-lined pit. The rain doors are shut. The snow lies soft and wet over every roof on the street, a white quilt over the whole ward. The neighbors on either side are sleeping. The patrol has made its rounds and gone home to bed. Everything that could be done has been done, by everyone, and done well.

Your breath is slow now. Slower than it has been all night. Your body is heavy and warm, from your toes, tucked in toward the coals, all the way up to your shoulders, wrapped in the soft collar of the quilt. Only the very tip of your nose is cool, and even that does not matter anymore.

The room is very dim. The lamp in its paper box is a tiny golden seed of light. The silver line of moonlight lies across the mats. Between them, the shapes of the sleepers are soft and dark and still, like hills under snow.

Kihei sleeps on his back, his hand resting on the quilt above the kotatsu, his face calm.

O-Tami sleeps on her side, turned toward him, one hand tucked under her cheek. Her fingers have stopped counting.

Shinkichi sleeps with his mouth a little open and his hair rumpled, looking younger than fifteen. All the dignity of the counter has gone out of his face, and what is left is a boy.

Chōkichi sleeps curled up small, with his quilt tucked in where Kihei left it, and his cold foot long since warm again.

O-Fumi sleeps against her grandmother, her small hand on the edge of O-Sei's quilt, her breath a tiny, even whisper.

And O-Sei sleeps, at last, truly sleeps, her neck on her little wooden pillow, her hands folded on her collar, her face soft and smooth in the gentle dark.

You listen to them, one by one. Six breaths, each in its own rhythm, rising and falling. Like the slow waves of the bay, far off, coming in under the snow.

In the middle of them all, the stick of charcoal that O-Sei laid on the old coals at midnight has caught all the way through now. It glows under its blanket of ash, a deep, quiet orange, giving up its warmth slowly, evenly, into the quilt, into the mats, into six pairs of sleeping feet.

There is nothing more to do tonight. Nothing more to learn. Nothing more to fear. Only to lie here, and be warm, and listen to the house breathe.

Think of the street around you, sleeping. Next door, the old tea dealer has stopped coughing, and lies beside his wife under their own quilt, their feet toward their own little bed of coals. On the other side, the comb maker's baby is asleep at last against its mother, and the comb maker snores, gently, like a cat purring. Down at the end of the ward, the gatekeeper has drawn his quilt around his shoulders in his tiny hut and nodded off beside his brazier, his clappers on the shelf beside him. The men of the patrol are home, in their own back rooms, their lanterns blown out and hung on their hooks, their wives grumbling sleepily about their cold feet.

All down the street it is the same. House after house after house, shoulder to shoulder under the snow, and in every one of them, a family asleep around a little buried fire. The fear that the street shares is asleep too. Only the care is awake, very quietly, in every banked bed of ash, in every hand that rested on a quilt before its owner slept.

Breathe in, slowly. The air is cool, and it smells of charcoal and cotton and new straw. Breathe out, slowly, and feel yourself sink a little deeper into the soft floor of the house. Breathe in again, and hear the six breaths around you. Breathe out, and let your own breath join them, until you cannot quite tell which one is yours.

Somewhere far off, beyond the snow, a dog barks once, sleepily, and is quiet. Then nothing.

The cold sits outside the rain doors like a patient guest who knows he will not be let in tonight. The snow sits on the roof like a quilt over a quilt. The paper of the screens is pale and still. The wood of the house holds itself quietly in the dark, posts and beams and boards, the shape the carpenters gave it last spring, the same shape the house had before, and before that, and before that, all the way back.

You think, drowsily, that this is what the house was for. Not the shop. Not the paper. Not even the shelter from the snow. This. Six people asleep in a small warm room, close enough to hear each other breathe, gathered around one little fire, through a long cold night.

Let your hands lie open and loose on the quilt. Let your jaw soften. Let your forehead go smooth, the way O-Sei's has gone smooth in her sleep. There is no list to remember, no account to settle, no shutter left to close. The house has closed itself around you, panel by panel, and the night has closed itself around the house.

If a thought comes, let it come, and let it go again, like the noodle seller's little bell passing in the street, growing smaller and smaller, until you cannot hear it at all.

Your eyes are closed. They have perhaps been closed for some time. It does not matter. You can see it all anyway, in the soft dark behind your eyelids. The glow of the paper lamp. The silver line of moonlight on the mats. The rounded shapes of the sleepers. And in the middle of them, under the quilt, the faint warm orange of the coals.

The little lamp flickers once, and steadies. The moonlight lies on the mats. The snow lies on the roof. And under the quilt, the charcoal glows on, the warm, quiet heart of the house on this street, on this night, in this city made of wood and paper.

## Part Twelve — Around Half Past Four in the Morning: Toward First Light

The bell comes one more time, so faint across the snow that it is almost a memory of a bell. Seven slow strokes. It is the seventh hour, the last hour of the night. Morning is still far off. But somewhere beyond the eastern roofs, beyond the bay, the dark is beginning, very slowly, to think about thinning.

Here is a small question for this part. What is left for the house to do now?

Nothing at all. That is the answer. Everything has been done. The rain doors were shut at dusk. The supper was eaten. The bath was taken. The books were put away. The beds were laid out. The coals were banked, and banked again. The patrol has passed. The snow has fallen. The house only has to go on sleeping, a little longer, until the light comes.

You are sleeping too, or very nearly. You are somewhere soft and warm between the two, where thoughts become pictures and pictures become nothing much at all.

The back room is very dim. The lamp in its paper box has burned its oil almost to the bottom of the dish, and its flame is the smallest it has been all night, a tiny gold point, like a single star behind thin cloud. The moon has gone over the roof, and the silver line along the gap in the screen has faded to a soft gray. The room is as dark now as it will be, and as warm.

Let your mind wander gently through the dark house, the way a cat might wander through it at night, soft-footed, unhurried. Out of the back room, through the fusuma, into the middle room, which is cold and empty and smells of cedar. Down onto the earthen floor of the passage, packed hard and smooth and very cold underfoot. Past the kitchen, where the clay stove sits dark and cold, with the big iron rice pot on top of it, ready and waiting for the morning fire. Past the big water jar, with its wooden lid. Past the wooden tub, where the rice from yesterday sits under its cloth.

On through to the shop at the front, where the bundles of paper sit in their neat stacks in the dark, cream and white, cold to the touch, smelling faintly of dry grass and flour. The counter. The empty hibachi with its gray ash, smoothed flat. The little barred door in the front boards. The folded paper lantern on its hook, with the shop's name painted on its side.

And then back again, all the way back, along the cold earthen passage, up onto the mats, through the fusuma, into the back room, into the warmth. It is like coming in from the rain. The air here is different. It is still cool. But it is a cool that has been breathed in and breathed out by six people all night, and warmed a little by the coals, and held close by the screens. It is the air of a home.

Edward Morse wrote, all those years ago in his book of 1886, that the hibachi was "an inseparable accompaniment of every Japanese home, from the most exalted to the very humblest." He might have said the same of the kotatsu on a winter night. In great houses and small ones, all across the sleeping city, in rooms just like this one, families are lying with their feet toward a little bed of banked coals, under quilts, in the dark. Thousands of small, quiet fires, buried in ash, glowing softly under the snow.

You do not need to think about any of them. Only this one.

Your feet are warm. They have been warm all night. Your legs are heavy. Your arms are heavy. Your shoulders have sunk down into the mattress, into the soft, springy tatami beneath it, into the floor of the house, which holds you up without any effort at all. Your breath comes and goes, slow and even, like the breath of the sleepers around you.

Somewhere in the house, a timber ticks as it settles. Somewhere outside, a little snow slips from a branch in the garden and falls with a sound like a sigh. Then quiet again, deep and soft.

The quilt over the kotatsu has been warm all night. If you could rest your hand on it now, in the middle, over the pit, you would feel the warmth coming up through the cotton, faint and steady, like the warmth of a sleeping cat curled on a lap. It is the gentlest heat there is. Not enough to warm a room. Only enough to warm the people who need it, exactly where they need it, for exactly as long as the night lasts.

Think of everything that little fire has seen since the afternoon. O-Sei's hands, lifting the quilt to look in. Chōkichi's frozen fingers, sliding under its edge after the shutters were closed. The supper trays set down around it. The steaming family back from the bath, with their hot sweet potatoes wrapped in paper. The account book lying open on its frame, and the click of the abacus. The beds laid out around it like the spokes of a wheel. Six pairs of feet, pushed in toward its warmth, one by one. The fresh stick of charcoal laid on at midnight, catching slowly from below. All of it gathered around one small square of glowing coals, in a clay-lined pit, in the floor of a paper house.

You let the pictures drift past, one by one, and fade, like snowflakes past a lantern. You do not need to hold on to any of them.

Your breath is very slow now. In, and a long pause, and out. In, and out. The mattress holds you. The floor holds the mattress. The house holds the floor. The snow holds the house. And the night holds everything, gently, the way you might hold a bowl of warm tea in both hands.

Somewhere far beyond the walls, the morning that is coming is already taking shape. You can almost see it, if you let yourself drift. The snow lying white and unbroken along the street. The great wooden bridge, its railings capped with snow, the river running dark and quiet beneath it. And far away to the west, if the sky is clear, the white mountain, standing alone above the roofs, waiting for a small girl and her grandmother to come and look at it. There will be a snow rabbit by the door, with red berry eyes. There will be hot rice from the morning pot, and miso soup, and the kettle singing on the coals.

But all of that belongs to the morning, and the morning is not here yet. It is still far off, beyond the bay, walking slowly toward us across the snow. There is plenty of time. There is all the time in the world.

So let the morning wait. Let the snow lie. Let the bridge and the mountain keep until the light. Here, in the back room, there is only the dark, and the warmth, and the breathing.

Let us look at them one more time, one by one, as the house sleeps.

O-Sei, sixty-eight, the keeper of the fire, sleeps with her hands folded on her collar and her neck on her little wooden pillow, very straight, very peaceful. Her face, in the dim light, looks younger than it did in the afternoon. All night she watched the coals, and now, at last, she lets them watch themselves.

O-Fumi, eight, sleeps curled against her grandmother, her cheek on her hand. Her little brush lies on the mat beside her pillow, waiting for morning. In a few hours, she will be woken early, and wrapped in her padded jacket, and taken by the hand to the great bridge, to see the snow. But that is a long way off, and for now she is simply asleep.

Kihei, forty-four, the paper dealer, sleeps on his back, his hand resting on the quilt above the kotatsu. His face is calm. The house he drew in the dirt with a stick is all around him, whole and warm.

O-Tami, thirty-nine, sleeps turned toward her husband, her fingers still at last. The accounts can wait. The New Year can wait. Everything can wait until morning.

Shinkichi, fifteen, sleeps with his hair rumpled and his mouth a little open, dreaming of an indigo curtain with his name on it.

And Chōkichi, twelve, the apprentice, sleeps curled up small, tucked in where his master tucked him, warm all the way through, dreaming perhaps of a village across the bay, where it is also snowing, and where his mother is also asleep.

You lie among them, the seventh sleeper, and nobody minds you there. Your feet are under the same quilt as theirs. Your breath rises and falls with theirs. For this one night, you have been part of the house.

Six people. One narrow house. One small fire.

Outside, the snow lies over everything. Over the rain doors and the tiled roof. Over the white walls of the storehouse and the little roof above the tub of clay. Over the stepping stones and the well. Over the gates at the ends of the ward, and the gatekeeper's hut, and the watch post with its little ladder. Over the bathhouse, cold now, and the noodle seller's empty stand, and the long gray rooftops of the whole sleeping city, all the way to the castle and its pines. Soft, and white, and still.

And inside, in the back room of the Minoya, under the heavy quilt, in the small square pit in the floor, the last of the night's charcoal glows on in its bed of ash.

And now, very slowly, so slowly you might miss it, something changes in the paper of the screens. They were dark. Now they are not quite dark. A very faint gray is coming into them, the color of ash, the color of a dove's wing. It is not yet light. It is only the promise of light, beginning far away over the bay and making its way, very gently, across the snowy roofs of the whole city to this one narrow house, to this one small room.

The lamp is almost out. The gray in the paper is almost there. In a little while, the first birds will stir in the snowy garden. In a while after that, the bell will ring the sixth hour of dawn, and the rain doors will rumble open all down the street, and O-Sei will wake O-Fumi to go and see the snow. But not yet. Not for a long time yet.

For now, the house sleeps on, in the soft gray hush before morning, warm and safe and still.

Thank you for spending this long winter night with us in Edo, and if it brought you a little peace, a like or a subscribe is always a kind way to say so. If there is a time or place you would love to spend a night in, leave it in the comments, because your ideas truly help.

Another night, we will go somewhere very different, to another kind of house, in another kind of winter, and sit beside another kind of fire.

But for now, stay here a little longer. Let your eyes stay closed. Let your feet stay warm. The paper walls are quiet. The snow is soft on the roof. And under the quilt, the charcoal glows, orange and patient, like a sleeping animal breathing, the warm heart of the paper house, keeping everyone safe until morning.

Sleep well, friend. Good night.

- **Computed.** I computed the astronomical times for Edo (35.68 N, 139.77 E, local mean time) on 15 January 1830 with PyEphem:
