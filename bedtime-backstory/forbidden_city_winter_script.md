# How the Emperor Survived Winter in the Forbidden City | History for Sleep

## Part One — Around Two in the Afternoon: Yellow Roofs Under a White Sky

It is a January afternoon in Beijing, in the year 1773, and the cold has come down from the north like a closed door. Over the Forbidden City, the sky is white and low, and the yellow roofs sit under it in long, quiet rows. The wind smells of dust and frost and far-off coal smoke. And in one small inner room, in a hall called the Hall of Mental Cultivation, there is a broad brick bed, a kang, that is already warm to the touch, although no flame is burning anywhere in that room.

Not the Forbidden City of the films, all thrones and plots and swirling silk. Just one ordinary winter night, in one ordinary corner of an extraordinary palace.

And without a few old tricks, you would not have lasted until morning, not in a city of wooden halls, where the air outside is cold enough to split a jar of water.

So tonight I would like you to carry three small questions with you. The first is this. How do you heat a palace made almost entirely of wood, without burning it to the ground? The second. In a city this cold, where did the summer ice come from, the ice that cooled the emperor's fruit in July? And the third. Who stayed awake all night long, so that the emperor could sleep?

These are not three separate questions. They are three doors into the same hall, on the same night, and we will open each of them in its own time.

The emperor we will meet tonight is real, and so is his hall. The eunuchs and maids who look after him never quite existed under the names I will give them, but every detail of their night is real.

So let your head grow heavy now. Let your shoulders loosen and drop. Your eyes may close whenever they like, and the warm bed in the inner room will stay warm without you. Let us go back, gently, to a white winter afternoon, to a courtyard deep inside the palace walls. So let us begin.

The courtyard is paved with large gray bricks, swept clean this morning, with a thin rime of frost still lying in the joints between them. On its north side stands a hall with red walls, red columns and a roof of glazed yellow tile, its eaves turned up a little at the corners, like the brim of a hat lifted in greeting. Above the central door hangs a board with gold characters on a blue ground. They read Yangxin Dian. In English we usually call it the Hall of Mental Cultivation. A softer translation might be the Hall for Nourishing the Heart.

This is where the emperor lives. Not in the great ceremonial halls in the middle of the palace, which you may have seen in pictures, rising on their white marble terraces. Those are for the grand occasions, for the New Year audiences, the enthronements and the formal banquets. The emperor does not sleep there. He lives here, in a cluster of rooms a little to the west, behind a modest red gate, in a hall that is smaller and lower and, on a night like this, much warmer.

The emperor is called Hongli. But nobody in the palace would ever say that name aloud. The world knows him by the name of his reign, Qianlong, which means something like Lasting Glory. He is sixty-one years old. He has been on the throne for thirty-seven years, and he will remain there, as it happens, for more than twenty years longer. In the portraits his court painters made of him, he has a long, calm face and a level gaze. He rises early, he reads a great deal, and he writes poems almost every day of his life.

But he is only one of the people in this hall. And the people we will spend most of tonight with are the ones who keep it running.

The first is a eunuch named Liu Changshou. He is fifty-six, and he is the senior eunuch of the emperor's private rooms here. He has served in the palace since he was a boy of twelve, and he knows every door, every lamp, every quilt and every hour of this hall. His face is smooth and lined at once, and he walks with his hands tucked into the long sleeves of his dark robe.

The second is Zhao Fu. He is forty-seven, broad and quiet, and his hands are always a little gray. He is in charge of the fires of this hall. Not the fires inside it, because there are almost none, but the fires beneath it, which we will visit before long.

Working beside Zhao Fu is a boy named Zhang Deyu. He is fourteen. He came into the palace two winters ago, and he still loses his way among the gates. He fetches charcoal, trims lamps, carries water and runs errands, and he is very often cold.

Then there are two palace maids. Palace maids in the time of Qianlong were mostly girls from the bondservant families of the imperial household, chosen as young teenagers and released, in the usual run of things, when they reached their middle twenties. The elder of our two is Yulan. She is twenty-four, and she has one year of service left before she may go home. She looks after the bedding, the winter robes and the endless sewing of the hall. The younger is Xiuzhu. She is fifteen, quick and shy, and she has been in the palace for less than a year.

And outside the gate, in a fur-lined coat, with a sword at his hip, stands a young Manchu bannerman named Fulu. He is twenty-nine, and he is one of the emperor's guards. Tonight, his turn of duty is the long one, through the dark hours.

So that is our household. The emperor, and six people who serve him. Seven people around one warm bed.

Why does an emperor live in such a modest hall, when he owns the greatest palace in the land?

The answer begins with his father. For centuries, the emperors of the Ming dynasty, and then the first emperors of the Qing, had lived in a large hall in the center of the inner palace called the Palace of Heavenly Purity. Qianlong's grandfather, the great Kangxi emperor, lived there for sixty years, and died there. When his son, the Yongzheng emperor, came to the throne in 1723, he did not move into his father's rooms. Instead, he took up residence here, in the smaller Hall of Mental Cultivation, which had been built almost two centuries earlier, in the Ming dynasty. He lived and worked here for the rest of his reign. And his son, our emperor, did the same. From then on, this hall was where the emperors of China actually lived.

There is another reason it suits a winter night. A big hall is hard to heat. A small one, divided into small rooms, with low ceilings and thick walls, can hold its warmth. And so the Hall of Mental Cultivation, in the language of the palace, is full of what are called warm chambers, little rooms closed off with screens and partitions, each one a pocket of heat inside the cold.

Here is the thing that surprises most visitors. The whole Forbidden City covers about one hundred and eighty acres.

It holds close to a thousand buildings, wrapped in a high wall and a broad moat. And very nearly all of it, under the tiles and the red paint, is wood. The columns are whole tree trunks. The beams and brackets are wood. The doors, the lattice windows, the ceilings and the floors of the upper rooms, all wood, carefully joined without nails, much of it brought from the forests of the far southwest of China, from trees that took centuries to grow. The red walls are brick and plaster, but the bones of every hall are timber. And timber, as every carpenter in Beijing knows, is only waiting to become fire.

Now, our first true record of the night is the name of the hall itself. It comes from the philosopher Mencius, who taught in the fourth century before Christ. In the book of his sayings there is a line that every educated person in this palace would have known by heart. In the old translation by the Scottish scholar James Legge, it reads, "To nourish the mind there is nothing better than to make the desires few." That is where the hall gets its name. Yang xin, to nourish the heart, or the mind. It is a strange name, when you think of it, for the home of a man who owns more than anyone else on earth. But it was chosen on purpose. It is meant as a reminder, written over the door.

Historians do not entirely agree about why Yongzheng moved here in the first place. Some take him at his word, or at the word of his court, that he could not bear to live in the rooms where his father had died, and that moving out was an act of grief and respect. Others point to something more practical. This hall stands close to the western side of the inner palace, near the offices where the emperor's closest advisers worked, and a hard-working ruler who read papers late into the night may simply have wanted his desk near his ministers. Both may be true. A son can grieve and still choose a sensible house.

You are standing in the courtyard now, just inside the gate. The cold presses on your face like a cool, flat hand. Your breath comes out in a slow white cloud and drifts away sideways on the wind. Under your felt-soled boots, the bricks are hard and dry and gritty with frost. The roof above you is the color of egg yolk, and along its ridge sit a little row of glazed clay animals, a little man riding a bird at the front, then a dragon, a phoenix, a lion, a horse, all in a line, looking out over the city as if they are waiting for a parade. The red walls glow faintly even under the white sky. Somewhere close by, a door bangs softly in the wind, and a pigeon lifts off the roof with a clatter of wings and settles again.

And there is something else, which you notice only after a moment. There is no smoke. You stand in the heart of a palace where hundreds of people live, on a freezing afternoon, and above its roofs there are almost no chimneys at all.

In our own time, a building this size would be heated by a boiler in a basement, by pipes and radiators and a thermostat on a wall. Here, there is nothing like that. And yet, in a little while, the people in this hall will be warm.

Inside, behind the paper windows, Yulan is kneeling on the floor of a small room at the back of the hall, unfolding a quilt. The room is the emperor's bedchamber, in the rear part of the hall, and along one side of it runs the kang. It is a platform of brick, about knee high, long enough for a man to lie on and wide enough for three, faced with polished wood along its edge. On top of the brick lies a thick mat of felt, and on top of the felt, a soft woven mat. When Yulan lays her palm flat on it, it is warm, like a sunny wall in spring. The warmth is coming up from underneath.

Xiuzhu is beside her, holding the other end of the quilt. It is heavy silk, padded with cotton, the color of apricots, and it smells faintly of the cedar chest it has been kept in. The girls shake it out together, gently, so it billows and settles. Then they fold it lengthwise and lay it along the back of the kang, ready for tonight.

"Is it always warm like this?" Xiuzhu asks, very quietly, because one speaks quietly in this room.

"All winter," says Yulan. "From the tenth month to the second. Zhao Fu sees to it."

Xiuzhu rests her own cold hand on the mat for a moment longer than she needs to.

Out in the courtyard, Liu Changshou is walking slowly along the front of the hall, looking at everything. He looks at the paper in the lattice windows, which was pasted fresh in the autumn, and which must be checked for tears every day, because one small tear in a window on a night like this lets in a thread of cold that can be felt across the whole room. He looks at the heavy padded curtains that hang over the doors, quilted cotton covered in blue cloth, to keep the warm air in each time someone goes through. He looks at the sky, and sniffs it, the way a farmer does, and decides that it may snow before morning.

Then he stops beside a square of wooden boards set into the paving close to the wall of the hall, under the overhang of the eaves. The boards are thick, and a little scorched at their edges. He lifts the corner of one with his foot, and a breath of warm air rises out of the dark below, smelling of charcoal.

Down in the darkness, about a man's height under the courtyard, a small orange glow is breathing.

Liu Changshou lets the board fall back into place. Down there, under the courtyard of the Hall of Mental Cultivation, the fire is already lit, and the kang in the inner room is already warm.

## Part Two — Around Three O'Clock: The Charcoal in Red Baskets

By three o'clock, the white sky over the palace has turned a shade grayer, and the wind has found its way into every passage between the walls. Along a narrow lane on the western side of the inner palace, a line of men in padded coats is walking slowly, each one with a carrying pole across his shoulders, and at either end of every pole hangs a round basket, painted red.

Why would anyone paint a basket of charcoal red?

To answer that, we have to start with fuel itself, and with the city outside the walls.

Beijing in the eighteenth century was a city of perhaps a million people, sitting on a dry plain with mountains to the west and north, and with a winter that came early and stayed late. Almost every household in it needed fire for four or five months of the year. And most of that fire, for ordinary people, came from coal. There were coal mines in the Western Hills, a day or so away by mule, and a steady stream of camels and carts brought their coal into the city all winter long. Poorer families mixed the coal dust with clay and water and pressed it into round balls, which they dried in the sun and burned in small stoves. It was cheap. It was hot. And it was smoky, sulfurous and dirty, and it filled the narrow lanes of the city with a brown haze on still winter mornings.

That kind of fire could never be allowed into the emperor's rooms. Coal smoke would blacken the painted beams and the silk hangings. It would sting the eyes and catch in the throat. And in a building of wood and paper, an open coal stove was a terrible risk.

So the palace burned charcoal. Charcoal is wood that has been slowly baked in a kiln or a covered pit, with very little air, until almost everything but the carbon has been driven out of it. What is left is light, black and brittle, and it burns with a steady glow rather than a flame. Good charcoal gives off very little smoke. And the very best charcoal of all, in the eyes of the palace, was made from hardwood, from dense mountain trees, and it burned long and hot and clean.

That is the charcoal in the red baskets. It is called hongluo tan. Red basket charcoal.

Here is where the red comes from. In the late years of the Ming dynasty, a palace eunuch named Liu Ruoyu wrote a long account of life inside the Forbidden City, which later readers came to call the Ming Palace History. In it, as he describes it, the charcoal used in the inner palace was hardwood charcoal, burned in the mountains around Yizhou, a district to the southwest of Beijing. It was carried into the city, cut to a regular size, and packed into small round baskets that were coated with red earth. Those red baskets gave the charcoal its name. And the depot where it was stored became known as the Red Basket Yard. If you walk through the western part of central Beijing today, you can still find streets named after it, more than three hundred years later.

The surprising part is how carefully it was measured. The palace did not simply hand out charcoal as people needed it. Every person of rank in the inner court had a fixed daily allowance, written down in the records of the imperial household and gathered into a great book called the Palace History of the Reigning Dynasty, which was compiled under the Qianlong emperor's own orders. According to the allowance lists, the empress was given, each day in winter, twenty jin of red basket charcoal, and sixty jin of the plainer black charcoal. The jin is the old Chinese pound.

A consort of the middle ranks received ten jin of red basket charcoal a day in winter. The allowances went down, rank by rank, all the way to the lowest attendants, whose fires were small and plain. A jin, by the way, is a little heavier than our own pound.

So the empress, on a winter day, could burn more than a hundred pounds of charcoal.

Warmth, in the Forbidden City, was a matter of rank, written in a ledger.

And the ledger was very old. Under the Ming emperors, more than a century before our night, the palace had kept a whole office of eunuchs whose only work was fuel. It went by a gentle name, which translates as the Office for Cherishing Firewood. Its men received the wood and charcoal at the city gates, stored it, counted it, and sent it out to the halls. The Qing emperors reorganized the palace offices when they came to power in 1644, and handed this work to the departments of the imperial household. But the habit of care stayed exactly the same. Nothing that burned in the Forbidden City was ever casual. Every basket had a place it came from and a place it was going, and a man who answered for it.

Think of what that means in a palace of several thousand people, in a winter that lasts from the tenth month of the Chinese calendar into the second. Thousands of baskets. Tens of thousands of sticks of charcoal. All of it carried in by hand, all of it written down, all of it burned slowly, and safely, in places where it could do no harm.

You are walking behind the charcoal carriers now, along the lane. The walls on either side of you are high and red, and the lane between them is so narrow that the wind is squeezed into it and comes rushing down at you like water down a gutter. Your ears ache. You pull your fur collar up around your chin. Ahead of you, the poles creak on the men's shoulders, and the red baskets swing gently, and every few steps one of the carriers shifts his load with a small grunt. The baskets are made of split bamboo, tightly woven, and their red coating is chalky and a little faded, and it has left faint pink smudges on the men's coats where they have brushed against them.

One of the baskets has a hole in its side, and as you pass, you see the charcoal inside. It is not a heap of black crumbs. It is a stack of short, even sticks, each about the length of your hand, cut clean at both ends, and so dense and hard that they look almost like stone. When the basket swings and two of the sticks knock together, they make a small, high, clear sound, a clink, like two pieces of porcelain touching.

That sound is how the charcoal men tell good charcoal from bad. Soft, poorly made charcoal crumbles and thuds. Hard charcoal rings.

In our own time, warmth arrives through a pipe or a wire, and the only record of it is a bill at the end of the month. Here, every stick of it is carried in on a man's shoulders, through the cold, past three or four gates, and counted.

Historians are a little cautious about the word smokeless. Many modern accounts of the palace describe red basket charcoal as burning with almost no smoke at all, and well-made hardwood charcoal really is very clean. But charcoal of any kind, burning slowly in a closed room, gives off a gas that cannot be seen or smelled, and that can make people drowsy, then ill, and worse. Some historians point out that the palace's caution with open braziers, and its habit of keeping the main fires outside and below the rooms, which we will see in a little while, suggests that people understood this danger very well, even if they did not have a name for the gas. Both things are true. The charcoal was clean. And it was still treated with respect.

The carriers turn in through a side gate, into a small yard behind the Hall of Mental Cultivation, and set their baskets down in a row against a wall. Waiting for them is Zhao Fu, with his gray hands tucked into his sleeves, and the boy Zhang Deyu beside him, stamping his feet.

Zhao Fu does not thank the carriers, exactly. He nods to them, and they nod back, the way men nod who have done this together every winter afternoon for years. Then he crouches by the first basket, takes out a stick of charcoal, and turns it over in his fingers. He taps it against another. It rings. He tries a second basket, and a third. In the fourth, he finds a stick that is light and crumbly at one end, and he holds it up for Deyu to see.

"Feel that," he says.

Deyu takes it. It is lighter than the others, and when he presses it with his thumb, it leaves a black smear and breaks.

"That one was not baked long enough," says Zhao Fu. "It will smoke. Put it aside. It can go to the guardroom braziers. Not under the emperor's floor."

Deyu puts it in a small pile by the wall. There are only a few sticks in that pile by the end. Most of the charcoal is good.

Then they begin to carry it in. There is a storeroom along the back of the yard, low and dry, with a wooden door, and they stack the charcoal inside on wooden racks, off the floor, away from the damp. Zhao Fu keeps a tally on a slip of paper with a brush and a little stone of ink, and makes a small mark for every basket. Somewhere, in another office, another man will keep the same count, in a bigger book.

Deyu's hands are aching with cold by the third basket. He has been rubbing them on his coat between loads, and blowing on them, and they are red and stiff. Zhao Fu notices. He says nothing for a while. Then he goes into the storeroom and comes out with a small round object of brass, about the size of a large apple, with a handle like a basket and a lid pierced all over with little holes in the pattern of a flower. It is a hand warmer. He lifts the lid, takes a pinch of glowing charcoal from a covered pot just inside the door, buries it in the soft gray ash that already fills the little warmer, and closes the lid.

He hands it to the boy.

"Keep it in your sleeve," he says. "Not against your skin. And do not drop it."

Deyu holds the little brass globe in both hands. It is warm all over, gently, the way a cup of tea is warm. He tucks it into his wide sleeve, where it rests against his wrist, and for the first time in an hour he stops shivering.

The hand warmer is one of the small, ordinary wonders of a Beijing winter. Rich families had them in brass and copper, in enamel and silver. In the palace, there were exquisite ones, decorated with painted flowers or covered in bright cloisonné enamel, made for the ladies of the inner court to carry in their sleeves through the long cold afternoons. Deyu's is plain and dented and very old. He would not trade it for the finest one in the palace.

You can feel the warmth of it yourself, if you hold out your hands. Imagine the smooth, round weight of it in your palm, the faint roughness of the pierced lid under your fingertips, and the soft heat coming through the metal, steady and kind. Through the little flower-shaped holes in the lid, a very faint smell rises, of hot ash and wood. It is the smell of every Beijing winter.

While they work, a door opens on the other side of the hall, and there is a stir in the courtyard. The emperor has gone out. In these weeks, his mother, the Empress Dowager, now past eighty, is living in her own palace on the western side of the inner court, and the emperor was known throughout his reign for the regularity with which he went to pay his respects to her, and for the care he took over her comfort. A sedan chair is carried out through the gate, curtained in yellow, and a small group of eunuchs and guards walks with it. Liu Changshou goes with them as far as the gate, then returns, and watches them out of sight.

The courtyard goes quiet again. Yulan steps out onto the terrace for a breath of cold air, and stands there for a moment with her hands in her sleeves, looking up at the gray sky. Somewhere in the lanes beyond the wall, the charcoal carriers are walking home, their empty red baskets swinging lightly now on their poles.

Back in the yard, Zhao Fu closes the storeroom door, and the last basket is counted.

"Enough for three days," he says to Deyu. "Four, if it stays this cold and the wind drops."

Then he walks across to the low wooden cover set into the paving, under the eaves at the back of the hall, kneels, and lifts it.

Below him is a square pit, dark and warm, with a ladder of a few rungs leading down. At its bottom, in the side wall that faces the hall, there is a small arched opening, and inside it, something glows. He takes a handful of the good red basket charcoal from the bucket Deyu has brought, reaches down, and feeds it, piece by piece, into the glow.

Up in the inner room, under the felt and the woven mat, the bricks of the kang begin, very slowly, to warm a little more.

## Part Three — Around Five O'Clock: Sunset, and the Fires Beneath the Floor

A little before five o'clock, the sun, which has been only a pale coin behind the clouds all afternoon, slips down behind the Western Hills, and the light in the courtyard turns from white to blue. On this day in January, in Beijing, the sun sets at about three minutes to five. In half an hour, it will be dark.

The day has a name in the old calendar, too. By the Chinese reckoning, it is the twentieth day of the twelfth month, and the New Year is only eleven days away. And it falls in the stretch of winter that people in Beijing called the third nine. From the winter solstice onward, they counted the cold in nines, nine periods of nine days each, eighty-one days in all, until the spring. The third nine and the fourth were known as the coldest of all. On nights like this one, the air over the city can fall to about ten degrees Fahrenheit, which is around twelve degrees below freezing in Celsius, and the dry wind off the northern plains makes it feel colder still.

How does a fire burning under a courtyard warm a bed inside a room?

The answer is one of the cleverest things in the whole palace, and it is almost entirely hidden. In the language of the palace, it is called the fire floor.

It works like this. Just outside the wall of a heated room, usually below a window, under the overhang of the eaves where rain and snow cannot fall straight in, a square pit is dug into the ground. In the halls of the Forbidden City, these pits are usually about two and a half feet across, and deeper than a man's waist, lined with brick, and covered when they are not in use with heavy wooden boards. This is the stoking pit. A man climbs down into it to tend the fire.

In the wall of the pit, facing the room, there is a small firebox, a kind of brick oven built into the foundations, beneath the level of the floor. That is where the charcoal burns. And from the firebox, a set of narrow channels runs out under the whole floor of the room, like the roots of a tree, between low brick supports, beneath the large square floor tiles. The hot air from the charcoal creeps along those channels, slowly, warming the brick and the tiles above it as it goes. At the far side of the room, the channels gather together again and lead to a small outlet in the outer wall, low down, where the last of the warm air and the faint breath of the charcoal escape into the open.

So the fire is never in the room at all. It is outside, and below. Only its warmth comes in, through the floor.

And the kang, the brick bed in the inner room, works in just the same way. It is simply a raised piece of fire floor, a hollow brick platform with its own channels inside it, connected to the firebox. When the charcoal burns in the pit outside, the warm air flows through the inside of the kang, and the whole bed becomes a gentle, even stove, as wide as a man is tall, that you can lie down on.

Here is the part that surprises most people today. Brick holds heat. A thick floor of brick and tile, once warmed through, gives that heat back slowly for hours. So the stokers do not need a roaring fire. They need a small, steady one, fed little and often, with good charcoal, all through the winter. The floor does the rest. It soaks up warmth during the day and breathes it out all night, and the heat rises gently through the room from the ground up, which is exactly where cold feet and cold bodies need it most.

Because heat rises from the floor, the rooms that were heated this way had to be small, and closed, to hold it in. That is why the Hall of Mental Cultivation is broken up inside into little rooms with wooden partitions and lattice screens and padded curtains. In palace language, a room like that is a warm chamber. The hall has an East Warm Chamber and a West Warm Chamber, and in the rear hall, behind them, the emperor's bedrooms, each with its own kang.

Our record for this hour is a short and very serious sentence. The Palace History of the Reigning Dynasty, the great book of palace records gathered under Qianlong, preserves an edict written by his father, the Yongzheng emperor, in the year 1727. In it, Yongzheng ordered that the long rows of rooms beside one of the main gates of the inner palace be rebuilt, so that their back walls were sealed with brick right up under the eaves, to stop a fire from spreading from one to the next. And he gave his reason in a single line. In a plain translation, it reads, "In the palace, fire and candles demand the greatest care."

That line could stand over every door in the Forbidden City. Every person who worked here knew it, or something very like it, from their first day.

And the fire floor is the clearest answer to that warning. By putting the fire outside the room and beneath it, in a brick pit, in a brick firebox, feeding brick channels, the builders of the palace had made a way to warm a wooden building in which the wood never comes near the flame. A spark cannot jump out of a firebox buried under the ground. A falling coal cannot roll across a carpet. The only thing that enters the room is heat.

Historians have different views about where the heated kang itself came from. Some point out that it was an old Manchu habit. The Qing emperors were Manchus, a people from the northeast, beyond the Great Wall, where winters were even harsher than in Beijing, and their family houses often had kang running along three sides of the main room. Qianlong himself wrote a long poem in 1743 praising his ancestral homeland around the city of Mukden, and in 1770, only three years before our night, a French Jesuit living in Beijing, Jean-Joseph-Marie Amiot, published a translation of it in Paris. Others point out that heated brick beds were also very old in northern China, long before the Manchus came, and that the Ming palace already had heated rooms of its own. In this view, the Qing did not bring the kang to Beijing so much as find it waiting for them, and make themselves at home on it. Both views have a good deal of truth.

Now, you are down in the pit with Zhao Fu. It was not easy getting here. You climbed down the short wooden ladder, rung by rung, with your coat catching on the edges, and now you are standing on a brick floor, in a space hardly bigger than a large cupboard, with the dusk sky a small blue square above your head. It is surprisingly warm. The bricks of the walls are warm under your hand, and the air smells of charcoal and hot clay. In front of you, at about the height of your knees, is the low arched mouth of the firebox, and inside it, the charcoal glows. It is not a flame. It is a deep, soft orange, with darker veins running through it, and when Zhao Fu breathes on it, very gently, the orange brightens and pulses, as if the charcoal were breathing too.

He uses a pair of long iron tongs to lift a fresh stick from the bucket and lay it, very precisely, against the glowing heap. Then another. Then he takes a small iron rake and draws a little of the gray ash forward over the front of the fire, to slow it.

He works without hurrying. Every movement is small. He watches the fire the way you might watch a sleeping cat.

You crouch beside him, and the warmth of the firebox reaches your face, gentle and dry, like the warmth of a summer stone. Your cold cheeks begin to tingle. There is a faint ticking from the bricks around the firebox mouth, the tiny sounds of clay expanding as it warms, and once, deep inside the glow, a small soft crack, as a stick of charcoal splits along its grain.

Above your head, through the brick and the earth, the floor of the warm chamber is warming. Somewhere beyond that wall, the kang is slowly filling with heat, like a teapot.

In our own time, if you wanted to warm a cold room, you would turn a dial, or tap a screen, and somewhere a machine would wake up and do it for you. Here, warmth is something a man makes with his own hands, kneeling in a pit in the dark, one stick at a time.

Zhao Fu climbs up out of the pit and sets the boards back over it, leaving one corner a finger's width open, for air. Deyu is waiting at the top, holding a lantern, its candle already lit.

The courtyard has changed in the few minutes you were below. The blue of the sky has deepened. The red walls have gone dark brown. And all along the terrace of the hall, the lanterns are being lit.

They are not open flames. In a palace that fears fire above all things, every light is enclosed. There are lanterns of thin horn, shaved and pressed until it is almost as clear as glass, which glow a soft amber. There are lanterns of silk stretched over frames of wood or bamboo, painted with flowers and characters. And there are a few of glass, the newest and most precious kind, in frames of carved red sandalwood. Inside each one stands a tall candle. Deyu goes from one to the next with a lit taper, lifting each lantern's small door, touching the wick, and closing the door again, carefully, every time, before he moves on.

Liu Changshou follows a few steps behind him, checking each one.

"Is that door shut?" he asks, quietly, and Deyu goes back and pushes the little door of a horn lantern until it clicks.

Inside the hall, in a side room near the West Warm Chamber, the two maids are sewing by the window, trying to catch the last of the daylight before the lamps are brought. Yulan is mending the hem of a winter robe, dark blue silk lined with soft gray squirrel fur. Xiuzhu is working on something smaller, a pair of padded cotton socks, with a pattern of tiny clouds embroidered along the top in white thread. Her stitches are slower than Yulan's, and not quite so even, and she knows it.

"Smaller," says Yulan, without looking up. "And pull it less tight. The cloth must lie flat, or it will rub."

Xiuzhu pulls out the last three stitches and does them again.

Then the light from the window is simply gone. One moment the white thread is still visible on the cotton, and the next it has vanished into the gray, and the girls put their needles down at the same time, and laugh a little, softly, at themselves. A eunuch comes in with a lamp, a small brass oil lamp with a glass chimney, and sets it on the table between them. The room turns gold.

From outside comes the sound of voices and footsteps at the gate. The yellow sedan chair has come back. The emperor has returned from his mother's palace, and the household stands, and goes quietly about its work again, as the doors of the hall are opened and closed and the padded curtains swing behind him.

He will go, as he did on most evenings, into the West Warm Chamber, where his desk is. The floor under it is already warm.

Out in the yard, before he goes in, Zhao Fu does one more thing that he does every evening. He walks slowly along the front of the hall, and at each window he stops, bends, and lays the flat of his hand on the wall just above the ground, where the outlet of the flue comes through. He is feeling for warmth, and for the faint breath of air moving out of the little opening. Deyu walks beside him and copies him, window by window, his hand beside the older man's.

"Warm here," says Zhao Fu. "Warm here. A little less here. That one is pulling slowly. The channel may want sweeping in the spring." He says it almost to himself.

"How do you know it is right?" Deyu asks.

"By the hand," says Zhao Fu. "And by the nose. If you smell charcoal inside the room, it is wrong. If the wall is cold, it is wrong. If the wall is warm, and the room smells of nothing at all, then it is right."

Deyu holds his hand to the last outlet, feels the faint, warm breath of it on his palm, and nods, very seriously, as if he has been told a secret.

Above them, the windows of the hall are glowing now, one after another, soft gold behind their paper. The paper is tough, pale mulberry paper, pasted onto the inside of the lattice in the autumn, and when a lamp is lit behind it, the whole window shines like a lantern, and the lattice pattern stands out against it in dark lines, squares within squares, like the frost on a pond.

Down in the pit outside the inner room, the charcoal settles with a faint, dry whisper, and the kang above it holds its heat in the dark.

## Part Four — Around Six O'Clock: The Water Jars in Their Winter Coats

By six o'clock, it is fully dark. The lanterns along the terrace make small islands of amber light on the paving, and between them the courtyard lies in deep blue shadow. Out at the gate of the hall, the guard is changing, and a young man in a long fur-lined coat steps forward into the light to take his place. This is Fulu.

Standing on either side of the courtyard, just within the light of the lanterns, are two great round shapes, each about as tall as a man's chest, each one wrapped in what looks like a thick quilted coat. What are they?

They are water jars. And they are one of the oldest answers the palace has to its oldest fear.

All through the Forbidden City, in the courtyards and beside the great halls and along the main lanes, there are huge vessels of bronze and iron, filled with water. Some are plain iron, dark and heavy. Some are bronze. And the finest, in front of the most important halls, are bronze covered in gold, with lion-head handles on their sides, gleaming even at night. The Palace Museum today counts three hundred and eight of them, of all sizes, although other counts give fewer, and nobody is quite sure how many there were at the height of the Qing. In the palace, people sometimes called them the seas at the gate. They were there for one reason only. If a fire broke out, the water was already waiting, a few steps away, in every courtyard.

The largest of them hold more water than a whole household could carry in a day, and they were kept full all year round, by eunuchs carrying buckets from the wells.

And that leads to a very simple problem. What happens to a great jar of water, standing in an open courtyard, on a January night in Beijing?

It freezes. And a jar of solid ice is no use at all against a fire. Worse, as the water freezes, it swells, and it can crack even an iron vessel.

So here is the surprising detail. In winter, the water jars of the Forbidden City were dressed for the cold, just like the people. From about the tenth month of the Chinese calendar until the second, they were wrapped in thick padded covers of quilted cotton, with a lid over the top. And each one stood on a stone base, hollowed out underneath, with an opening in the side. Into that hollow, every day, someone put burning charcoal, so that a small, gentle fire glowed under the jar all winter long, keeping the water just above freezing.

Think of it. Hundreds of iron and bronze jars, all through the palace, each in its quilted coat, each with its own little charcoal fire burning underneath it through the night, so that the water would stay water, in case it was ever needed.

You are standing beside one now, in the dark courtyard. Fulu has walked over to it, as he does at the start of every watch, and you have followed him. Up close, the jar is enormous. Its quilted cover is gray-blue cotton, a little stained and faded, stitched in a pattern of diamonds, and when you press it with your hand, it gives softly, like a mattress. Fulu lifts the edge of the lid. Underneath, the water is black and still, with a single star of lantern light lying on it. He dips one gloved finger in. It is cold, but it moves. It is not ice.

Then he crouches and looks at the opening in the stone base. Inside, far back, there is the same small orange glow that you saw in Zhao Fu's pit. He feels the side of the stone with the back of his hand. Warm.

He stands, and nods to himself, and lets the lid fall back.

You look at him properly for the first time. He is not tall, but he is broad in his heavy coat, which is dark blue on the outside and lined with sheepskin, the curly wool showing at the collar and cuffs. On his head is a round winter hat with a turned-up brim of black fur and a tassel of red silk on top. His face is wide and weathered, with a short mustache, and his cheeks are already red with cold. The guards of the inner palace were Manchu and Mongol bannermen, men from the hereditary military families of the dynasty, and many of them had grown up with stories of hunting on horseback in the forests of the north. Fulu's grandfather, he will tell you if you ask, once rode with the old emperor Kangxi on the great autumn hunts beyond the Great Wall. Fulu himself has never hunted anything bigger than a hare, on a winter visit to his uncle's farm outside the city. But he can stand in the cold for hours without stamping his feet, and he is quietly proud of that.

You walk with him to the edge of the courtyard. Beyond the wall, very faint, you can hear the city. A dog barking far away. The creak of a cart. And a sound that rises and falls on the wind, a hollow knocking, wood on wood, that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. It is a watchman, out in the lanes of the city, beating his clapper to tell the people the hour, and to remind them, as watchmen did all over Beijing on winter nights, to mind their fires.

In our own time, a building like this would have smoke detectors on every ceiling, sprinklers in the roof, and hydrants in the street outside, with pipes running under the ground from a reservoir miles away. Here, the reservoir is a jar in a quilt, with a fire under it, and a man to check it in the dark.

And now we can answer our first question. How do you heat a palace of wood without burning it down?

You keep the fire out of the rooms. That is the whole secret, and the palace kept it in a dozen different ways at once. The main heat comes from fires burning outside the walls and beneath the floors, in brick pits and brick fireboxes, sending only warm air through brick channels under the tiles and into the hollow kang. The fuel is not smoky coal, but hard, clean charcoal, chosen stick by stick, counted, and stored in dry rooms away from the halls. The lamps are shut inside horn and silk and glass, and their little doors are closed every time. The back walls of long rows of rooms are sealed with brick, so that fire cannot run along under the eaves from one to the next. The water stands ready in every courtyard, kept liquid all winter by fires of its own. And over all of it there is a rule, written down by an emperor and repeated by every servant, that fire and candles demand the greatest care.

Not one of these tricks would have been enough alone. All of them together, kept up every single night by people like Zhao Fu, Deyu, Liu Changshou and Fulu, are why this wooden palace was still standing.

Historians are honest that it did not always work. The palace did burn, more than once, in its long history. Only a few months after it was first finished, in 1421, a fire caused by lightning destroyed the three great ceremonial halls at its center, and other fires broke out over the following centuries. Some historians doubt that a few hundred jars of water could ever have stopped a great blaze once it had taken hold in the timber roofs. Others argue that this was never really their purpose. The jars, they say, were for the first few minutes, for the small fire, the fallen lamp, the smoldering curtain, and that the real defense of the palace was the habit of care, which stopped most fires from ever starting at all. Both are fair points.

Inside the hall, in the little side room where the maids work, there is a sheet of paper pinned to the wall beside the window. It is not a picture of a battle, or of a famous landscape. It is a picture of a single branch of plum blossom, drawn in plain black ink. The branch has nine flowers on it, and each flower has nine petals. Eighty-one petals in all.

Some of the petals have been filled in with red. Most are still white.

This is called a chart for dispelling the nine nines of cold. And our source for this hour is a book about the customs of Beijing, written in the late Ming dynasty by two scholars, Liu Tong and Yu Yizheng, and printed in 1635. Describing the winter customs of the city, they wrote that on the day of the winter solstice, people would paint a single branch of plain plum blossom with eighty-one petals. Each day, they would color one petal. And when every petal was colored, the nine nines were over, and spring had come.

By tonight, twenty-three days have passed since the solstice. Xiuzhu counts them, touching each red petal with her fingertip. Twenty-two. She has not done today's yet.

Yulan gives her a small dish with a little red paint in it, and a fine brush. Xiuzhu chooses the next white petal on the third flower, and very carefully, holding her breath, she fills it in. The red is bright and wet in the lamplight.

"Fifty-eight left," she says.

"Then spring comes," says Yulan. She is looking at the chart, but her eyes have gone somewhere else, a long way off. This is her last winter in the palace. When the eighty-one petals are colored, and the next eighty-one, and the summer has passed, she will be twenty-five, and in the ordinary run of things, she will go home to her family. She has not told Xiuzhu how often she thinks about it.

Xiuzhu washes the brush in a little cup of water and dries it on a scrap of cloth. Then she looks up at the older girl.

"Will you have a chart like this at home?"

"My mother always kept one," says Yulan. "On the wall by the stove. My father used to say he could tell the weather by it."

There was a further custom, which some families followed, of marking each petal according to the day's weather, coloring the top of it on a clear day, the bottom on a cloudy one, the left for wind, the right for rain, and the very center for snow. Yulan's father, it seems, was one of those men. By the end of the winter, his chart would have been a record of every day's sky.

Xiuzhu looks at the window. Behind the paper, the night is black. She thinks of the low gray clouds she saw this afternoon.

"If it snows tomorrow," she says, "I will color the middle."

You are sitting in that little room with them now, on a low stool by the wall. The floor under your feet is warm, warmer than the air, so that your toes are comfortable for the first time since the afternoon, and you can feel the heat coming up through the soles of your felt boots. The lamp hisses very faintly. It smells of oil and warm brass. On the table, beside the dish of red paint, there is a tray with a pot of tea and three small cups, and a plate of dried persimmons, flattened and frosted white with their own sugar. Yulan pours you a cup without asking. You wrap your hands around it. Its warmth goes into your fingers and up your wrists, and the steam rises against your face and smells of roasted leaves.

Through the partition wall, from the direction of the West Warm Chamber, there is no sound at all. The emperor is at his desk. When he works, the whole hall goes quiet around him, the way a pond goes quiet around a heron.

Liu Changshou comes in for a moment, looks at the chart on the wall, and at the newly colored petal, and nods.

"Twenty-three," he says. "In my first winter here, I colored mine every day, all the way to eighty-one. I have not missed a winter since." He does not say how many winters that is. Xiuzhu tries to work it out, and gives up, and decides it must be more than forty.

He takes a dried persimmon from the plate, which is a great liberty, and the girls pretend not to see. Then he goes out again, to see to the lamps in the passage.

The girls sit for a while without speaking. Xiuzhu picks up her padded socks with the little white clouds and looks at them in the lamplight, and decides that the second sock, at least, is better than the first. Yulan takes up the blue robe again and finds her place in the hem. The lamp flame stands straight and still in its glass chimney. Somewhere in the hall, a door curtain swings and falls, and the flame leans for an instant, and straightens. Then the room is quiet once more, except for the soft pull of thread through silk.

Out in the courtyard, Fulu walks slowly to the second jar, and lifts its lid, and dips his finger in, and feels the warm stone beneath it. Then he goes back to his place by the gate.

Under the floor of the inner room, the charcoal glows on, and the kang is warm right through.

## Part Five — Around Seven O'Clock: The Evening Trays from the Kitchen

At seven o'clock, a line of eunuchs comes along the covered passage toward the side door of the hall, walking quickly and very carefully, each one carrying a lacquered box with a lid, held level in both hands. Steam slips out from under the lids and curls away into the cold. The smell of the food reaches the courtyard before they do, ginger and roasted meat and something sweet.

How many meals a day did an emperor actually eat?

Fewer than you might think. The main meals at the Qing court were two. The first was early, in the morning, often served not long after six o'clock, after the emperor had risen and dressed and begun his day. The second was in the early afternoon, around the middle of the day, and in palace language this was confusingly called the evening meal, because it was the last of the two great meals. After that, there was no grand dinner at night. There were lighter dishes, refreshments, cups of tea and milk, sweets and small plates, brought when the emperor wanted them. So the trays coming along the passage now are not a feast. They are something closer to an evening snack, though by our standards, it is a very generous one.

The court kept extraordinary records of all this. Every dish that went to the emperor's table was written down, day by day, in ledgers kept by the imperial kitchens. Many of them survive. They tell us what he ate, where he ate it, what time it was served, and even which cook made each dish.

Here is one of them, as our record for this hour. A kitchen ledger from a dozen years after our night, from the twenty-first day of the eleventh month of the year 1785, records that the Qianlong emperor took his morning meal at a quarter to seven, in a pavilion on an island in the lakes just west of the palace. The dishes listed are mostly meat, chicken and duck, pork and mutton, and wild game, the food of a Manchu ruler whose ancestors had been hunters and horsemen in the northern forests. Another ledger, for the last night of an old year, in early 1784, records that the emperor's family banquet began at two o'clock in the afternoon. Even on New Year's Eve, at the court of the Qing, the great meal was eaten in daylight.

And the ledgers name names. Again and again in the kitchen records of these years, beside dish after dish, appears the name of one cook, Zhang Dongguan. He came from Suzhou, the elegant canal city far to the south, famous for its delicate, slightly sweet cooking, and the emperor had brought him north to serve in the palace kitchens after one of his long journeys through the southern provinces. So on the emperor's table, among the mutton and the duck and the game of the Manchu north, there would also be the soft, clear, careful dishes of the south, made by a man who must have missed his own city's mild winters very much.

The surprising detail lies in the way much of that food was kept hot. The palace kitchens were some distance from the halls where the emperor ate, and a plate of food carried through a January night can be cold in minutes. So the kitchen ledgers are full of dishes listed as being served in hot pots. Some were stews and soups, brought to the table in metal pots with a little charcoal burner built into the base, still bubbling. Others were served in bowls with a double wall, filled with hot water between the layers, so that the food inside stayed warm. In the coldest months, the emperor's table was a little landscape of small, warm, steaming vessels.

Historians argue gently about what those hot pots really were. Some modern writers read the word in the old ledgers and imagine something like the hot pot of a Beijing restaurant today, a pot of simmering broth in the middle of the table, into which thin slices of mutton are dipped and cooked. Others warn that the word in the records often seems to mean simply a hot dish, served in a heated vessel, already cooked, and not a meal you cook yourself at the table. The truth is probably both, depending on the dish and the day. What everyone agrees on is that warmth, at the winter table of the Forbidden City, was something the kitchen worked very hard to protect.

The emperor ate alone. That was the custom of the court. He would sit at a table in one of his warm chambers, and the dishes would be set out before him by eunuchs, in bowls and plates of yellow porcelain, the color reserved for the emperor, painted with dragons. He would eat what he liked, and leave the rest. The leftover dishes did not go to waste. By long custom, they were sent out as gifts, as marks of favor, to members of the imperial family, to consorts, and to officials. To receive a dish from the emperor's table was an honor.

And with almost every meal came milk. The Qing court kept its own herds of dairy cows, and the emperor and the members of his family each had milk allotted to them every day, as part of their allowances. Much of it went into milk tea, in the Manchu and Mongol style, strong tea boiled with milk and a little salt or butter. It is a drink for cold places and for people who spend long days on horseback, and it was one of the small ways in which the court, even here in the middle of a Chinese city, still kept the habits of the northern steppes and forests.

Tonight, the boxes go in through the side door, and the passage is quiet again. For a moment, the smell of ginger and roasted duck hangs in the cold air where they passed, and then the wind takes it, and there is only the smell of snow clouds and charcoal.

In our own time, if we want a hot meal late in the evening, we might open a refrigerator and switch on a microwave, and have it steaming in two minutes. Here, a hot meal is a small expedition, through gates and passages and the freezing dark, with charcoal under the pot.

You are in the servants' room now, off the passage at the back of the hall. It is a small, plain room with whitewashed walls, and it has its own low kang along one side, much rougher than the emperor's, its top covered only with a reed mat, but warm. A big earthenware pot sits on a small brick stove in the corner, which has its own little flue going out through the wall. There is a smell of millet porridge, and of steamed bread, and of the sharp, salty pickled vegetables that every household in north China kept through the winter in great jars.

Zhao Fu is sitting cross-legged on the kang with a bowl in his hands. Deyu is beside him. You take your place at the end, and someone hands you a bowl too. It is millet porridge, thick and golden, with a few strips of pickled turnip on top, and a round steamed bun on the side, white and soft and still warm in the middle. You hold the bowl in both hands. The heat of it soaks into your palms. You blow on the first spoonful and taste it, and it is plain and comforting and just a little sweet.

Under you, through the reed mat, the kang is warm. Your legs are folded on it. Slowly, the cold that has been in your knees all afternoon begins to leave them.

You look around the room. On a shelf by the door, there is a row of small cloth bundles, the few possessions of the men who sleep here. On a nail hangs a padded coat, its elbows patched. Above the stove, a string of dried red chilies hangs from the beam, and a bundle of garlic, and a small paper image of the Kitchen God, the household spirit who watches over every stove in China, his painted face a little browned by years of steam. In a few days, on the twenty-third of the month, families all over Beijing will smear a little honey or sticky malt sugar on the lips of their Kitchen God, so that when he goes up to heaven to report on the household for the year, he will speak only sweet words. Deyu has already asked Zhao Fu whether they will do it here. Zhao Fu said yes. He says it every year.

Deyu eats fast, the way boys eat, and then looks at the pot. Zhao Fu, without a word, takes the boy's bowl and fills it again.

"You will grow," he says. "One day you will be too tall for the pit."

Deyu thinks about this. "Then who will tend the fire?"

"Someone else," says Zhao Fu. "Someone small." He almost smiles.

Liu Changshou comes in and sits at the other end of the kang, with a cup of tea instead of porridge. He has already eaten, earlier, standing, as he often does, between one task and the next. He sits with his eyes half closed, letting the warmth of the kang come up through him. It is the first time he has sat down since the morning.

Yulan and Xiuzhu do not eat here. The maids have their own small room, on the far side of the hall, and they eat together, with the other girls who serve in the hall. Tonight, there is a little more than usual on their tray, a plate of thin slices of cold mutton, a dish of bean curd with chili oil, and a few sweet cakes of sticky rice, rolled in sesame. The cakes have come from the emperor's table. Not from his plate, but from the great kitchen's extra trays, which the eunuchs share out in the passages when the emperor has finished. Xiuzhu eats hers in very small bites, to make it last.

Xiuzhu, who grew up in a crowded house in the bondservant quarter of the city, with seven brothers and sisters, has never in her life had a whole sweet cake to herself before this winter. She still finds that strange. She is not sure yet whether she likes the palace. But she likes the cakes, and she likes the warm floor, and she likes Yulan, who is patient with her stitches. That, she has decided, will do for now.

Yulan watches her eat, and remembers being fifteen, in her own first winter here, and nibbling her cakes in exactly the same way. She does not say so. She only pushes the plate a little closer to the younger girl.

If you could taste one of those sesame cakes, you would find it soft and chewy and only gently sweet, with a faint nutty warmth from the toasted seeds, and you would understand at once why Xiuzhu is in no hurry.

Out at the gate, someone carries a covered bowl of noodles in hot broth to Fulu, and he eats it standing up, quickly, his breath and the steam from the bowl rising together into the lantern light. Then he hands the bowl back, wipes his mustache with the back of his glove, and resumes his place.

In the West Warm Chamber, the emperor has finished his evening dishes. The yellow porcelain is carried out. A cup of tea is set at his elbow, and his desk is cleared again for work.

There is one more thing that comes to him at this hour, on most winter evenings, and it is not food. A eunuch comes in quietly with a small brass brazier, its pierced cover polished bright, and sets it down on a stand some distance from the desk, near the door. Inside it, a little of the red basket charcoal is glowing. It is only a touch of extra warmth, to take the edge off the cold that comes in each time the door curtain is lifted. It will be watched all the time it is there. And it will be taken out again before the emperor goes to bed.

In the servants' room, Zhao Fu puts down his empty bowl, unfolds his legs, and climbs down from the kang.

"Time," he says to Deyu.

Deyu finishes his bun in two bites, wraps his hands back around the little brass warmer in his sleeve, and follows him out into the cold.

There is a rhythm to Zhao Fu's night, and it is as regular as the hours. He feeds the fires at the start of the evening, then again after the evening meal, then again before the emperor goes to bed, and then, at intervals, through the night, in a pattern he has kept for so long that he no longer needs to think about it. Little and often. Never too much at once. Too much charcoal, and the floor grows too hot, and the room grows stuffy, and the smell creeps in. Too little, and the bricks begin to cool, and once a brick floor has gone cold in a Beijing January, it takes half a day to warm it again.

At the stoking pit outside the inner room, he kneels, lifts the boards, and climbs down. Deyu hands down the bucket. A few new sticks of charcoal go into the firebox, one by one, laid against the glowing heap.

Up in the bedchamber, on the far side of the wall, the kang is warm under its folded apricot quilt, waiting.

## Part Six — Around Eight O'Clock: Vermilion Ink in the West Warm Chamber

By eight o'clock, the hall has settled into its evening stillness. The lanterns along the terrace burn steadily. The courtyard is empty except for Fulu at the gate. And behind the paper windows of the West Warm Chamber, a lamp has been burning for more than two hours, and the shadow of a seated figure has hardly moved.

What does an emperor actually do at his desk, on a winter evening?

Mostly, he reads. And then he writes, in red.

The Qing empire in 1773 was one of the largest states on earth. It stretched from the forests of Manchuria to the deserts of Central Asia, from the steppes of Mongolia to the tropical coasts of the south, and it held perhaps two hundred and fifty million people, possibly more. All of it, in principle, was governed from this room. Every day, from every province, reports came in to the capital, written by governors, generals, tax officials and judges, on folded sheets of paper, each one carefully sealed. They were called memorials. Some reported harvests and rainfall. Some reported floods, or the price of rice, or the repair of a dike on the Yellow River, or the movement of troops on a distant frontier. Some asked for decisions. And the most important of them came here, to the emperor himself.

He read them. And then, with a fine brush dipped in vermilion ink, a brilliant red that only the emperor was permitted to use, he wrote his response directly on the page, in the margins or at the end. Sometimes it was a few words of approval. Sometimes it was a question, or a warning, or a long and careful instruction. Sometimes, it seems, it was a single impatient character. These are called vermilion rescripts, and thousands of them survive in archives in Beijing and in Taipei, the red ink still bright on the old paper.

Once the emperor had written his comment, the memorial went out of this room and across a short distance to the offices of the Grand Council, a small group of the emperor's most trusted ministers whose offices stood just outside the gate, close by. There, clerks copied each annotated memorial in a fast running script, kept the copies for reference, and sent the instructions on to the departments that would carry them out. From a lamp-lit desk in a warm room in the Forbidden City, the red ink went out to the edges of the world.

And this particular winter, there was something else on the emperor's desk. About a year before our night, he had issued an order to officials all over the empire, asking them to search out rare and important books, in private libraries and old family collections, and send them to the capital. It was the beginning of one of the largest projects of its kind in history, a plan to gather, check and copy out the most important books in the Chinese language into one immense library. It would take about ten years, and thousands of scholars and copyists. In this January of 1773, it was only just beginning.

Now, the room itself. The West Warm Chamber is not large. It is divided into smaller spaces still by wooden partitions, carved and lacquered, with openings shaped like moons and vases. On its walls hang scrolls of calligraphy. And above the place where the emperor sits there is a plaque, written in the hand of his father, Yongzheng, with four characters on it. They mean, roughly, Be diligent in government, and keep close to worthy men.

On either side of it hangs a couplet, two long vertical boards, also from Yongzheng's time. And this is our record for the hour. Translated plainly, the two lines say, "One man is here to govern the realm. The realm is not here to serve one man." It is an extraordinary thing to hang on the wall of a ruler's study. It is a reminder, like the name of the hall, written large where the emperor would see it every time he lifted his eyes from his work.

And at the far end of the West Warm Chamber, there is a door into the smallest room in the hall.

It is only a few paces across. It has a window looking south, with a low kang beneath it, and a seat on the kang, and a small table. The walls are crowded with little shelves and tiny framed paintings. The emperor called it the Hall of Three Rarities. He named it in 1746, a quarter of a century before our night, after three treasures he kept there, three pieces of calligraphy by famous masters of the fourth century. And the most precious of the three, to him, was a short letter by Wang Xizhi, the most famous calligrapher in Chinese history.

It is a very short letter, just a few lines long, written to a friend after a winter storm. Its title is usually given in English as Timely Clearing After Snowfall. In a plain translation, it begins, "Xizhi bows his head. A brief snowfall, and now the weather is clearing. It is lovely. I hope you are well."

That is almost all. A man, about fourteen hundred years before our night, looked out of his window at the snow and the clearing sky, and wrote a few lines to tell a friend that it was beautiful.

The emperor loved it. He wrote on the mounting beside it, in his own hand, again and again over the years, notes and poems and words of praise. Among them is a phrase that the museum which now holds the letter translates as "The one and only; a masterpiece for all time."

Historians and connoisseurs, for their part, mostly agree that the letter the emperor kept was not written by Wang Xizhi himself at all. It is now generally believed to be a copy, made with great skill in the Tang dynasty, around three hundred years after Wang Xizhi's death, by tracing and filling in the strokes of the original. Some scholars think it a very faithful copy, close enough to the master's hand to show us how he wrote. Others are more cautious, and say that what we see is partly the copyist's art. Either way, it has outlasted every one of the hands that held it. It is kept today in the National Palace Museum in Taipei.

In our own time, a ruler's office might glow with screens, and messages arrive from the far side of the world in a fraction of a second. Here, a report from the far west of the empire might take weeks to reach this desk, carried by riders changing horses at relay stations along the roads. And then the answer goes back the same way, in red ink, at the speed of a horse.

You are standing just outside the West Warm Chamber now, in the shadow of the passage, beside the heavy padded curtain that hangs over its door. You do not go in. Nobody goes in unless they are called. But the curtain does not quite meet the frame at one edge, and through the narrow gap you can see a sliver of the room. Lamplight on red lacquer. The corner of a desk, piled with folded memorials in neat stacks. A brush rest of white jade, shaped like a little range of mountains. An inkstone. And a sleeve of dark blue silk, lined with fur, resting on the desk, the hand above it holding a brush very still, the way a calligrapher holds it before the stroke.

The air that seeps through the gap is warm, and it smells of ink and lamp oil, and faintly of the sandalwood of the furniture. Beneath your felt boots, the floor of the passage is cool. But you can feel, through the gap, the warmth of the room's floor rising up into the air, gentle and even, all the way across.

The brush moves. A line of red. Then stillness again.

You step back.

And here is a small thing worth knowing about that brush. When the emperor was not writing in red on memorials, he was very often writing poems. He wrote them on journeys, at banquets, in gardens, on paintings, on porcelain, on jade, and very often at this desk. His poems were gathered into five great collections during his reign, one every twelve years or so, and the National Palace Museum in Taipei counts about forty-one thousand eight hundred poems in them. That is more, by a very long way, than the whole surviving work of many of China's most famous poets put together. Scholars still argue about how many of them he truly wrote himself, and how many were polished, or even drafted, by the learned men around him. But the habit itself was his. It was one of the most documented habits of his life. For this emperor, a poem was a way of noting down a day, the way someone else might write a line in a diary.

You let the curtain settle, very gently, so that it does not sway. Your heart is beating a little faster than before, and you are not quite sure why.

Further along the passage, Liu Changshou is waiting, as he waits every evening. He stands near the door with his hands in his sleeves, not leaning on anything, close enough to hear if he is called, and far enough not to be noticed. He has done this for so many evenings that he can do it for hours without moving his feet. Beside him, on a small table, is a covered cup of tea, kept warm in a padded basket, ready in case it is wanted.

In the side room, the maids are doing a different kind of waiting. Yulan has set up a tall frame of woven bamboo over a small covered brazier, a thing rather like a birdcage, with a smooth rounded top. Over it, she has laid the emperor's night robe, a long gown of soft silk lined with fur, and a pair of padded socks, so that the warmth of the charcoal rises through the bamboo and gently fills the cloth. The cage keeps the robe from touching the brazier. And a pinch of fragrant wood has been dropped onto the coals, so that the warmth comes through faintly scented.

Xiuzhu kneels beside it, watching. Her task, tonight, is simply to watch. To make sure nothing slips, nothing scorches, nothing touches the coals. Every few minutes, she lifts the edge of the robe and lays the back of her hand against it. Warm, but not hot.

"How will I know when it is ready?" she asks.

"When Liu Changshou comes for it," says Yulan. "That is how."

Xiuzhu smiles, and goes on watching.

Then she notices something at the window. The paper, which has been dark all evening, has gone faintly pale. She gets up quietly and goes to it, and puts her eye to a tiny hole in the corner, where a fleck of paper has worn away.

Outside, in the lantern light, small white flakes are drifting down slowly through the air, turning as they fall. They are landing on the paving, on the red balustrades, on the quilted covers of the water jars, on the fur of Fulu's hat.

It is snowing.

You are out there in it, for a moment, standing on the terrace beside the door. The cold closes around you again after the warmth of the hall, and it is a clean, still cold now, because the wind has dropped. The flakes come down out of the dark above the roof, out of nowhere, and pass through the lantern light, and fall. One lands on the back of your hand, a perfect small star, and you watch it for a moment before it melts into a bead of water. Another lands on your eyelashes. The whole courtyard is filled with a soft, faint hiss, the sound of millions of flakes touching the tiles of the roofs and the stones of the paving at once. It is the quietest sound in the world.

At the gate, Fulu has not moved. But he has tilted his head back, just a little, and is looking up into the falling snow, the way a boy would.

Along the courtyard, the quilted covers of the water jars are turning white on top. The paving around the stoking pits, though, stays dark and wet, where the snow drifts in under the eaves. The warmth rising from the fires beneath melts each flake the moment it lands. If you look along the front of the hall, you can see exactly where every firebox is, by the dark, damp squares in the whitening courtyard, like footprints of the hidden fire.

You breathe in the cold air, and it tastes of nothing at all, clean and sharp, like water from a deep well. Then you turn, and lift the padded curtain, and step back into the warmth.

"Tomorrow," says Xiuzhu, very softly, "I will color the middle of the petal."

In the West Warm Chamber, perhaps, the man at the desk has noticed it too. He was, after all, the man who kept a letter about snow in a tiny room next door, and who wrote poems about snow many times over. But he does not get up. The lamp burns. The red brush moves on.

Beneath his room, and beneath the inner room where the apricot quilt is folded on the kang, the charcoal glows in the dark, and the bricks hold their warmth, and the snow falls softly on the boards above the stoking pits.

## Part Seven — Around Nine O'Clock: The Ice Cutters on the Moat

At nine o'clock, the snow is still falling, slow and steady, and the courtyard of the Hall of Mental Cultivation has turned white from wall to wall. Somewhere above the clouds, the moon has risen, a little past full, but tonight nobody will see it. And from far off, beyond the high walls of the inner palace, beyond the outer wall, comes a faint sound that has been going on, on and off, since dark. A dull knock. A pause. Another knock. And sometimes, carried on the still air, a long, low groan, like a great door being opened very slowly.

What is that sound, out in the dark?

It is ice. Men are cutting ice, on the moat of the Forbidden City, in the middle of the night.

To understand why, we have to go back a very long way. The people of China had been storing winter ice for summer use for more than two thousand years before our night. In one of the oldest books of Chinese poetry, the Book of Songs, there is a poem about the farming year which describes, in the coldest months, the sound of men cutting ice with ringing blows, and then carrying it into the ice house. In the old ritual texts of the Zhou dynasty, there was even an official whose whole duty was ice, to see it cut in winter, stored safely, and brought out again in the heat. Cutting ice in winter, and keeping it until summer, was one of the oldest rhythms of the year in north China.

And the Qing court kept that rhythm on a grand scale. In the coldest weeks of winter, in the third nine, when the ice on the waters around the palace was at its thickest and clearest, workmen were sent out onto the ice to cut it into blocks. They came from the moat that wraps around the Forbidden City, from the long lakes just to the west of the palace, and from the channels and streams that ran through the imperial city. They worked in teams, organized by the water office of the Board of Works, more than a hundred and twenty men at a time.

Here is the detail that most people do not expect. They often worked at night.

There were good reasons for it. At night, the ice is hardest, and the cold keeps the cut edges from softening. The surface is not crowded with people. And the work could go on without interruption for as long as the cold lasted. The men cleared the snow off the ice first, then marked out a grid on its surface, and then cut along the lines with long iron ice chisels, taller than a man, with a heavy point at one end.

Each block was cut to about a foot and a half on each side, a little more than the length of a man's forearm, so that they could be stacked neatly, one on another. The whole harvest could take about a month.

Our source for this hour is a little book about the customs of Beijing through the year, written by a Manchu scholar named Dunchong and published in 1906, near the very end of the dynasty, describing the ways of the city as he had known them all his life. Of the ice, he wrote, in a plain translation, that in the third nine of winter, when the ice is solid, it is cut at night, and that after the third nine, the ice is no longer fit to use. He also described what happened to it in the summer, which we will come to in a moment.

Our night falls in the very middle of the third nine. The ice cutters are exactly on time.

You are out there with them now. You have walked out through the gates, through the snow, past the great outer wall of the palace, and down onto the moat. The ice under your boots is so thick that it does not even creak. All around you, in the falling snow, are small lights, lanterns on poles set into the ice, and in their glow you can see the men, dozens of them, in padded coats and fur caps, their faces wrapped in cloth against the cold. Some are sweeping. Some are stooping over the ice with the long chisels, lifting them and driving them down, again and again, with a dull, heavy thud that you feel through your feet as much as hear. Chips of ice fly up and glitter in the lantern light. Little by little, a straight line opens in the ice, and then another, at right angles to the first.

When a block comes free, men with iron hooks drag it up out of the black water onto the surface, and it lies there steaming faintly in the cold air, a perfect glassy cube, so clear that you can see the lantern light through it, and a few white bubbles frozen in its heart. Somewhere along the moat, the ice shifts under the weight of all this work and lets out that long, low groan you heard from the courtyard, and nobody even looks up.

The air smells of snow and of cold water. Your breath freezes on the cloth around your mouth. And above you, on the other side of the moat, the great dark wall of the Forbidden City rises up into the snow, more than thirty feet high, with the shape of a corner tower at its end, its many roofs layered one above another, like a lantern made of shadows.

Then the blocks are loaded onto sledges and dragged away along the ice, toward the cellars.

You walk beside one of the sledges for a little way. It is a low, flat frame of heavy timber on wooden runners, and two men pull it by ropes over their shoulders, leaning forward into the snow, while a third walks behind to steady the load. The runners hiss on the ice. The blocks on the sledge clink softly against one another, glass on glass. Snow settles on them, and does not melt. At the edge of the moat, the men haul the sledge up a ramp of packed snow, grunting, and then on through a small gate, and the lanterns of the ice field fall away behind you into the white dark.

How can ice cut tonight possibly last until summer? The answer is mass, and darkness, and patience. A single block left in the sun would be gone in an afternoon. But twenty-five thousand of them, packed tightly together, in a room half underground, behind walls several feet thick, with no window and the door sealed, keep each other cold. Only the outer layer melts, slowly, and the meltwater runs away down the drain. The heart of the stack stays frozen for months.

Here is what the Collected Statutes of the Qing dynasty, the great official handbook of the government, recorded about those cellars. Inside the Forbidden City itself, there were five ice cellars, holding, between them, twenty-five thousand blocks of ice. Four of them still stand today, on the western side of the palace, a short walk from the Hall of Mental Cultivation, just outside the gate beside the offices of the Grand Council. They are long, low buildings of stone and brick, half sunk into the ground, with arched roofs and walls several feet thick, and no windows at all. Their floors are paved with great slabs of stone, with a small drain in one corner to carry away the meltwater. Inside, the ice was stacked from the floor almost to the roof, block upon block, and sealed in, packed with straw, in the dark and the cold, and left there to wait for summer.

Historians are cautious about some of the numbers. The figure of twenty-five thousand blocks in the palace's own cellars comes from the official handbook, and is fairly secure. But Beijing as a whole had many more ice cellars, some belonging to the government, some to merchants who sold ice to the public in summer, and the modern estimates of how much ice the whole city stored each winter vary so widely that some historians simply decline to choose between them. Everyone agrees, though, that it was a great deal of ice, and that the city depended on it.

In our own time, we make ice in a freezer in a few hours, in any season. Here, every block of summer ice is cut by hand from a frozen moat on a winter night, and carried away in the snow.

And then, in summer, it came out again. Dunchong described how, from the beginning of the hottest days until the start of autumn, ice was handed out from the cellars to the government offices of the city, a fixed amount for each, to cool the rooms and keep food fresh. Inside the palace, ice was brought to the halls in the heat. In the emperor's rooms there were tall ice chests, some of them made of bronze covered in bright cloisonné enamel, like great square jewel boxes on stands, with a lining of lead or tin. The blocks of winter ice were packed inside them, and fruit and melons and drinks were set among the ice to chill. The lids had small openings in them, so that the cold air could breathe out into the room. There are pairs of these ice chests, from the Qianlong reign, still in the collection of the Palace Museum in Beijing today.

Imagine the summer room for a moment, so that you can feel the difference. The same West Warm Chamber where the emperor sits tonight in fur, but in July. The padded curtains gone, replaced by fine blinds of split bamboo. The paper windows replaced by thin gauze, to let the air through. The floor cool, the stoking pits closed and empty, their boards dry and dusty in the sun. And in the corners of the room, the tall enamel chests, faintly sweating, breathing out a thin, cold air that you can feel on your ankles as you pass.

So here is the answer to our second question. Where did the summer ice come from?

It came from here, from tonight, from the moat and the frozen lakes around the palace, cut by hand in the third nine of winter, in the dark, by men with long iron chisels. It was dragged on sledges into stone cellars a short walk from the emperor's bed, stacked in the cold, sealed, and kept for six months. And on a sweltering afternoon in July, a block of this very ice, cut on this very night, might be lifted out of the dark and carried to the Hall of Mental Cultivation, and packed into an enamel chest beside the emperor's desk, with a plate of peaches sitting on top of it.

Back in the courtyard, Deyu has heard the sound from the moat again. He is standing by the stoking pit with Zhao Fu, holding the lantern while the older man feeds the fire, and he tilts his head toward the distant thud of the chisels.

"Is that the ice?"

Zhao Fu nods without looking up. "Third nine," he says. "They will be at it every night now, for weeks."

"They must be so cold."

"Colder than you." Zhao Fu lays another stick of charcoal against the glow. "But in the summer, when you are sweating in the courtyard and they bring the ice into the hall, you will remember them."

Liu Changshou, passing along the terrace behind them, hears this and stops. He remembers summers. He remembers the great enamel ice chests standing in the corners of the warm chambers, which are not warm at all in July, but cool and shaded behind bamboo blinds. He remembers the faint, sweet smell of melon rising from among the ice, and the little beads of water running down the enameled sides, and the way the young eunuchs used to stand as close to the chests as they dared, to feel the cold on their faces.

He does not say any of this. He only says, "Mind the boards, Deyu. They are slippery with the snow."

Deyu nods, and steps more carefully along the snowy edge.

Then, because he is fourteen, he cannot resist. He scoops up a handful of the new snow from the edge of the terrace and presses it into a ball between his gloved hands, and looks at it, and thinks about throwing it at the nearest water jar. Liu Changshou is still watching him. Deyu looks at the snowball, and then at the old eunuch, and then, slowly, he sets it down on the balustrade, very neatly, like an offering.

Liu Changshou's face does not change. But when he turns away along the terrace, there is something at the corner of his mouth that might, in a less serious man, have been a smile.

The snowball sits on the red balustrade in the lantern light, collecting a fine white fur of new flakes.

Out on the moat, far beyond the walls, the chisels go on, thud and pause and thud, and a new block of ice rises out of the black water.

And in the inner room, on the far side of the wall, the kang is warm under its apricot quilt, the snow and the ice and the cold all held outside, beyond the brick.

## Part Eight — Around Ten O'Clock: Snow Water for the Last Cup of Tea

By ten o'clock, the snow has laid a soft white layer two fingers deep over everything in the courtyard. Along the top of the marble balustrade, it has gathered into a perfect smooth ridge, untouched, glittering a little in the lantern light. And Yulan has come out onto the terrace with a small, clean porcelain jar in one hand and a little wooden scoop in the other, and she is gathering it, very carefully, scoop by scoop, from the top of that ridge.

Why would anyone in a palace full of servants and wells make tea from melted snow?

Because this emperor loved it.

Among all the drinks that Qianlong took over his long life, the one most closely tied to his name is a winter tea he called Three Purities Tea. It was a blend of things that he and his court thought of as clean and noble and wintry. There were the blossoms of the plum tree, which flowers at the very end of winter, sometimes through the snow, and which was a symbol of purity and endurance. There were slices of Buddha's hand, a fragrant yellow citrus fruit with long curling fingers. There were pine nuts, from the evergreen pine, another symbol of long life. And all of it was steeped, with fine green tea, in water made from melted snow.

The emperor wrote a poem about it in 1746, the same year he named his little Hall of Three Rarities. And he liked that poem well enough to have it inscribed, later, on tea bowls made in the palace workshops, in porcelain and in carved red lacquer, so that whenever someone drank from one of them, the poem was there around the rim. In it, as he describes it, he sets the plum blossoms and the citron and the pine nuts in the cup, boils the snow water over a small fire in a pot on a folding stove, pours it, and sits sipping the fragrant tea and composing his verses, while the snow lies outside. It is a poem about a quiet hour, and about the pleasure of small, pure things.

That poem is our source for this hour. Several of the tea bowls that carry it, dated to the year 1746, survive today in museums and collections around the world.

And it was not only a private habit. Every year, early in the first month of the New Year, the emperor held a gathering at the Palace of Double Glory, the palace where he had lived as a young prince, to which he invited a small group of his most learned ministers and scholars. They composed poems together, on a theme he set. And they drank Three Purities Tea, from bowls inscribed with his poem. In some years, it is said, the guests were allowed to take their bowls home with them afterward, as gifts. Those gatherings had begun in 1743. In eleven days' time, when the New Year comes, there will be another one.

Here is the surprising part. Qianlong was very serious about water. In his time, people who loved tea believed that the water mattered as much as the leaves, and that the best water was the purest and the lightest. And the emperor wrote, in a short essay of his own, that of all the springs he had tasted, the finest was the Jade Spring, which rises at the foot of a hill just west of Beijing, and he named it the First Spring Under Heaven. A famous story, told and retold ever since, says that he decided this by weighing the waters, using a small measure made of silver, filling it with water from springs all over the empire, and finding that the water from Jade Spring weighed the least of all. Whether he really did it in quite that way, nobody can now be sure. But water from the Jade Spring was carried into the city for the palace, day after day, and it was the water that most often filled the emperor's teapot. By long tradition in Beijing, the water carts from the Jade Spring came into the city through the gate in the northwest corner of the wall, and that gate is still remembered, in the city's stories, as the gate of the water carts. On a snowy night, though, the emperor had another choice. Snow, freshly fallen and gathered clean, was thought the purest water of all.

Historians and collectors have trouble agreeing on where the poem was first written. One account says the emperor composed it while traveling, during a snowstorm, on a journey to the sacred mountains of Wutai in the west. Another places it at a stop along the road in the province around the capital, at a town called Dingxing. A third simply has him sitting in his study with a cup of tea, around the time of his birthday. The tea bowls themselves only give the year. All the stories agree on the snow.

You are standing beside Yulan on the terrace as she works. The snow is still falling, but more lightly now, a few large flakes drifting down and turning slowly in the air. She has chosen this spot, along the top of the balustrade, because the snow here has fallen straight from the sky onto clean marble, and nobody has walked on it or brushed against it. She takes only the top layer, the newest, lightest snow, with the edge of her little scoop. It falls into the jar with no sound at all. You can see her breath, and yours, curling up together in the cold, and the lantern light catches each flake on the rim of the jar before it disappears inside.

Your own fingers ache with cold just watching her. When the jar is full, she presses a lid onto it and carries it in, holding it against her body with both hands.

In a small room off the passage, where the emperor's tea things are kept, a eunuch of the tea room is waiting with a little brazier and a pot. The snow goes into the pot. Over the red glow of the charcoal, it shrinks and darkens and collapses, and in a minute or two, a whole jar of snow has become a small pool of clear water, hardly enough to fill two cups. That is the other thing about snow water. It takes a great deal of snow to make very little.

You lean over the pot to watch. The warmth of the brazier is on your face, and the air above the pot is damp and soft. The eunuch has laid out the other things on a small black lacquer tray beside him, and you can see each one clearly in the glow. A few dried plum blossoms, pale pink and papery, no bigger than your fingernail. Three thin slices of Buddha's hand, golden at the edges, with a sharp, sweet smell like lemon peel and incense together. A tiny heap of pine nuts, ivory white, each one shaped like a small tear. And a pinch of green tea leaves, long and flat, dark against the black lacquer. If you breathe in slowly over the tray, you can smell all of them at once, the faint honey of the plum, the bright citrus, the soft, resinous sweetness of the pine. It smells, somehow, like a clear cold morning.

Then the water heats, and begins to sing in the pot, and the eunuch watches it closely. In the old tea books, they say that water for fine tea should be heated only until the small bubbles rise like fishes' eyes, and not boiled hard. He lifts it off the brazier at just that moment.

The tea is made. The eunuch sets the pine nuts and the citron in the bottom of a covered cup of fine white porcelain, pours the snow water over the green leaves, and then, last of all, lays a single dried plum blossom on the surface. You watch it float there, pale pink, unfolding slowly in the warm water, its petals opening one by one as they soften, until it looks almost as if it had just come off the branch. The steam rises from the cup, and with it, all those small clean smells together, the honey, the citrus, the pine. The cup is covered, set on a small tray, and carried along the passage to the curtain of the West Warm Chamber, where Liu Changshou takes it in.

While that is happening, the maids are making the bed.

Yulan and Xiuzhu move together through the rear hall, into the emperor's bedchamber. The room is small and warm and very quiet, lit by a single lamp in a glass shade, set high on a stand, well away from the bed. Along one side runs the kang. Above it, hanging from a frame, are bed curtains of heavy silk, which can be let down at night to close the bed into a warm little room of its own, a room inside a room inside a room.

Yulan lays her palm flat on the felt. It is warm, evenly warm, all the way along. She moves her hand to the far end, and to the near end, and to the middle. The same. She nods. Zhao Fu has done his work well tonight.

You are standing in the doorway, and the warmth of the room comes to meet you. It is a different warmth from the warmth of a fire. There is no single hot place, no glow, no crackle. The whole room is simply warm, evenly, from the floor up, the way the air is warm on a still afternoon in late spring. You take off one glove, without quite meaning to, and the air on your bare hand is soft and kind. The silk of the bed curtains catches the lamplight and gleams like still water. The floor tiles under your feet are warm right through the soles of your boots. If you were to kneel and lay your cheek on them, they would feel like the flank of a sleeping animal.

Then the girls unfold the apricot quilt from the back of the kang, and shake it out between them, and lay it over the felt. A second quilt goes on top, lighter, of yellow silk. A long pillow, firm and cool, filled with dried tea leaves and the husks of grain, is set at the head, and over it a soft cover of embroidered silk. Xiuzhu smooths every wrinkle out of the top quilt with the flat of her hands, slowly, from the middle outward, the way Yulan taught her, until it lies perfectly flat and smooth, like the snow on the balustrade.

Then Liu Changshou comes in, and takes the night robe and the padded socks from the warming cage in the side room, and lays them over his arm, and carries them to the bedchamber. He checks the lamp. He checks the curtains. He lays the back of his hand on the kang, as the girls did, and holds it there a moment.

Then he looks around the room once more, at everything, and goes out to the passage to wait.

Xiuzhu lingers for a moment at the door, looking back at the bed, at the smooth yellow quilt and the silk curtains and the soft small lamp, and the warm floor shining faintly. She has never seen anything so peaceful in her life. Then Yulan touches her elbow, and she turns and follows.

The small brass brazier that stood near the door of the West Warm Chamber all evening is carried out now, covered, by a eunuch who walks very slowly with it, holding it level. It goes back to the tea room, where it will be emptied into a stone jar of ash. Not one coal is left burning in any room where the emperor will sleep.

A little later, there is a soft movement of curtains and footsteps in the passage. The lamp in the West Warm Chamber is turned down. The emperor has finished his work and his tea for the night, and he goes through to the rear hall, with Liu Changshou walking a pace behind. What happens in the bedchamber is the business of the eunuchs who attend him there, and of no one else.

The maids are already gone, back to their own small room on the far side of the hall. Xiuzhu is yawning. Yulan is folding the extra silk covers and putting them away in their chest, and laying a sprig of dried sweet herbs among them, against the moths.

"Did he drink it?" Xiuzhu asks, sleepily. She means the tea.

"I expect so," says Yulan. "It is his winter tea."

Out at the gate, Fulu has seen the lamp in the West Warm Chamber go dim, and the faint glow move through the hall toward the back, and he knows what it means. He straightens a little. The emperor has gone to bed, and from this moment until morning, the hall is in the hands of the night watch. In a little while, the gates of the inner palace will be closed and barred for the night, and the lanterns along the terrace will be trimmed for the long hours.

He brushes the snow from his shoulders with one gloved hand, and from the brim of his fur hat, and settles his feet a little more firmly on the paving. Then he is still again. The flakes come down past his face, and settle on his shoulders once more, and he lets them.

Behind the closed curtains of the bedchamber, the lamp is turned low. And the kang is warm, all the way along, from one end to the other, with the snow falling softly on the roof above it.

## Part Nine — Around Eleven O'Clock: The Gates Are Barred, and the Watch Begins

At eleven o'clock, far off to the north, beyond the palace walls, a drum sounds. It is a deep, slow sound, so low that you feel it more than hear it, carried over the snowy roofs of the city from the great Drum Tower, which stands north of the palace near the middle of Beijing. It beats, and pauses, and beats again. And inside the Forbidden City, the hall goes quieter still.

How did the people of the palace know what hour of the night it was?

They listened. The night, in old Beijing, was divided into five watches, each about two hours long, beginning in the evening and ending before dawn. The first watch began around seven o'clock. The second around nine. The third, the deepest watch of the night, began around eleven, and ran until about one. Then the fourth, and the fifth, which ended around five in the morning, as the first faint signs of day were still far off. At each change of watch, the drum in the Drum Tower sounded, and its beat was carried out over the city. In the streets, the watchmen took it up, walking the lanes with their wooden clappers, knocking out the number of the watch. And inside the palace, the night watch of eunuchs and guards kept the same hours.

So when the deep drum sounds now, everyone in the hall who is still awake knows, without thinking, that it is the third watch. The middle of the night has begun.

The Drum Tower itself is a great building of red walls and dark roofs, raised high on a massive brick base, and it has stood at the heart of the northern city since the early fifteenth century, built in the same years as the Forbidden City itself. Just north of it stands its partner, the Bell Tower, with a huge bronze bell hanging inside it. At the start of the night, the drum and the bell were sounded, and the city gates were closed. At the end of it, they sounded again, and the gates were opened. All through the night, between those two moments, the drum beat out the watches. For hundreds of thousands of people in Beijing, sleeping on their own warm kangs under their own low roofs, that slow, deep sound was the voice of the night itself.

There was another kind of timekeeping in the palace, too, and it was much newer. The Qing emperors loved clocks. European clocks had first arrived at the court with Jesuit missionaries in the late Ming dynasty, as gifts, and by the time of our night, the palace held one of the greatest collections of mechanical clocks in the world. There were clocks in gilded bronze, shaped like pavilions and pagodas and temples, with little figures that moved, birds that sang, flowers that opened, and tiny men who wrote characters with a brush. Some came from London, from the workshops of famous English clockmakers. Some were made right here, inside the palace, in an imperial workshop where Chinese craftsmen worked alongside a few European missionaries who knew the art. In the halls of the inner palace, on a still night, you could sometimes hear several clocks ticking in different rooms at once, and chiming the hours a moment apart.

But the drum is older, and it is the drum that tells the night watch what to do.

At the inner gate of the Hall of Mental Cultivation, the moment has come to close it. Two eunuchs swing the heavy red doors shut, one on each side, slowly, and the great iron studs on their faces catch the lantern light as they turn. The hinges groan softly in the cold. Then a long wooden bar is lifted into its iron brackets across the back of the doors, and settles with a deep, solid knock. An officer turns a key in a heavy bronze lock and hangs the key on a cord at his belt. And the little world of the hall is closed in for the night, like a box with its lid set on.

Here is the surprising detail. A palace this full of people, at night, with every gate locked, was run by rules as old as the dynasty, and many of them were written down. When the gates of the inner palace were closed at dusk, no one could pass through them without permission. The keys were kept by appointed officers. Anyone who needed to go in or out in the night had to have a proper reason and a proper token. And the eunuchs who served inside, the people who would be closest to the sleeping emperor, were bound by an especially long list of rules.

Our source for this hour is, once again, the Palace History of the Reigning Dynasty, the great book of palace records gathered under Qianlong. It preserves a series of instructions from his grandfather, the Kangxi emperor, about the conduct of palace eunuchs. In them, as the book records, eunuchs were forbidden to gamble, forbidden to drink, and forbidden to quarrel and fight among themselves, and they were strictly limited in their comings and goings through the gates. Kangxi, by all accounts, had studied the history of the Ming dynasty very carefully, and believed that in its last years, eunuchs had grown too powerful at court. He was determined that it would not happen again. His son and grandson kept the same rules.

Historians see those rules in two lights. Some argue that they worked remarkably well, and that, compared with the Ming, the eunuchs of the Qing court in the eighteenth century were kept firmly in their place as servants, with little real say in the government of the empire. Others point out that rules forbidding gambling and drinking are only written down because people gamble and drink, and that eunuchs, who stood so close to the emperor every day, always had quiet ways of making themselves useful and favored. Both views are probably right. The rules held. And the men under them were still men.

On a night like this, though, the rules mean something simple. They mean the gates are barred, the keys are put away, the lanterns are trimmed, and the people who must stay awake take their places.

You are standing in the passage outside the bedchamber now. It is dim and warm. A single lantern hangs from a bracket on the wall, its candle burning low and steady behind horn. The floor under you is warm. And in front of the closed door of the bedchamber, on a thick square of felt, Liu Changshou is settling down for the night.

He does not lie down. He sits, cross-legged, with his back straight and his hands folded in his sleeves, facing the door. Beside him, on a second square of felt, sits Deyu. Tonight is one of the boy's first nights on the inner watch, and he is trying very hard to sit as straight as the older man.

There is a small padded basket beside Liu Changshou, holding a pot of tea kept warm inside it, and two cups. There is a little bell on a cord, which could be rung to call others if it were ever needed. And that is all.

This is the night duty of the eunuchs of the bedchamber. Two or three of them would sit in the outer rooms all night, close enough to hear the emperor if he called, if he needed water, or the lamp trimmed, or a cover brought, or anything at all. They sat in turns, so that some could rest while others watched. In the morning, they would be there when he woke, and they would help him dress.

You sit down on the warm floor beside them, with your back against the wall. The quiet is very deep. You can hear the faint hiss of the candle in its lantern. You can hear Deyu breathing, a little fast, and Liu Changshou breathing, very slowly. And behind the door, if you listen hard, you can hear nothing at all, which means that all is well.

Deyu whispers, almost without sound, "How do you stay awake?"

Liu Changshou does not turn his head. "You count," he says, very softly. "Not numbers. Things. The beams in the ceiling. The petals on the lantern. The sounds outside. When you have counted them all, you begin again."

Deyu looks up at the ceiling and begins to count the beams.

You find yourself counting with him. The beams are dark and painted, with small patterns of blue and green and gold along their sides, flowers and clouds and little coins, faded by the years and the lamp smoke, so that you can only just make them out in the dimness. One. Two. Three. Between them, the ceiling is made of square wooden panels, each painted with a round flower in its center. You lose count of those almost at once. The lantern flame is so steady that its shadow on the wall does not move at all. The warmth of the floor comes up through the felt into your legs, and your back is warm against the wall, and your eyes, without quite asking you, begin to grow heavy.

You let them. Liu Changshou is awake. He will be awake all night. That is his work, and he is very good at it.

Out in the courtyard, the snow has almost stopped. Fulu has walked his round, to the gate and back along the terrace and to the gate again, and the snow on the paving shows his footprints in a long neat loop, like a line of stitches. At the corner of the hall, he meets Zhao Fu, who is coming round with his lantern and his bucket.

They nod to each other. They have met like this, at this hour, on more winter nights than either of them could count.

"Cold," says Fulu.

"Colder before dawn," says Zhao Fu. "The wind will come back."

He kneels by the boards over the stoking pit outside the bedchamber, brushes the snow off the edge of them, and lifts them. A soft breath of warm air rises into the lantern light, and the snow on the boards' edges melts at once into dark drops. Their two lanterns make a small pool of gold light in the blue snow, and inside it the two men look, for a moment, like figures in a painting of a winter night, the guard in his fur hat and the stoker in his gray coat, bent over a square of darkness in the ground. Around them, the snow lies untouched. Above them, the lattice windows of the hall glow faintly from the lamps within, and the eaves are fringed with a thin line of white.

He climbs down. Fulu holds the lantern for him, as Deyu would have done, if Deyu were not sitting on a square of felt indoors counting beams.

The glow in the firebox is lower now than it was in the evening, a deep, dusky red under a skin of gray ash. Zhao Fu stirs it gently with his little iron rake, and it brightens. Then he feeds it, a few sticks only. Not too much. The bed above has to stay warm until morning. It must not grow hot.

"That will do until the fourth watch," he says, climbing out.

Fulu lowers the lantern. Zhao Fu sets the boards back in place, leaving the corner open a finger's width, and brushes his gray hands on his coat. Then he walks on, along the front of the hall, to the next pit, and the next.

On the far side of the hall, in the little room where the maids sleep, the lamp has been put out. There is a kang there too, low and plain, along the back wall, with its own small firebox outside, which Zhao Fu has fed as well. Yulan and Xiuzhu lie side by side on it, under padded quilts of blue cotton, with their winter robes folded on a stool beside them, and their felt boots standing together by the door. The room is dark except for the faint gray of the snow light through the window paper.

Xiuzhu is not quite asleep yet. She is lying on her back, with the quilt pulled up to her chin, feeling the warmth of the brick come up through the mat and the felt into her shoulders and back. It is like lying on the sun-warmed stones of a courtyard in the autumn. She thinks of the red petal she painted this evening. She thinks of the snow falling past Fulu's hat. She thinks of the little sweet cake.

"Yulan," she whispers.

"Mm."

"Is it still snowing?"

Yulan opens one eye and looks at the pale square of the window.

"A little," she says. "Go to sleep."

Xiuzhu turns onto her side, toward the warm wall, and closes her eyes.

If you were lying there with them, in the dark, you would feel it too, the slow, even warmth of the brick beneath you, coming up through the mat and the felt into your back and your shoulders and the backs of your legs, steady as a heartbeat. You would hear the faint sigh of the quilt as Yulan turns over. You would smell the clean cotton, and the faint wood smoke in the felt, and a little of the dried herbs in the clothes chest. And above you, on the roof, you would hear the very last of the snow, so light now that it hardly makes a sound at all.

In the bedchamber, behind the silk curtains, the kang holds its warmth like a cupped hand, and the third watch goes slowly on.

## Part Ten — Around One in the Morning: Moonlight on the Snow

Around one o'clock, the clouds over Beijing begin to break apart. They thin, and tear, and drift away slowly to the south, and through the gaps the moon comes out, a little past full, high in the southern sky. Its light falls on the new snow. And all at once, the whole courtyard of the Hall of Mental Cultivation is silver.

What do the emperor's guards do in winter, when they are not standing at gates in the snow?

Some of them skate.

Out on the frozen lakes just west of the palace, the long chain of lakes that the court called the Great Liquid Pool, the Qing emperors held a great winter festival on the ice. It was called the ice games. In the weeks around the New Year, when the lakes were frozen thick and hard, hundreds of skaters from the banner armies, the hereditary soldiers of the dynasty, gathered there to perform before the emperor. They raced. They wove in and out of one another in long processions, carrying banners and flags, in a great spiral pattern across the ice, like a dragon coiling. They shot arrows from bows as they skated past a target hung from a gate. They played a rough game with a leather ball. And some of them performed feats of balance and grace, leaping and turning, standing on one another's shoulders, gliding past on one foot.

The emperor watched from a decorated sledge or a pavilion on the ice, wrapped in furs. And Qianlong loved it. He wrote about it, more than once, as an old custom of his own people, something the Manchus had brought with them from the frozen rivers of the northeast. He spoke of skating as a national custom, a part of what it meant to be a bannerman.

Our record for this hour is a painting. It is a long handscroll, nearly nineteen feet from end to end, made by two court painters of the Qianlong reign, Zhang Weibang and Yao Wenhan. It is called the Ice Games, and it is in the Palace Museum in Beijing today. Unrolled, it shows the frozen lake from above, as if from a bird's height, with the great spiral of skaters winding across it, each one a tiny, careful figure in a colored jacket, with a flag on his back. At the center sits the emperor's sledge. Along the shore, officials stand in rows in their winter robes. And all across the white ice, hundreds of men are skating, very small, very bright, in the cold clear light of a winter afternoon. One account of the festival gives the number of banner skaters taking part as about sixteen hundred.

Historians differ on what the ice games were really for. Some see them chiefly as training, a way of keeping the banner troops strong and skilled through the long winter, in a tradition that went back to the days when the Manchus fought and hunted across frozen rivers. Others see them as something more like a pageant, a beautiful, carefully staged display of the dynasty's northern roots, put on for the emperor and his court at the turn of the year. Most would say it was both, and that over the long peace of the eighteenth century, the pageant slowly grew larger than the training.

Fulu has skated in the ice games twice. He was not one of the leaping, spinning men. He was one of the hundreds in the long spiral, a small figure in a yellow-bordered jacket with a flag on his back, skating in line, keeping his place, as the great coil of men turned slowly across the lake in the winter sun. He remembers the hiss of hundreds of blades on the ice, all at once. He remembers the cold burning in his chest. And he remembers looking up, as the spiral turned, and seeing, far away across the white ice, the small, still, yellow-roofed shape of the emperor's sledge.

He thinks of it now, standing at the gate in the moonlight. In a few weeks, if the ice holds, they will be out on the lakes again.

His skates are hanging on a peg at home, in his family's small house in the banner quarter of the city, wrapped in an oiled cloth. They are not like the skates of our own time. Each one is a flat piece of wood, cut to the shape of the sole of a boot, with a single long blade of iron set into its underside, and a set of leather straps to bind it tightly over the shoe. Before the festival, he will take them down, and unwrap them, and rub the blades with a whetstone until they shine, and test the straps, and oil the leather. His little son, who is five, will sit beside him and watch, and ask, as he asked last year, whether he may come too. And Fulu will say, as he said last year, when you are bigger.

You are standing beside him. The moonlight is so bright on the snow that you can see the whole courtyard clearly, every corner of it, every line of the roofs, every carved post of the balustrade. But there is no color in it. The red walls have turned a deep gray. The yellow tiles are pale, like old ivory. The snow is blue-white and glitters faintly, as if someone had scattered powdered glass over it. Your own shadow lies beside you on the snow, sharp and dark, with the shadow of Fulu's hat next to it.

It is very cold now. The wind has not returned yet, but the clear sky has let the last warmth of the day escape upward, and the air is still and sharp and clean. When you breathe in through your nose, it stings a little. When you breathe out, your breath hangs in the moonlight for a long moment before it fades.

You pull your hands up into your sleeves, the way Liu Changshou does, and find that it works. The fur lining is soft against your wrists. Your feet, in their felt boots, are cold on the snow, but not unbearably so. And when you look down at the snow by the stoking pit nearest the gate, you can see the faint warm breath of the hidden fire rising through the gap at the corner of the boards, a thin thread of mist, silver in the moonlight, curling up and vanishing. Fire, here, is something you can only see by its breath.

There is no sound. The ice cutters on the moat have paused, or moved further off. The city beyond the walls is asleep. The drum will not sound again for some time. Somewhere, very far off, a dog barks once, and stops.

Above the roof of the hall, the little row of glazed clay animals on the ridge stands out sharp and black against the moonlit sky, the little man on his bird at the front, and the dragon, and the phoenix, and the lion, all with their little caps of snow.

And along the front of the hall, in the silver snow, there are still those dark, wet squares by the stoking pits, where the warmth from below keeps the snow from settling. Fulu looks at them. Then he looks at you, and almost smiles, as if to say that he has seen them every winter of his life, and still likes them.

You leave him at the gate and walk slowly back along the terrace. Your boots make a soft, squeaking crunch in the new snow, the sound that snow makes only when it is very cold and very dry. The marble balustrade is capped with white, and halfway along it you come to Deyu's snowball, still sitting where he left it, now wearing a little white cap of its own, perfectly round in the moonlight. You leave it be.

At the main door of the hall, the padded curtain hangs still. You lift its edge and step inside, into the warmth and the dark. Here, in the central room of the hall, there is no lamp. Only the moonlight, coming pale through the paper of the high windows, laying soft gray squares on the floor. At the back of the room, on a low platform, stands the emperor's throne, a broad chair of carved and lacquered wood, empty, with a screen behind it. Above it hangs a board with four large characters, written by Yongzheng, which mean something like Upright and fair, kind and harmonious. In the moonlight you can just make out the gold of them, faint and soft, like the last light on a river.

The room is warm. The floor beneath you is warm. It is perfectly silent. And for a moment, standing there alone in the moonlit hall, you have the strange, peaceful feeling that the whole great palace around you, all its thousand roofs and its miles of red wall and its frozen moat, is simply holding its breath, so as not to wake anyone.

You step back out, softly, and let the curtain fall.

You stand there with Fulu for a long while, saying nothing, the way he does, letting the moonlight and the cold and the quiet settle on you like the snow settled on his shoulders earlier in the night. There is something restful about it. Nothing is asked of you here. You only have to stand, and breathe, and watch the silver light lie on the roofs, and listen to the silence of the sleeping palace.

Inside, in the warm passage outside the bedchamber, the candle in its horn lantern has burned down by a third. Liu Changshou sits exactly as he sat at eleven o'clock, straight-backed, hands in his sleeves, facing the door. He has not moved.

Beside him, Deyu has lost the battle. Somewhere around the twentieth beam, or perhaps the thirtieth flower, his head grew heavy, and tipped forward, and then sideways, and now he is asleep, sitting up, leaning against the warm wall, with his mouth a little open and his hands still tucked into his sleeves around the brass warmer.

Liu Changshou looks at him for a long moment. By the rules, he ought to wake him. On the inner watch, a boy who sleeps must be woken, and a boy who sleeps often must be sent back to the outer courtyards.

But it is the boy's first winter on the inner watch. And Liu Changshou remembers his own first winter, a very long time ago, and an older eunuch with a lined face, now long dead, who let him sleep for one watch, just one, and then woke him gently and gave him a cup of hot tea.

He lets Deyu sleep. He will wake him at the fourth watch.

The boy is dreaming. You can tell from his face, which twitches once, and then smooths out, and from the small sound he makes, almost a word. Perhaps he is dreaming of his village, far to the south of the city, which he left when he was twelve, and of his mother's stove, and her hands. Perhaps he is dreaming of ice, and of men with long chisels in the snow. Perhaps he is dreaming of nothing at all, only of warmth, which at fourteen, on a cold night, is the best dream there is.

Behind the bedchamber door, there is no sound. Liu Changshou listens, as he has listened all night. Nothing. A sleeper's silence, deep and even and calm. He pours himself a little tea from the pot in the padded basket. It is still warm. He drinks it slowly, holding the cup in both hands.

And outside, in the moonlight, there is a soft creak of boards. Zhao Fu has come round again, at the edge of the fourth watch, as he said he would. You watch him kneel at the stoking pit outside the bedchamber, and lift the boards, his shadow black and long on the silver snow. A breath of warm air rises up into the cold, and turns into a small cloud of mist in the moonlight, and floats away over the courtyard, like a ghost of the fire.

He climbs down. A few sticks of charcoal. The glow rising. The ash drawn forward. Little and often.

Then he climbs back up, and sets the boards in place, and stands for a moment in the moonlight, looking up at the sky, and stretching his back, which has begun to ache on cold nights these last few winters.

Fulu, from the gate, lifts one hand to him. Zhao Fu lifts his back. Neither says a word. They do not need to.

They have never, in all these winters, had a long conversation. They do not need one. One of them keeps the fire, and the other keeps the gate, and on nights like this, each of them is glad, without saying so, to see the other's lantern moving in the dark.

Then Zhao Fu goes on, slowly and patiently, along the front of the hall, to the next pit, his lantern swinging, his footprints joining Fulu's in the snow.

In the bedchamber, behind its silk curtains, under the apricot quilt, the kang is still warm. It has been warm since the afternoon. It will be warm until morning. That is what all these quiet people, in their different places, are here to make sure of.

## Part Eleven — Around Three in the Morning: The People Who Keep the Night

Around three o'clock, the drum sounds again from the north, far and low, for the fifth watch, the last watch of the night. The moon has moved across the sky toward the west, and the shadows in the courtyard have turned and grown longer. The snow lies blue and still. And at the far end of the West Warm Chamber, the little window of the Hall of Three Rarities is a dark square of paper, silvered at one corner by the moon.

Why would a man name his smallest room after three rare things?

The first answer is the one we already know. The emperor kept three precious pieces of old calligraphy in that room, among them the little letter about the snow, and he named the room for them. But in 1746, when he gave the room its name, he also wrote a short record explaining it, as he did for many of the rooms and gardens he loved. And in it, as he explains, the word for rare, xi, can also mean to hope, or to aspire. He linked the name to a famous line by a philosopher of the eleventh century, Zhou Dunyi, which says, in a plain translation, that the scholar hopes to become worthy, the worthy man hopes to become a sage, and the sage hopes to become one with heaven. So the Hall of Three Rarities is also the Hall of Three Hopes. Three treasures on the wall, and three steps of a life, written over a door.

That short record, from 1746, is our source for this hour.

Historians who write about the room tend to lean one way or the other. Some see the name as a collector's name, first and last, a proud announcement of three of the most precious objects in the empire, kept close at hand. Others take the second meaning seriously, and see the little room as a place where an emperor tried, at least in his own eyes, to be a scholar, to read and to look and to improve himself, in a few square feet of quiet. The emperor himself, it seems, wanted it to mean both. He was a man who liked a word to do two things at once.

You are standing outside that window now, on the terrace, in the snow. The paper is pale in the moonlight, and through it, very faintly, you can make out the shapes of the lattice, little squares and diamonds, like the frost patterns on a pond. Inside, beyond the paper, the tiny room lies in darkness. You cannot see the shelves, or the small kang under the window, or the scrolls. But you know they are there. The air around you smells of snow, and of cold stone, and, very faintly, of charcoal, rising from a pit somewhere along the wall.

The window of the little room is dark tonight. The letter about the snow is in its place. And outside, the snow has stopped falling, and the sky is clearing, just as the letter said.

So here is the answer to our third question. Who stayed awake all night, so that the emperor could sleep?

The answer is, a great many people, in a great many places, and almost none of them were ever written down by name.

There is Liu Changshou, sitting on his square of felt outside the bedchamber door, straight-backed, listening to the silence, as he has done on more winter nights than he can count. There is Deyu beside him, who slept through one watch, and has now been woken, gently, with a cup of warm tea, and is sitting up again, blinking, trying very hard to look as if he had never closed his eyes. There is Zhao Fu, making his slow rounds of the stoking pits, kneeling in the snow, feeding the fires beneath the floors, little and often, so that the bricks of the kang never cool. There is Fulu at the gate, and the other guards at the other gates, all through the inner palace, standing in the cold under the moon. There is the officer with the keys on his belt.

And beyond our hall, there are more. There are the eunuchs who go from courtyard to courtyard through the night, checking the little fires under the great water jars, so that the water in their quilted coats never turns to ice. There are the ice cutters out on the moat, with their long chisels and their lanterns, cutting the summer's ice out of the winter dark. There are the drummers in the Drum Tower, beating out the watches. There are the watchmen in the lanes of the city, walking with their clappers, knocking out the hours and reminding the sleeping streets to mind their fires. And soon, before any light shows in the sky, the cooks in the imperial kitchens will be awake too, lighting their own fires, so that the emperor's morning tea and his first meal will be ready when he rises.

A palace of wood, on a January night, with a frozen moat and a sleeping emperor, does not keep itself. It is kept, hour by hour, by hands. That is the whole answer.

And now, at last, the night can begin to let them rest.

In the little room on the far side of the hall, Yulan is asleep. She fell asleep not long after Xiuzhu, with her hand under her cheek, facing the pale square of the window. Her breathing is slow and deep. Beside her, Xiuzhu is curled on her side toward the warm wall, her knees drawn up under the blue quilt, her hair loose on the pillow. She is dreaming of something that makes her smile a little in her sleep. Perhaps it is the red petal. Perhaps it is the sweet cake. Perhaps it is spring.

The kang beneath them is warm. The room is dark and quiet. Their boots stand together by the door.

Out in the courtyard, Zhao Fu has come to the last pit of his last round. He kneels. He lifts the boards. He climbs down, slowly, because his back is stiff, into the warm dark. He looks at the glow for a long moment, the way you might look at the face of someone you love who is sleeping. Then he feeds it. A few sticks. That is all. That will carry it until morning.

He climbs out. He sets the boards back in place, leaving the corner open a finger's width. He brushes his gray hands on his coat.

Then he goes, not to the next pit, because there is no next pit, but to the servants' room at the back of the hall. He lifts the padded curtain and steps inside, into the warmth. The room is dark. The embers in the little stove in the corner glow very faintly. He takes off his boots and sets them by the stove. He climbs up onto the rough kang, under its reed mat, and lies down on his side, still in his padded coat, and pulls a quilt over himself.

The warmth of the brick comes up into his tired back. It is the warmth he made. He closes his eyes.

In a few breaths, he is asleep.

You can stand in the doorway of that room for a moment, and look. It is very dark. The stove in the corner gives only the faintest red light, enough to show the round shape of the earthenware pot, and the string of dried chilies on the beam, and the little paper Kitchen God above the stove, smiling his faded smile. Zhao Fu lies on his side under his quilt, his gray hands tucked under his chin, the way a child sleeps. His breathing is slow and heavy and even. His boots stand by the stove, steaming very slightly as the snow melts off them.

The room smells of charcoal and millet and wool. It is warm, and close, and safe.

You let the curtain fall softly behind you, and leave him to his sleep.

In the passage outside the bedchamber, a soft step sounds. Another eunuch has come to sit the last part of the watch, an older man from the outer rooms, quiet and gray, with his own square of felt under his arm. He bows slightly to Liu Changshou, and Liu Changshou bows back, and rises, very slowly, unfolding himself from the felt the way an old tree unbends after a storm.

He looks down at Deyu. The boy is blinking, and his hair is pressed flat on one side where it leaned against the wall, and there is a crease on his cheek from his own sleeve.

"Come," he says, very softly.

Deyu gets up. His legs are stiff. He picks up his square of felt and the little brass warmer, and follows the old man along the passage, past the dark curtain of the West Warm Chamber, past the moonlit window of the little room of three rarities, to a small room at the end of the hall, where the eunuchs of the inner watch may sleep when their watch is done.

It is a tiny room, hardly wider than the kang inside it. There is a narrow kang along one wall, and it is warm. There are two folded quilts on it.

Liu Changshou does not say anything. He only nods toward the kang. Deyu climbs up onto it, and pulls a quilt over himself, and lies down with the brass warmer held against his chest. Its last little coal is almost out, but the brass is still faintly warm. He is asleep before Liu Changshou has finished taking off his boots.

Liu Changshou sits for a moment on the edge of the kang beside the boy. He listens. Far off, beyond the walls, the thud of an ice chisel, once, and then no more. Closer, the creak of Fulu's boots in the snow at the gate. Behind him, the boy's slow breathing.

Then he lies down too, on his back, with his hands folded on his chest, and the quilt drawn up, and the warm brick under his old shoulders. He looks up at the dark beams of the ceiling. He does not count them. He has counted them enough.

He closes his eyes, and lets the warmth hold him.

At the gate, the snow glitters under the setting moon. Fulu stands at his post, wrapped in his fur-lined coat, his breath rising in slow white clouds. His watch is nearly over. Before long, his relief will come across the courtyard, leaving a new line of footprints in the snow, and Fulu will walk back to the guardroom by the outer gate, where there is a long warm kang, and a pot of tea on the stove, and a quilt that smells of horses and wood smoke. He will lie down there, among the other guards, and sleep until noon.

But not yet. For now, he is still standing. He is looking up at the sky.

His face is calm. His cheeks are red with the cold, and there is a little frost in his mustache, where his breath has frozen on it through the long hours. His feet are numb in his boots, though he would never admit it. But he is content. The night has been quiet. The snow has been beautiful. And in a little while, he will sleep.

You are standing beside him again. The air is very cold and very still, and very clear now, cleaner than it has been all night. Above the yellow roofs, the clouds have gone. The stars are out, thousands of them, bright and sharp in the black, and the moon hangs low over the western wall, pale and soft. Your breath and Fulu's breath rise together and fade.

The stars seem very close. They seem to hang just above the ridge of the roof, above the little row of clay animals, so close that you could reach up and brush the snow off the dragon's head and touch them. They do not twinkle much, in this still air. They simply shine, steady and white, the way the snow shines below them.

There is nothing to do. Nothing to fetch, nothing to carry, nothing to watch for. Everyone who needed to be awake has done their work. The fires are fed. The jars are warm. The gates are barred. The ice is cut. The bed is warm.

You can rest now, too. Let your shoulders drop. Let your hands grow loose. Let your breathing slow, the way theirs has.

Breathe in, slowly, and feel the cold, clean air fill you. Breathe out, and watch it drift away in the starlight. In, and out. There is warmth waiting for you, just behind that padded curtain. A warm floor, and a warm room, and a warm brick bed under a soft quilt. You do not have to go yet. You can stay out here a little longer, under the stars, in the quiet, until you are ready.

Behind the walls of the rear hall, behind the silk curtains, under the apricot quilt and the yellow one, the emperor sleeps. And under him, through the felt and the mat, the bricks of the kang are warm, gently, evenly warm, from end to end, just as they were when Yulan first laid her hand on them in the white light of the afternoon.

## Part Twelve — Around Five in the Morning: The Small Hours, and the First Gray Light

Around five o'clock, the moon has gone down behind the western wall, and the courtyard of the Hall of Mental Cultivation lies in the soft, deep dark that comes just before the end of a winter night. The stars are still out. The snow is still. And the hall is quiet from end to end.

What is left to happen, in a palace where everyone is asleep?

Almost nothing. And that is the gift of this hour.

The snow lies where it fell, smooth and deep, on the roofs and the terrace and the paving. The quilted water jars wear their white caps. The little snowball sits on the balustrade in its white fur. The footprints of Fulu and Zhao Fu wind across the courtyard in long, soft loops, already blurred at their edges by the cold. Along the front of the hall, by the stoking pits, the dark damp squares in the snow are still there, a little smaller now, where the warmth of the hidden fires rises through the boards.

The wind has not come back. The air is perfectly still.

Along the terrace, the lanterns have burned low. Their candles are short stubs now, and their light is small and soft and amber behind the horn, each one a little glowing moon of its own in the snow. One of them, at the far end, has gone out altogether, its candle finished, and nobody has hurried to replace it. There is no need. The night is nearly done.

Out beyond the walls, in their long, low stone houses half sunk into the ground, the ice cellars lie dark and silent, holding the new blocks cut tonight, stacked in the cold, waiting patiently for July. In every courtyard of the inner palace, under its quilted coat, each great water jar keeps its small warm fire beneath it, and its water dark and still. And in the tea room, on its black lacquer tray, the emperor's white cup has been rinsed and dried and set upside down, ready for another day, with a single dried plum blossom left beside it.

You are sitting on the step of the terrace now, just outside the main door of the hall, wrapped in a quilt of your own. It is heavy and soft, padded with cotton, and it smells faintly of cedar. Under you, the stone step is cold, but the quilt is thick, and you are warm inside it. Your hands are folded in your lap. Your feet are tucked up. Your breath rises in the dark, slow and white, and fades.

Behind you, through the padded curtain, you can feel the faint warmth of the hall, coming through the gap at the edge, like the warmth from a sleeping animal.

Think back, for a moment, to the little letter in the dark room at the end of the hall. A man, fourteen centuries ago, looked out of his window after a storm and wrote a few lines to a friend. A brief snowfall, and now the weather is clearing. It is lovely. I hope you are well.

That is exactly the kind of night this has been. A brief snowfall. And now the sky is clearing. The emperor kept that letter close to his desk for most of his life, and tonight, of all nights, it fits.

It is lovely. And everyone is well.

After a while, slowly, keeping the quilt around your shoulders, you lift the padded curtain and step back inside into the warmth. The curtain closes without a sound. The hall is dark and soft and still. The floor of the passage is warm under stockinged feet, every step, warm tile after warm tile, like a beach in the evening after a long summer day.

At the end of the passage there is a small room, and in it a low kang, and on the kang a quilt, folded back, waiting for you. You climb up onto it and lie down. The brick beneath the felt is warm, gently and evenly warm, and the warmth comes up into your back and shoulders and tired legs, soaking slowly in, the way the warmth of the hand warmer soaked into Deyu's cold fingers in the afternoon. The quilt comes up to your chin. It is heavy and soft. The pillow under your head is firm and cool and smells faintly of dried tea leaves.

Above you, the painted beams are lost in the dark. You do not count them.

In the passage outside the bedchamber, the gray old eunuch from the outer rooms sits on his square of felt, keeping the last of the watch. His eyes are half closed, but he is awake. The candle in the horn lantern above him is a short stub now, burning very low and very steady. It will last until the morning. Behind the door, there is only silence, the silence of a deep and peaceful sleep.

In the courtyard, the snow has frozen a little on its surface, so that it glitters very faintly in the starlight, like sugar on a cake. The ridge of snow along the balustrade, where Yulan gathered the snow for the tea, still shows the small, neat marks of her scoop, a row of little hollows, each one now edged with frost. Nobody will smooth them away. By the afternoon, the sun will have softened them, and they will be gone.

The paper windows of the hall are dark now, all but one, at the end of the passage, where the stub of a candle glows behind them like a single warm coal.

In the little room on the far side of the hall, Yulan sleeps. She lies on her side, with her hand under her cheek, her dark hair spread on the pillow. Her face, in sleep, looks younger than twenty-four. Perhaps she is dreaming of home, of her mother's plum blossom chart on the wall by the stove, and of her father telling the weather by the petals. In a few months, she will be there. But not tonight. Tonight she is here, warm, asleep.

Beside her, Xiuzhu sleeps curled toward the warm wall, her knees drawn up, the blue quilt over her ear. When she wakes, there will be snow in the courtyard, and she will remember, and she will go to the chart on the wall and paint the very center of today's petal. But that is later. For now she sleeps.

In the small room at the end of the hall, Deyu sleeps on the narrow kang, his cheek on his folded arm, the little brass warmer in the crook of his elbow. Its coal has gone out now, but the brass has kept a little of the warmth, and the kang beneath him has kept the rest. He has not moved in two hours. He is very deeply asleep.

Beside him, Liu Changshou sleeps on his back, his hands folded on his chest, the quilt drawn up to his chin. His face is calm. The lines in it have smoothed a little. He has kept the door all night, and now someone else is keeping it, and for a few hours, he does not have to listen for anything at all.

In the servants' room at the back of the hall, Zhao Fu sleeps on the rough kang under the reed mat, still in his padded coat, his gray hands under his chin. The little stove in the corner has gone dark. But the kang is warm beneath him, the warmth he made.

At the gate, a new guard stands in the snow, wrapped in fur, very still. Fulu is gone. A little while ago, his relief came across the courtyard, and Fulu walked slowly away toward the outer gate, his boots crunching softly in the snow. He lifted one hand as he went, to the gate, to the hall, to the night. Now, in the guardroom by the outer gate, he is asleep on a long warm kang among the other guards, under a quilt that smells of horses and wood smoke. He is dreaming, perhaps, of the frozen lake, and the long spiral of skaters turning slowly in the sun, and the hiss of hundreds of blades on the ice.

And in the rear hall, behind the silk curtains, under the apricot quilt and the yellow one, on the warm brick bed, the emperor sleeps.

The whole palace sleeps around them. All its thousand roofs, white with snow. All its long red walls. All its quiet courtyards and closed gates. Out on the moat, the ice cutters have gone home, and the new-cut squares of black water are already skinning over with fresh ice. In the city beyond the walls, a million people lie asleep on their own warm kangs, under their own low roofs, with the snow on top.

There is nothing left to do. There is nowhere you need to be.

So let your breathing slow. In, and out. Let the quilt be heavy and warm around your shoulders. Let your hands lie loose in your lap. Let your eyes close, if they want to. You are safe here. You are warm. You can stay as long as you like.

The stars shine. The snow lies still. The hall sleeps.

Far off to the north, very faint, the great drum sounds once more from its tower, slow and deep, the last drum of the night. You hear it the way you might hear a sound in a dream, from very far away. Then it is quiet again. Nobody in the hall stirs. They have heard that drum all their lives, and in their sleep, it only tells them that all is well, and that there is a little time still left before the morning.

Then, very slowly, toward six o'clock, something changes. It is so slight that at first you are not sure of it. Low in the east, above the dark line of the palace roofs, the sky is no longer quite black. It is a very deep blue. And then, a little later, a blue that is almost gray, thin and cold and faint, like the color of the ice on the moat. The stars along the eastern edge of the sky grow dimmer, one by one, and fade. The rest still shine.

It is not light yet. It is only a little less dark, a gentle softening at the very edge of the world.

In a little while, the hall will begin to wake. This emperor was always an early riser. Soon there will be soft footsteps in the passage, and a lamp lit, and water warmed, and the low voices of the eunuchs of the morning. Soon Zhao Fu will wake, and stretch his stiff back, and pull on his boots, and go out into the cold to lift the boards and feed the fire for a new day. Soon the kitchen will send its steaming boxes along the covered passage. Soon Xiuzhu will paint her petal.

But not yet. Not yet.

For now, everyone is still asleep.

Yulan is asleep, with her hand under her cheek, dreaming of home. Xiuzhu is asleep, curled toward the warm wall, dreaming of spring. Deyu is asleep with his little brass warmer, and Liu Changshou is asleep beside him, his hands folded, his long watch done. Zhao Fu is asleep in his padded coat on the kang he warmed himself. Fulu is asleep in the guardroom, dreaming of the ice. And the emperor is asleep, in the Hall for Nourishing the Heart, behind his silk curtains, under the snow.

The palace is quiet. The palace is warm. The night has passed safely, every hour of it.

The ice is cut, and resting in the dark. The jars are full, and liquid, in their quilted coats. The gates are barred. The lanterns are low. The chart on the maids' wall has twenty-three red petals, and fifty-eight white ones, waiting for the days to come. The snowball sits on the balustrade. The tea cup waits on its tray. And every person in this hall, the oldest and the youngest, the one who keeps the door and the one who keeps the fire, is lying on warm brick, under a soft quilt, asleep.

And down beneath the courtyard, in the dark at the bottom of a small brick pit, the charcoal glows, deep and soft and orange, under its thin gray blanket of ash. It has burned all night, little and often, fed by patient hands. Its warmth has crept through the narrow channels under the floor, and into the hollow brick of the bed. And there, in the inner room, under the apricot quilt, the kang is still warm, gently, evenly warm, from one end to the other, just as it was when we found it yesterday afternoon, holding the sleeping emperor safe above the winter, waiting, quietly, for the morning.

If you enjoyed spending this winter night in the Forbidden City, a like or a subscription would mean a great deal, but only if you truly enjoyed it. And if there is a time or a place you would love to spend a night in, leave it in the comments, because your ideas truly help.

Next time, we will visit a very different kind of house, on a very different kind of night, somewhere far from the yellow roofs of Beijing.

Sleep well, friend. Good night.
