## Part One — Around Half Past Two in the Afternoon: The Last Errands on Upper Street

It is Christmas Eve in London, in the year 1880, and the light is already thinning. In a tall brick house in Canonbury, in the north of the city, the front parlor is empty, but a coal fire is burning in its iron grate, low and red and patient. Outside, the air is raw and damp. It smells of coal smoke, horses and frost. A brass fender catches the firelight. On the mantelpiece, a row of new Christmas cards leans against the looking-glass, and the coals settle with a soft, small sigh.

Not the Christmas of the picture books, all stagecoaches and snowdrifts and lords in velvet. Just one ordinary family, on one ordinary Christmas Eve, in one ordinary London house.

And without a few old tricks, you would not have lasted until morning, not in a room with thirty lit candles on a dry fir tree, a hot coal fire, and three excited children in wool and cotton.

So tonight I would like you to carry three small questions with you. The first is this. How did a family light a real tree with real candles, in a room full of curtains and paper, without burning the house down? The second. Why was the Christmas pudding, the great dark pudding of the day, made five whole weeks before Christmas, and then left hanging in a cloth in the kitchen? And the third. Who was still awake in this house, long after everyone else had gone to sleep?

These are not three separate questions. They are three doors into the same house, on the same night, and we will open each of them in its own time.

This family never quite existed under one name, but every detail of their night is real.

So let your head grow heavy now. Let your shoulders loosen and drop. Your eyes may close whenever they like, and the fire will keep burning without you. Let us go back, gently, to a gray December afternoon, and to a busy shopping street not far from that quiet parlor. So let us begin.

The street is called Upper Street, and it runs north through Islington like a long spine. At half past two on this Friday afternoon it is crowded from one end to the other. Omnibuses pulled by pairs of horses roll along the middle of the road, their roofs crowded with men in overcoats and bowler hats. Carts and cabs squeeze past them. On the pavements, women in dark winter coats and bonnets move from shop window to shop window, with baskets on their arms and children at their skirts.

Among them is a woman of thirty-eight in a plum-colored coat with a fur collar, walking quickly, with a list in her gloved hand. Her name is Margaret Hale. Beside her, carrying the basket, is a tall, serious girl of twelve with her hair in a long brown plait under a felt hat. This is her daughter Florence.

Margaret is the mistress of that house in Canonbury, the one with the fire in the parlor. Her husband is Edward Hale. He is forty-three, and he is the chief clerk at a firm of tea brokers in Mincing Lane, in the City, the old square mile of banks and counting houses by the river. Edward is not rich. But he is comfortable, in the way that a great many London families had become comfortable by 1880. He earns a steady salary. He rents a house with four floors and a basement. And he keeps two servants, which was, for many families of the time, the line that marked the middle class.

Those two servants are at home now. One is the cook, Sarah Nash, who is forty-one and has been with the family for six years. The other is the housemaid, Lizzie Pike, who is sixteen, and who came to London from a village in Suffolk only last spring. This is her first Christmas away from home. And with them, at home, are the two younger children, Percy, who is nine, and Alice, who is five. So that is the whole household. Seven people under one roof.

Why is Margaret out shopping on the very afternoon of Christmas Eve, rather than a week ago?

Part of the answer is simply that this was how it was done. In 1880 there was no refrigerator in any kitchen in this street, and fresh things, the fruit, the bird, the butter, the cream, had to be bought as late as they possibly could be. And part of the answer is that the shops expected it. On Christmas Eve, the shops of London stayed open late into the evening, and the grocers, poulterers and butchers did more trade on this one day than on almost any other day of the year. For many working families, Christmas Eve was also the evening when wages were paid, and so the busiest hours of all were still to come, long after dark, under the gas lamps.

So Margaret has come early, before the worst of the crowds. And her list is a long one.

The first stop is the grocer's. You can smell it before you reach the door. Inside, the long mahogany counter is heaped with things that most of the year are luxuries. There are boxes of raisins and currants, and sticks of candied peel, lemon and orange and citron, sugary and translucent. There are figs pressed flat in little round boxes, and dates in long boxes with paper lace around the edge. There are almonds and walnuts and Brazil nuts in sacks. And there are oranges, piled in a bright pyramid, which in a London December were one of the true signs that Christmas had come.

Here is something that may surprise you. Many of the fruits on this counter had come a very long way, and very fast. Raisins and currants came by steamship from the Mediterranean, the currants from Greece, where they had been grown for centuries, the very word "currant" coming from the old name of the Greek city of Corinth. Oranges came from Spain and Portugal. Tea came from China and India and Ceylon, and passed, as it happens, through Mincing Lane, through offices exactly like the one where Edward works. A middle-class London Christmas of 1880 was, in its kitchen cupboards, a gathering of half the world.

Margaret buys a pound of good tea, two dozen oranges, a box of figs, and a bag of nuts for the children's stockings. The grocer's boy will deliver most of it to the house before five o'clock, because in 1880 a respectable woman in a respectable street did not usually carry heavy parcels home herself. Florence's basket holds only the small, precious things.

The second stop is a small shop that sells toys, stationery and fancy goods. There Margaret buys a box of thirty small wax tapers, white and red, each one about as long as a finger, and a little bundle of tin holders to clip them to the branches of a tree. She buys a box of Christmas crackers, with colored paper twisted at both ends. And she buys a packet of gold paper for Florence, who has promised to make little cones to hang on the tree, filled with sugared almonds.

You are standing just behind them, in the doorway of the shop. The bell above the door has just jingled. The air inside is warm and dusty and smells of paper and glue and varnish. A row of jointed wooden dolls sits along a shelf, staring with painted eyes. A tin horse on wheels stands on the counter, with real hair for its mane. There is a Noah's ark, its animals marching two by two on a shelf below. Your boots are wet at the toes from the street. Your fingers are stiff inside your gloves. Through the window behind you, you can see a man with a barrow of holly at the corner, calling out his price, and a woman holding up a sprig of mistletoe to the light, turning it, looking at the white berries, the way you might look at a jewel.

Florence is looking at the Noah's ark too. She knows something her little sister does not.

Outside again, the street is busier than before. The poulterers' shops are the most astonishing sight of all. They have hung their birds in rows outside their windows, on iron rails and hooks, from the top of the shop front almost to the pavement: geese, turkeys, chickens and ducks, and here and there a hare, all fully feathered, all labeled with little cards showing the price. A man in a long blue apron stands under them, calling to the passing women. The butcher next door has done the same, with whole sides of beef decorated with sprigs of holly and paper rosettes, and the best joints marked with prize ribbons from the cattle show.

In our own time, if we want a turkey for Christmas, we order it weeks ahead, often with a click, and it arrives wrapped in plastic, already prepared. Here, you choose your bird hanging in the open air, feathers and all. And then the poulterer plucks it for you, or sends it round to your kitchen for your cook to deal with.

Margaret ordered her turkey a fortnight ago, and it is already hanging in the cool larder at home. But she pauses at the poulterer's to pay the bill, and to put a coin into the box on his counter for the boy who carried it round.

There is one more errand, and it is the one Florence has been waiting for. At the corner of the street stands a pillar box, a tall cast-iron post box, painted red. In Margaret's gloved hand are six last Christmas cards, addressed in her neat hand to cousins and old friends. Florence is allowed to post them.

Here is a small fact that very few people know. When the first pillar boxes appeared in Britain in the 1850s, many of them were painted green, a soft, unobtrusive green, so they would blend into the street. But people complained that they could hardly find them. And so, from 1874, only six years before our afternoon, the Post Office began painting them bright red, to be seen. The box at this corner is one of the red ones, and its paint is still quite new.

And here is our first true record of the night. In the archives of the Post Office, now kept at the Postal Museum in London, there is a notice from December 1879, just one year before our evening. It is the earliest notice the museum holds that asked the public to post their Christmas letters early, to spare the overworked sorting offices. But early, in 1879, did not mean November. It did not even mean the week before Christmas. It meant posting on the morning of the twenty-fourth of December, rather than the evening. And so Margaret, posting her cards at half past two on Christmas Eve, is not being careless at all. By the standards of her own time, she is being quite thoughtful. Her cards will be delivered tomorrow morning. In 1880, in London, the post still came on Christmas Day.

The cards drop into the dark mouth of the box. Florence listens for each soft fall.

Historians do not entirely agree about how new all of this was. Some describe the Christmas that Margaret and Florence are shopping for, with its cards and crackers and trees and toy shops, as something that the Victorians more or less invented, in a few short decades, between about 1840 and 1880. A good part of it was new. Fifty years before this afternoon, in the London of the 1820s, the great winter party of the year was often held not at Christmas but on Twelfth Night, the sixth of January, with a huge iced Twelfth cake. Other historians point out that almost every element was older than it looked. Christmas boxes, carols, evergreens, plum puddings, and feasting had all existed for centuries. What the Victorians did, in this view, was gather the old pieces together, move them indoors, and put children at the center. Charles Dickens helped, of course, with *A Christmas Carol* in 1843, but he was one voice among many. Both views have truth in them.

By a quarter past three, Margaret's list is finished. The sky above the rooftops of Upper Street has turned a pale, cold pearl color. The first shopkeepers are lighting the gas jets in their windows, and the oranges in the grocer's pyramid glow suddenly brighter, as if someone had switched them on.

Mother and daughter turn down a side street, away from the noise, toward Canonbury. The houses here are taller and quieter, in long, flat-fronted terraces of brown-yellow London brick, with black iron railings in front and stone steps going up to each front door, and narrow steps going down to the basement kitchens below the pavement. Each house has a little brass knocker. Some have a holly wreath tied to it already.

Florence carries the basket in both arms now, carefully, because the box of tapers is on top. She is thinking about the tree. She has been told that this year, for the first time, being twelve, she may help her parents dress it, behind the closed parlor door, while Percy and Alice are kept out of the way upstairs. It is the greatest honor of her year so far.

Margaret is thinking about other things. She is thinking about whether the turkey will be large enough, and whether Sarah has finished the mince pies, and whether Lizzie, who cried a little this morning over a letter from her mother in Suffolk, will be all right tonight. She is thinking about the three stockings that will need filling, very late, in the dark. And she is thinking, as she has been thinking all afternoon, about the fire in the front parlor, which Lizzie lit at seven o'clock this morning, and which must burn brightly all evening, for the tree, for the children, and for the long night ahead.

As they turn into their own street, Florence sees it first. In the front parlor window of their house, through the gap in the curtains, there is a warm, steady orange glow. The fire is still burning, waiting for them.

## Part Two — Around Four O'Clock: Lamplight and the Postman's Knock

By the time Margaret and Florence climb the front steps and Lizzie opens the door to them, the sun is almost gone. Tonight, over London, it sets at four minutes to four, and the long blue dusk of a winter afternoon begins to pour into the streets like cold water.

Why does the postman knock twice?

We will come to that in a moment, because he is on his way down this street right now. Before that, there is the light.

In 1880, the streets of London were lit by gas, and they had been for most of the century. The first public gas lamps had been put up on Pall Mall in 1807, as a kind of demonstration, and within a few decades almost every street of any importance in the city was lined with them. The lamps stood on iron posts, each one topped with a glass lantern, and inside each lantern was a small burner fed by a pipe that ran underground from the gasworks. Every evening, at dusk, a man had to come and light them, one by one.

That man is coming up the street now. He is the lamplighter, and he carries a long wooden pole over his shoulder, taller than he is, with a little brass hook and a small flame at its top. He walks briskly, without stopping to look at anything. At each lamp post, he lifts the pole, hooks open the little door of the lantern, turns the tap, and touches the burner with his flame. There is a soft pop, and a blue-white light blooms in the glass. Then he is already walking on to the next post. Behind him, the street fills up with small pools of light, one after another, stretching back toward the corner, like a string of pearls being laid down in the dusk.

Inside the house, the light is gas too, at least downstairs. In the hall, the dining room and the front parlor, there are brass gas brackets on the walls, with glass shades shaped like tulips. Upstairs, in the bedrooms and the nursery, the family still uses candles and small oil lamps. Gas pipes were costly to lay, and many people believed that gas in a bedroom was unhealthy, because the burning jets used up the air and left a faint, sour smell. And down in the basement kitchen, Sarah the cook works by gaslight and by the red glow of her great iron range.

Lizzie goes from bracket to bracket in the hall with a long taper, turning each tap and lighting it, and the patterned tiles of the floor appear under her feet, red and black and cream.

Here is something about this house that you would never think to notice. Out on the pavement, just in front of the railings, there is a small round iron plate set into the paving stones, about a foot across, with a raised pattern cast into it, a little star or a ring of dots. This is the coal hole. Under it, a short chute runs down into a vault beneath the pavement itself, where the family's coal is kept. When the coalman comes, with his horse and cart, and his black leather cap with a long flap down the back to protect his neck, he lifts the iron plate and pours sack after sack of coal straight down the hole, without ever setting foot inside the house. Then Lizzie fetches it out, scuttle by scuttle, from the cellar below.

A house like this one might burn a great deal of coal in a winter. Every fire in the house, in the parlor, the dining room, the kitchen range and, on cold nights, the bedrooms, was fed from that vault. Multiply that by every house in London, and you can understand why the city's winter air smelled the way it did, sharp and sooty and sulfurous, and why on still evenings a yellow-brown haze hung over the rooftops. Tonight is cold and fairly still. You can see the smoke rising almost straight up from a thousand chimney pots, in thin gray columns, into a sky that is turning from blue to violet.

And now, the postman. You hear him before you see him. Rat-tat. Two sharp knocks on the front door, quick and firm, almost one sound.

That double knock was the London postman's signal, known to households all over the city. A visitor might knock in one way, a tradesman in another, a servant at the area door below in another again. But two brisk raps on the front door meant one thing only. The post had come. And on Christmas Eve, the post came, and came, and came again. In 1880, London had several deliveries every single day, and in the City itself as many as a dozen. A letter posted in the morning could easily be read across town in the afternoon.

Lizzie opens the door. The postman stands on the step in his dark uniform with its red piping and his tall hat, a heavy leather bag on his hip, his breath smoking. He hands her a bundle of envelopes tied with string, eleven of them, wishes her a happy Christmas, and touches his hat. He has perhaps a hundred more houses to visit before his round is done. In a day or two, on the Monday after Christmas, he will knock once more, and the Hales will give him a Christmas box of a few shillings, as nearly every household on his round will do. It was one of the ways that a London postman made his year bearable.

Most of those eleven envelopes hold Christmas cards. And this is where our first named source of the evening comes in.

In December 1843, thirty-seven years before this night, a busy and very inventive civil servant named Henry Cole found that he had too many friends to write to at Christmas. Cole was the kind of man who could never leave a problem alone. He had already helped to bring about the penny post, three years before, which let anyone send a letter anywhere in Britain for a single penny. Now he asked an artist friend, John Callcott Horsley, to design a card that could be printed, sold, and sent instead of a letter. Horsley drew a family at a table, raising glasses of wine, with two smaller panels at the sides showing people feeding and clothing the poor. Underneath, in plain, cheerful letters, were the words, "A Merry Christmas and a Happy New Year to You." About a thousand copies were printed, by most accounts, and sold for a shilling each, which was a good deal of money then. Some people were rather shocked by it, because one of the small children in the picture is shown sipping from a glass of wine.

That was the first commercial Christmas card. By 1880 there were millions. Printers across Britain and Germany were turning them out in vast numbers, with fringes of silk, embossed edges, and colored pictures of flowers, birds, children, holly, and sometimes, oddly, summer meadows and seaside scenes. They had become cheap, too. A card could be bought for a penny or two. And a card sent in an unsealed envelope could travel at a halfpenny rate, half the cost of a letter.

You are standing by the parlor mantelpiece now, with the bundle of cards in your hands, untying the string. The envelopes are cold from the street. The fire is warm against your knees and the front of your skirt or your trousers, and your back is cool, the way it always is in a room heated only by one open grate. You slit open the first envelope with your thumbnail. Inside is a stiff little card with gilded edges, and on it a painted robin, plump and bright, sitting on a snowy twig. You open the second. Another robin. You open the third. A spray of holly and a robin again.

Why so many robins? There is a pleasant story about this. In the middle of the nineteenth century, British postmen wore bright red coats, and people nicknamed them robins, or robin redbreasts. And so, the story goes, the little bird on the front of the card became a kind of joke and a compliment at once, the robin bringing the Christmas greeting just as the red-coated postman did.

Historians are not sure that story is true. Some think it is quite right, and that the red-coated postman explains the robin perfectly. Others point out that the robin had been a beloved winter bird in Britain for centuries, a bird that stayed and sang in the cold months, when most birds fell silent, and that it would have appeared on winter cards whatever the postmen wore. Both ideas may be part of it. The robin was already loved, and the postman made the joke.

In our own time, many of us send our Christmas greetings by email, or by a message on a phone, in a moment, without a stamp. Here, every card is carried by hand, by a man in a uniform, up the steps to the door, and knocked for twice.

There is a scrape of a key in the front door, and a cold breath of air through the hall, and a man's voice calling out. Edward Hale is home.

He has come up from the City by the North London Railway, which runs in a long curve around the northern edge of London, and has a station at Canonbury, only a few minutes' walk away. On most days he would not be home until six. But on Christmas Eve his firm in Mincing Lane, like many offices in the City, closed early, at three, and the partners shook hands with every clerk on his way out, and gave each one a small envelope, and wished him the compliments of the season.

Before Edward can take off his coat, the hall is full. Percy comes thundering down the stairs, nine years old and all knees and elbows, in a sailor suit that is already too short at the wrists. Behind him, holding the banister with both hands and taking each step one at a time, comes Alice, who is five, in a white pinafore over a red wool dress, her fair hair tied back with a ribbon. Percy wants to know whether the tree has come. Alice wants to know whether Father Christmas is coming tonight, and if he is, whether he will come down the parlor chimney, and if he does, whether the fire will burn his boots.

Edward laughs and lifts her up. He tells her that Father Christmas is a very sensible old gentleman, and always waits until the fires are low.

The tree has come. It was delivered this morning, by a man with a handcart, and it is standing now in the back passage near the garden door, in a wooden tub, out of sight. It is a little Norway spruce, about five feet high, with a sharp green smell that has crept into the hall. Percy has been forbidden to look at it all day, and has looked at it four times.

You are in the hall with them, pressed against the wall to make room. Edward's overcoat is cold and damp under your hand as Lizzie takes it from him, and it smells of the railway, of coal smoke and wet wool and the faint, sweet, dusty smell of tea, because Mincing Lane smells of tea all year round. His hat goes on the hall stand, on its brass hook, next to the umbrella. From his coat pocket he takes a small parcel wrapped in brown paper, and slips it, without anyone but you noticing, into the drawer of the hall table. That is for Margaret. It is a little silver brooch shaped like a sprig of holly, and it has been in his pocket since Tuesday.

Margaret sends the two younger children back upstairs to the nursery with Lizzie, where there are paper chains to be glued together, strips of red and green and gold paper with a pot of flour paste. They go reluctantly. Florence stays down. She is twelve now, and this is the evening she has been promised.

Edward comes into the front parlor at last, and stands in front of the fire, and holds his cold hands out to it, the way men did then, with his coat tails lifted a little behind him to let the heat reach the backs of his legs. The fire is deep and red, and it ticks softly as it burns. He looks at the eleven new cards that Margaret has stood along the mantelpiece, among the older ones, and reads the names on them. His sister in Bristol. Margaret's cousins in Dorchester. An old school friend in Manchester.

Margaret tells him the turkey has been paid for. He tells her the partners were generous this year. They look at each other with the quiet satisfaction of two people who have got through a great many small tasks and can see the end of them.

Then Edward puts more coal on the fire, from the brass scuttle beside the hearth, one careful shovelful at a time, so that it will burn up well for the evening. The room grows brighter. Somewhere above them, through the ceiling, comes the sound of Percy and Alice arguing gently over the paste pot.

Out in the street, the last of the lamplighter's pearls has been laid down. And here, in the parlor, the fire burns higher, and lights the faces of the cards.

## Part Three — Around Five O'Clock: Dressing the Tree Behind a Closed Door

At five o'clock, with the curtains drawn and the gas turned up, Edward carries the little spruce into the front parlor in its tub, walking backward through the door, with Florence steering the top. Margaret closes the door behind them, and turns the key in the lock. For the next hour, this room belongs to the three of them, and nobody else in the house may come in.

Where did all the shining things for the tree come from?

Most of them, as it happens, came from Germany. And that is the right place to begin, because the Christmas tree itself came from there too.

The custom of bringing an evergreen tree indoors at Christmas, and decorating it with candles, apples, sweets and little gifts, had grown up in the German lands over several centuries. By the early nineteenth century it was common in German homes of every kind. In Britain it was still a curiosity, something a few families with German connections kept up, and that most people had only heard about. The royal family had German connections in plenty. And so did the many German merchants, clerks, bakers and sugar refiners who lived in London, a sizable community in the East End and the City, who brought their customs with them.

So the tree is German. And many of the decorations that Margaret has kept, wrapped in tissue paper, in a cardboard box at the bottom of the linen cupboard, are German too. As Florence lifts the lid, the first thing she sees is glass.

There are a dozen glass ornaments in the box, thin as soap bubbles, silvered inside so that they shine like mirrors, and colored outside, deep red, pale green, gold and blue. Some are round. Some are shaped like pinecones, or walnuts, or little bunches of grapes. One is a tiny glass bird with a tail of spun glass, which clips onto a branch. They were made in a small town called Lauscha, in the forested hills of Thuringia, in the middle of Germany, where glassblowers had been working since the 1590s. By 1880 the families of Lauscha were blowing these ornaments in their own cottages, at small gas flames on their kitchen tables, father and sons blowing, mother and daughters silvering and painting, and they were shipped from there all over Europe and across the Atlantic. Around this time, an American shopkeeper named Frank Woolworth began buying them for his stores, and he would one day sell them by the millions.

There is tinsel in the box, too. And here is something unexpected about it. The first tinsel, made in Nuremberg in the 1600s, was real silver, beaten and cut into thin, glittering strands. It was beautiful. But silver tarnishes, and on a tree near candle smoke and a coal fire it turned dark within days. If you look closely at the strands in Margaret's box, you will see they are not silver at all. By 1880, most tinsel had been made of cheaper metals for a long time, of tin and lead and copper, cut into thin threads and twisted. It still glitters. It is just a little heavy, and it sags the branches into soft curves.

Now for our source. On the twenty-third of December, 1848, thirty-two years before this evening, the *Illustrated London News*, the most popular picture paper in the country, published a large engraving across its Christmas supplement. It showed Queen Victoria and Prince Albert, standing with their children around a decorated tree at Windsor Castle. The tree stands on a table. It is covered with candles and little ornaments, and there are toys and gifts laid out on the cloth beneath it. And the paper described it in loving detail. "The tree employed for this festive purpose," it said, "is a young fir about eight feet high, and has six tiers of branches. On each tier, or branch, are arranged a dozen wax tapers."

Six tiers, and a dozen tapers on each. If you count them up, you will find seventy-two candles on one tree, all lit at once. The paper went on to describe the baskets, trays and little boxes hanging from the branches, filled with expensive sweets, and the figure of an angel at the very top with its wings outstretched.

Families all over Britain saw that picture. If you had been a child in 1848, you might have pored over it by the fire for an hour. And here is the surprising part. Two years later, in 1850, an American women's magazine called *Godey's Lady's Book* printed the same engraving for its own readers. But someone had quietly changed it. The queen's crown was gone. Prince Albert's mustache and royal sash were gone. The little royal family at Windsor had become, with a few strokes of the engraver's tool, an ordinary well-to-do American family around an ordinary tree. And in that disguise it helped spread the tree across the United States as well.

Historians do argue about how much credit Prince Albert really deserves. He is often said to have brought the Christmas tree to Britain. But he did not quite do that. Queen Charlotte, the German wife of King George the Third, set up a tree at Windsor for a children's party at Christmas in 1800, forty years before Albert married Victoria. And Victoria herself had Christmas trees as a girl, long before she met him. What Albert and Victoria did, most historians agree, was make the tree fashionable. They made it the thing that a respectable family wanted. And it was the picture in 1848, more than any royal decree, that did the work.

Now, the room itself. You are inside, with the door locked behind you. The tree stands on a small table between the two front windows, as far from the fireplace as the room will allow. Its tub has been wrapped in green baize cloth and weighted with three bricks, hidden under a layer of moss, so that it cannot tip over. The spruce smells sharply of resin, and as Edward settles it, a few dry needles fall and patter on the tablecloth. The fire is warm behind you. The gas hisses gently in its brackets. And your fingers are a little sticky already, from the pine and from the sugared almonds that Florence is spooning into her gold paper cones.

You lift a glass bird out of its tissue paper. It weighs almost nothing. You can see your own face in it, tiny and curved and golden. You clip it onto a branch near the top, and it trembles there, and catches the gaslight. Below it, Margaret is hanging little red apples by loops of thread. Edward is winding the tinsel in loose spirals from the top downward. Florence hangs her cones, one by one, each with a ribbon handle. Then come the sugar mice, white and pink, with string tails. Then a handful of gilded walnuts, which Margaret painted with gold size and gold leaf on Wednesday evening, and which are still a little tacky. Then the small gifts, wrapped in colored paper and tied with thread, one for each person in the house.

Somewhere above your head, through the ceiling, you can hear the nursery. A chair scrapes. Alice is singing something to herself, tunelessly, over and over. Percy is explaining to her, in a loud and important voice, that the paper chain must go red, green, gold, red, green, gold, and never any other way. You smile, and so does Margaret, and she goes on hanging her apples.

You pick up one of the sugar mice. It is surprisingly heavy, a solid little lump of white sugar, molded and dried hard, with two pink dots for eyes and a length of real string for a tail. You hang it by its tail, so that it dangles upside down, and turns slowly. It will not be there long. Sugar mice are the first things children reach for, and by Twelfth Night there will be nothing left on this tree but glass and tinsel and a few apples.

Before the tree came to Britain, many English homes had a different centerpiece at Christmas, and some still had it in 1880. It was called a kissing bough, or a kissing bunch. It was a kind of hanging globe, made of two hoops of wood or wire crossed through each other, and bound all over with holly, ivy, rosemary and other evergreens. It was hung from a hook in the ceiling, in the middle of the main room, and decorated with red apples, ribbons, and small candles, with a sprig of mistletoe tied at the bottom. Anyone who stood beneath it could be kissed. In some country homes, the kissing bough was still the heart of the Christmas decorations long after the tree had reached the towns. Margaret remembers one from her grandmother's farmhouse in Dorset, hanging from a blackened beam, its little candles lit for a few minutes on Christmas night, and the smell of hot wax and rosemary.

The Hales have kept a little of that older custom too. Over the parlor door hangs a single bunch of mistletoe, tied with red ribbon, which Edward bought from a barrow yesterday. A good deal of the mistletoe sold in London came from the old orchards of Herefordshire and Worcestershire, and a good deal more came over by boat from France, from the apple country of Normandy and Brittany. Nobody in the house knows which this bunch is. Florence has already made up her mind not to stand under it.

The evergreens are everywhere else, as well. Holly runs along the top of the picture frames, its glossy leaves tucked behind the gilded wood. Ivy trails along the mantelpiece among the cards. A sprig of holly is stuck into the top of the clock on the mantel, and another into the frame of the looking-glass. Most families put their evergreens up on Christmas Eve, not before, and took them down again by Twelfth Night, the sixth of January. There was an old belief, in some parts of England, that it was unlucky to bring holly into the house any earlier. Margaret does not really believe it. But she waited until this morning anyway.

In our own time, many trees in many homes are made of plastic, and come out of a box in the attic, already shaped, year after year. Here, the tree was alive in a wood in Surrey or Kent only a few days ago. It will last perhaps a fortnight indoors before it begins to drop its needles in earnest. And the glass ornaments, so carefully wrapped each January, are the oldest things on it.

You stand back for a moment, beside the fire, and let your eyes travel over the whole tree. It is not a grand tree. It is a little lopsided, a little thin on the left side, where it grew too close to its neighbors in the wood. One branch near the bottom has been tied up with green thread, because it would droop. But every gap has been filled with something, an apple, a mouse, a gold cone, and the glass catches the gaslight, and the tinsel moves faintly in the warm air rising from the fire. You think that it is very much a family's tree, made of the things a family has kept, and made, and saved for.

Last of all come the tapers. There are thirty of them, white and red, each in its little tin holder. And this is the part of the evening that Edward does himself, slowly, while Margaret and Florence watch.

He does not put a single candle where it pleases him. He chooses each place. Every candle goes near the tip of a branch, not close to the trunk, where it would be crowded by twigs. Every candle is set upright, with its flame well clear of the branch above it. If there is a branch above, he moves the candle along, or bends the twig gently away. He clips each holder firmly, and then tests it with a finger. Some of the holders are a newer kind, with a small weight hanging below them on a little wire, like a tiny plumb line, so that the candle stays upright even when the branch droops. Edward is particularly fond of those.

Florence notices that he leaves the top third of the tree with only a few candles, and asks him why. He tells her that a flame low on the tree heats the branches above it, and that a crowded top is where trouble starts. He does not say anything more. But she understands, and she remembers it.

Finally, at the very top, Margaret ties a small wax angel, with gauze wings and a gold paper crown. She was given it as a girl, in Dorset, more than twenty-five years ago, and it has been to the top of every Christmas tree she has ever had.

It is a quarter to six. You step back with the three of them, to the warmth of the fire, and you look. The tree is not lit yet. That will come later, and only for a little while. But even unlit, with the gaslight in the glass and the tinsel and the gold, it is very beautiful.

Florence is quiet for a long time. Then she says that it looks like the picture in the old newspaper that her grandmother kept. And Margaret tells her that it does, a little, though the queen's tree was a good deal larger, and had a great many more servants to dress it.

Edward fetches a tin pail from the scullery, filled to the brim with water, and stands it on the floor beside the table, half hidden by the tablecloth. Beside it he lays a sponge tied to the end of a long cane. Florence looks at it, and then at her father, and so do you. He tells her that it is simply what a sensible man does, and that she must remember it when she has a tree of her own.

Then he goes to the fireplace, and with the poker he gently breaks up the top of the fire, so that it will burn clear and bright for the next hour, with no spitting, no sudden flames, and no stray sparks. The coals glow, deep and steady. The parlor is ready.

## Part Four — Around Six O'Clock: Thirty Small Flames

The clock on the parlor mantel strikes six, with a thin silvery chime, and upstairs in the nursery three heads lift at once. Lizzie has been told what to do. She brings the children down to the hall, and lines them up outside the closed parlor door, and there they wait.

Why did the children have to wait outside a closed door at all?

Because the whole point was the surprise. In the German custom that the British had borrowed, the tree was dressed in secret, and then, when everything was ready, the doors of the room were opened all at once, and the children saw it for the first time complete, blazing with light, as if it had appeared by magic. It was never meant to be watched slowly growing over a week, as many of our trees are now. It was meant to burst upon the eyes in one single moment, and that moment was the heart of the whole evening.

So the children wait. Percy shifts from foot to foot. Alice holds Lizzie's hand very tightly and stares at the keyhole, as though she might see through it. Florence is not with them. She is on the other side of the door, inside, helping. For the first time in her life, she is one of the people who make the magic, rather than one of the people who wait for it.

You are out in the hall with them, waiting too. The hall is cooler than the parlor, and the tiles are cold through the soles of your shoes. The gas in its glass tulip above you has been turned low, on purpose, so that the brightness, when it comes, will seem brighter still. You can smell the tree even through the closed door, green and sharp. You can hear small sounds from inside the room: a footstep on the carpet, the scratch of a match, a murmur from Margaret, and once, a soft laugh from Florence, quickly hushed. Under the door, a thin line of light changes color as you watch, from the white of gas to something warmer and more golden, and it begins, very slightly, to flicker.

Percy has noticed it too. He crouches down to look at the crack under the door, and Lizzie, without a word, lifts him up again by the back of his collar. Alice asks, in a whisper, whether it is Father Christmas in there. Lizzie whispers back that it is not, not yet, that he comes much later, when everyone is asleep. You remember that answer. It will matter later on tonight.

Down the passage, at the top of the kitchen stairs, Sarah the cook has come up to listen too, wiping her hands on her apron, because even a cook who has seen twenty Christmas trees in other people's houses likes to be there when the door opens.

And inside the parlor, the candles are being lit.

Edward does this himself, with a long wax taper, lit from the fire. And he does it in a particular order, which he learned from his own father. He lights the candles from the top of the tree downward. That way, he is never reaching his arm or his sleeve over a flame that is already burning. The topmost candles first, near the angel, then the next tier, then the next, working round the tree and down, one small flame after another, each one wobbling and then steadying in the still air.

Margaret stands a step behind him with her hands folded, watching the branches above each new flame. Florence stands beside the pail of water. She has been given the sponge on the long cane to hold, and she holds it with great seriousness, like a soldier with a musket.

It takes Edward perhaps three minutes to light all thirty. Then he blows out his taper, drops it into the fire, and turns down the gas.

The room changes completely. The gas jets sink to tiny blue beads in their glass tulips. And now the only light in the parlor is the low red glow of the fire, and the tree. Thirty little flames stand up among the dark branches, each one a soft gold point, and every glass bird and silver ball and twist of tinsel catches them, and multiplies them, until the whole tree seems to be shimmering from the inside.

Margaret opens the door.

And here, at last, is the answer to our first question. How did a family light a real tree with real candles, in a room full of curtains and paper, without burning the house down? The answer is that careful families never trusted it for a moment. The candles were lit only for a short time, usually a quarter of an hour or less, and never once left alone. A grown-up always stood by the tree, watching the flames. The tree stood in a heavy tub, weighted so it could not fall, and as far from the fire and the curtains as the room allowed. The candles went at the tips of the branches, with clear air above them. They were lit from the top down and put out from the bottom up, so that no arm ever reached across a flame. A pail of water stood ready on the floor, and a damp sponge on a long stick, to put out any twig that caught. Children were kept back, and skirts kept away. And when the candles had been admired, they were put out, every one, before anyone sat down to tea. In homes that kept those rules, the tree was a few minutes of wonder, carefully guarded. In homes that did not, it could be a disaster, and the newspapers of the time reported tree fires, often at children's parties.

The children come in.

Alice stops dead on the threshold. Her mouth opens, and nothing at all comes out of it. Percy, behind her, says a single word, which is "Oh," very long and low. Lizzie, behind both of them, puts her hand over her mouth. She has never seen a tree lit like this before. In her village in Suffolk, Christmas meant holly on the dresser and a sprig of mistletoe and a good dinner if the year was kind, but no tree.

Now, the Hales' tree stands on a table, as many trees did, because a five-foot spruce on a table is the right height to look a grown-up in the eye. And that, too, was an old custom. And our source for it is a young girl's diary, written nearly fifty years earlier.

On the twenty-fourth of December, 1832, a thirteen-year-old princess sat down in Kensington Palace to write in her journal about her Christmas Eve. She was Princess Victoria, and five years later she would be queen. That evening, she wrote, after dinner, she and her mother's household went into the drawing room, where there were "two large round tables on which were placed two trees hung with lights and sugar ornaments," with all the presents placed around them. That is one of the earliest descriptions we have of Christmas trees in an English house, and it is in her own hand.

Eighteen years later, in 1850, she was writing about her own children. That Christmas Eve, at Windsor, she wrote, "The 7 children were then taken to their tree, jumping & shouting with joy over their toys & other presents." Seven children, jumping and shouting. It is one of the most ordinary sentences she ever wrote, and it could be about any family in this street.

You are standing in the doorway now, just behind Percy. The air of the room is different from the hall. It is warm, and it smells of hot wax, of resin, of oranges, and very faintly of sugar. The candle flames make a tiny sound, too soft to hear at first, a kind of breathing. The branches nearest the flames give off a sharp, green, piney smell as they warm. You can feel the heat of the little tree on your cheek when you step close, a different warmth from the fire, lighter and more scattered, like standing near a crowd of people. And everywhere you look, there are reflections, in the glass balls, in the brass fender, in the looking-glass over the mantel, where a second, smaller tree burns in the dim silver, and behind it, a second fire.

Alice has found her sugar mouse. It is hanging at exactly the height of her nose. She does not touch it. She simply looks at it, upside down on its string, as if it were the most wonderful thing in the world, which tonight it is.

In our own time, the lights on our trees are electric, and we can leave them glowing all evening, and sometimes all night, without a thought. That change was very close in 1880. Only two years after this night, in December 1882, in New York, a businessman named Edward Johnson, who worked with Thomas Edison, wired eighty small electric bulbs, red, white and blue, onto a tree in his own parlor, and set it slowly turning on a little motor. A newspaper reporter came to see it. But it would be many decades before electric lights were cheap enough, and London houses wired enough, for families like the Hales to use them. For now, the tree is lit with fire, and watched.

Historians do not agree on how many families in 1880 actually had a tree like this. Some writers suggest that by this date the Christmas tree had become a normal part of a middle-class London Christmas, in house after house, thanks to the royal example and the pictures in the papers. Others think it was still far from universal, more common among the comfortable and the fashionable, and among families with German friends, while many others kept to holly, ivy and mistletoe alone, and perhaps a kissing bough. The truth probably lies between them. In a street like this one, in 1880, some houses would have had trees tonight, and some would not, and nobody would have thought either choice strange.

Sarah has come to the doorway too, and she stands with her hands folded on her apron and looks at the tree with the expression of a woman who is pretending not to be impressed. Then she says, to nobody in particular, that it is a very handsome tree, very handsome indeed, and the angel is a picture, and she goes back down to her kitchen, where the mince pies are waiting to come out of the oven. You can hear her steps going down the stone stairs, slow and heavy.

You stay. You let your eyes go soft, so that the thirty flames blur into one wide golden haze. You notice how the candles are not quite still. Each one leans and sways a little, all of them together, every time someone moves in the room, and every time the door opens, as if the tree were breathing with the house. You notice, too, that Margaret never once takes her eyes off it, even when she is smiling at the children. One part of her is enjoying the evening. The other part of her is counting flames.

Edward lets the children walk all the way round the tree, slowly, holding hands, keeping one long step back from it. Then he takes down the little wrapped gifts, one by one, by their threads, and gives one to each person in the room. These are only the small presents of Christmas Eve. The proper presents will come tomorrow, after church.

Percy's is a tin whistle. He is asked, at once and very firmly, not to blow it until the morning. Alice's is a little wooden doll's chair, painted blue, for the doll she does not know she is getting. Florence's is a pencil case of polished wood with a sliding lid. And there is one for Lizzie, too. It is a pair of warm knitted gloves, gray wool, which Margaret made herself in the evenings over the past month. Lizzie turns them over and over in her hands, and her eyes fill, and she curtsies, and says thank you, madam, twice. There is a parcel for Sarah, too, which Lizzie is to carry down to the kitchen.

And then, after a quarter of an hour, which feels to the children like a single minute, Edward says that it is time.

He puts the candles out in the opposite order from the way he lit them. Bottom tier first, then upward, so that his arm passes only over candles that are already dark. He does not blow them. He pinches each wick between a licked finger and thumb, very quickly, with a tiny hiss, and a thin ribbon of gray smoke rises from each one and curls away into the warm air. Percy begs to be allowed to do one. Edward lets him do one, the lowest, with his hand guiding Percy's. It is a great privilege.

The tree goes dark, tier by tier. The room grows dim. For a moment, before Margaret turns the gas back up, the parlor is lit only as it was at the very beginning of the evening, before any of this began, by the deep, steady glow of the coal fire.

And in that glow, the glass birds still shine a little, as if they were remembering.

## Part Five — Around Half Past Six: Tea, Crackers and the Blue Flame

By half past six, the family has moved across the hall into the dining room, where the table has been laid for a Christmas Eve tea. The gas is up, the curtains are drawn, a smaller fire burns in the dining-room grate, and in the middle of the white cloth, on a china dish, sits a pile of crackers in twisted colored paper, like a heap of fat, shining sweets.

What was inside a Christmas cracker in 1880?

Not quite what you might expect, and we will open one in a few minutes to see. Before that, the tea.

On most evenings, the children would eat their tea upstairs in the nursery, at about this hour, while their parents dined later, alone, at half past seven. That was the ordinary arrangement in a house of this kind. The children ate plain food early. The grown-ups ate a proper dinner late. But Christmas Eve is not an ordinary evening, and tonight, as a great treat, the whole family sits down together at the big table, the children on cushions to raise them up, with their napkins tucked in at the neck.

It is not a grand meal. The grand meal is tomorrow. Tonight there is thin bread and butter, cut in neat triangles, and a pot of strawberry jam from the summer. There is a seed cake, flavored with caraway, and a plate of small almond biscuits. There is a jug of hot milk for the children and a pot of tea for the grown-ups, Edward's good Ceylon from Mincing Lane. And there, on a plate in the middle of the table, still warm from Sarah's oven, are the first mince pies of the year.

A mince pie in 1880 was a small, round, covered pie of rich short pastry, filled with mincemeat. And mincemeat, in a good many English kitchens, was exactly what it had always been, a mixture of chopped dried fruit, apples, sugar, spices, suet and spirits, made weeks before and packed into stone jars to ripen. In older recipes, and in some households still, real minced beef or tongue went into it as well. Sarah's mincemeat has none, only beef suet, finely shredded, which gives the filling its richness and helps it keep.

You take one, because it would be rude not to. The pastry is crisp at the edges and soft underneath, and it breaks against your teeth in small buttery flakes, with a dusting of sugar on top that sticks to your lip. Inside, the filling is dark and sticky and very hot, and you have to breathe in quickly through your mouth to cool it. You taste raisins and currants, apple, lemon peel, nutmeg and cinnamon, and, somewhere underneath, the warm bite of brandy. It is not a light thing to eat. One is filling. Two would be a mistake. You can feel the warmth of it settle in your middle, like a small banked fire of its own.

There was a little saying that went with them. If you ate a mince pie on each of the twelve days of Christmas, in twelve different houses, you would have twelve happy months in the coming year. Percy has heard this, and has worked out that he will need a great many invitations. Edward tells him that he will settle for twelve pies in one house, and Margaret tells them both that one is quite enough for tonight.

Then come the crackers.

The man usually given credit for the Christmas cracker was a London sweet maker named Tom Smith. He had a shop in Goswell Road, not two miles from this dining room. And the story, as his firm told it ever after, goes like this. In 1840, on a visit to Paris, Tom Smith saw French sweet sellers selling sugared almonds wrapped in twists of colored paper, called bonbons. He began selling them in London, and they did well at Christmas, but poorly the rest of the year. So he tried putting a small slip of paper inside each twist, printed with a little love verse, to make the sweets popular as gifts between sweethearts. And then, the story says, sometime later, sitting by his own fire, he heard a log give a sharp crack in the flames, and he thought, what if the wrapper itself could go bang when it was pulled? He worked for some time to find a safe way to do it, with a narrow strip of card treated with a tiny amount of chemical that crackled with friction. The sweets inside grew less important. The bang became the point. And the people who bought them began calling them crackers.

By 1880 Tom Smith himself had been dead for about eleven years, but his family firm was making crackers by the hundreds of thousands, in their works on the edge of the City, near Finsbury Square, and so were several rival makers. And the crackers had changed. Here is the surprising detail. The cracker on the Hales' table tonight almost certainly holds no paper hat. The colored paper crown, which so many families now wear at the Christmas table, came a little later. According to the firm's own story, it was added by Tom's son, Walter Smith, as one of the ways he made his crackers stand out from his rivals. What a cracker of 1880 did hold was a motto, sometimes a riddle, sometimes a little love verse, and a small surprise, a tiny toy or trinket, a paper fan, a glass ring, a miniature tin whistle, a little charm.

Historians are cautious about the Tom Smith story. It is a lovely story. But most of it comes from the firm itself, told and retold over the years in its own advertising and its own histories, and few documents from the 1840s survive to prove the details. Other confectioners in London and in Paris were selling paper-wrapped sweets with mottoes around the same time, and it may be that the cracker grew up among several makers at once, with Tom Smith simply being the best at it, and the best at telling his story. Either way, by 1880, few people at a London Christmas table needed the story explained. They simply pulled.

You are sitting at the table now, between Percy and Alice, with a cracker held out in your hand. Percy has hold of the other end. The paper is shiny and slightly crinkled, red with a band of gold, and it rustles in your fingers. You can feel something small and hard inside it, sliding back and forth. Percy pulls. You pull. For a moment nothing happens, and then there is a sharp, tearing crack, much louder than you were ready for, and a tiny puff of smell, like a struck match, and the cracker comes apart in two ragged pieces, and something drops onto the tablecloth and rolls toward the jam. It is a little tin whistle, no bigger than your little finger. Percy now has two whistles, and he is not allowed to blow either of them.

Alice will not pull hers. She is frightened of the bang. So Edward and Florence pull it for her, over her head, while she covers her ears, and inside is a tiny glass ring, blue, which she puts on her thumb because it fits no other finger. Florence reads out the motto from her own cracker, which is a riddle. Why is a kiss like a rumor? Because it goes from mouth to mouth. Edward laughs. Margaret pretends not to have heard.

In our own time, crackers are pulled mostly at Christmas dinner, on the day itself, and they hold a paper hat, a joke and a small plastic toy. Here they are part of the Christmas Eve tea, and every one is a little mystery, with no hat at all.

And now, the last thing before the children's bedtime. The thing Percy has been waiting for all day.

Edward stands. Lizzie, who has been waiting by the sideboard, turns the gas down low, lower than it was in the parlor, until the room is almost dark. Only the dining-room fire is left, a dim red glow at one end of the room. Then Sarah comes up from the kitchen, carrying, with both hands, a wide, shallow china dish, and sets it in the middle of the table. In the dish is a handful of raisins, big plump ones, and a pool of warmed brandy. Edward strikes a match, and touches it to the brandy.

With a soft whump, the dish fills with flame.

It is not an orange flame like the fire, or a golden one like the candles on the tree. It is blue. A pale, shivering, unearthly blue, dancing low over the surface of the dish, with here and there a flicker of violet. In that light, every face around the table looks strange and ghostly, blue-white, with dark hollows for eyes. Alice gasps and holds on to her mother's sleeve. And Percy, who has been waiting for this moment since the summer, takes a deep breath, pushes up his sleeve, and darts his hand into the flames, and snatches a raisin, and pops it, still burning, into his mouth, and shuts his mouth on it, which puts the flame out.

Now it is your turn. Everyone is looking at you, blue-faced in the dimness, waiting. You can feel the heat rising off the dish against the inside of your wrist, gentle at first, then sharper as you lean in. The flames are so pale you can see the raisins through them, swollen and glistening, lying in the shallow pool. You tell yourself to be quick. You dart your fingers down, pinch, and pull back, and it is over before you can think, and there is a raisin in your hand with a tiny blue flame still clinging to it, like a drop of light. You put it in your mouth and close your lips, and the flame goes out at once, and you taste hot, sweet, scorched fruit and the sharp sting of the spirit. Your fingertips tingle. That is all. Percy cheers, and is hushed.

This was snapdragon, one of the oldest Christmas games in England. It had been played for centuries, at least since the time of Shakespeare, and in the nineteenth century it was one of the great amusements of Christmas Eve. The rules were simple. The lights were put out. The brandy was set alight. And everyone tried to snatch a raisin out of the flames and eat it, without being burned. The trick, as every child who played it learned, was to be quick. A slow hand got scorched. A quick one hardly felt it. Children did burn their fingers. It was, everyone agreed, part of the fun.

Our source for this part of the evening is a book that Florence has on her shelf upstairs. In 1871, nine years before this night, an Oxford mathematics lecturer named Charles Dodgson, who wrote under the name Lewis Carroll, published the second of his books about a girl named Alice, called *Through the Looking-Glass*. In the third chapter, Alice meets a gnat, who tells her about the strange insects of Looking-Glass country. One of them is the Snap-dragon-fly. And Carroll describes it like this: "Its body is made of plum-pudding, its wings of holly-leaves, and its head is a raisin burning in brandy." Carroll could write that line for a child in 1871, and be fairly sure that the children who read it knew exactly what a raisin burning in brandy looked like, because so many of them had seen one, in a dark room, at Christmas.

Florence takes a raisin, quickly and neatly. Edward takes one. Margaret takes one, with a small shriek that she is rather ashamed of afterward. Lizzie is invited to try, and does, and burns the tip of her finger a little, and laughs, and sucks it. Alice is lifted onto her father's knee, and with his big hand wrapped around her small one, she plunges in and out again, so fast that she does not feel a thing, and finds a raisin in her palm, and stares at it, astonished.

The flames burn lower and lower as the brandy burns away. The blue shrinks to a few small tongues, then to a single flicker in the corner of the dish, and then it is gone, and the room is dark, except for the red of the fire.

Lizzie turns up the gas. Everyone blinks.

And then Margaret says the words that children in so many houses have dreaded on Christmas Eve. It is bedtime.

There is a short and hopeless protest from Percy. There is a long yawn from Alice, which rather spoils her own protest before she can make it. Florence, who is twelve, is allowed to stay up until nine tonight, and she sits very straight in her chair so that nobody will forget it.

As the family files back across the hall, toward the stairs, Edward stops at the open parlor door, and looks in. The tree is dark, its candles cold. The glass birds are dim. But the fire in the grate has burned down into a deep, glowing bed of coals, red and gold, and the whole room is still warm and lit by it, waiting. He goes in for a moment, and puts on two more lumps of coal, gently, with the tongs, so that it will last until the grown-ups come back down.

## Part Six — Around Half Past Seven: Stockings at the Bedposts

At a little after seven, a small procession climbs the stairs of the house in Canonbury. Lizzie goes first, carrying a brass candlestick, because the gas stops at the first floor. Then Alice, holding the banister. Then Percy, two steps at a time and then, when told, one. And last of all Margaret, holding her skirt up off the stair carpet with one hand, and carrying a stone hot-water bottle wrapped in flannel in the other.

Where did a Victorian child hang a Christmas stocking?

The answer, in most houses, was not on the mantelpiece at all. It was on the bed. A long woolen stocking, usually one of the child's own, or sometimes one borrowed from a parent, because a father's stocking held more, was pinned or tied to the brass rail at the foot of the bed, or hung over the bedpost. In a few homes, stockings were hung at the bedroom fireplace. But a great many children's bedrooms had no fire lit at night at all, and the foot of the bed was where Father Christmas would surely look, and where a child could reach out a toe in the dark and feel whether the stocking had grown heavy.

You are climbing with them. As you leave the warm hall behind, you feel the air change on every stair. On the first-floor landing it is cool. By the second flight it is cold, and the little flame of Lizzie's candle leans backward as she climbs, pulled by the draft that comes down the stairwell from the attic. The banister is smooth and chilly under your palm. Your shadow and everyone else's go swinging up the wall beside you, huge and wavering, and then shrink and swing away again as the candle turns the corner. Somewhere below, you can hear Florence laughing at something her father has said, and the clink of the teacups as Sarah clears the dining-room table.

The nursery of this house is on the second floor, at the back, where it is quietest. It is a plain room, with a painted iron bedstead for each child, a washstand, a chest of drawers, a rag rug, and a low cupboard full of toys and books. There is a small fireplace, with a tall wire fireguard fixed in front of it and hooked to the wall, so that no child could ever pull it away. Tonight, because it is Christmas Eve and very cold, Lizzie lit a small fire here at six, and it has taken the worst of the chill off. But it is still a cold room. Away from the hearth, your breath would almost show.

That was the ordinary state of a London bedroom in winter. Downstairs, in the rooms where the family sat, the fires kept the air comfortable. Upstairs, rooms were heated rarely, or briefly, and on a frosty night a bedroom could easily fall to forty-five degrees Fahrenheit, about seven degrees Celsius, by morning, or lower. Water in a jug on the washstand sometimes had a skin of ice on it at dawn. People did not expect to be warm in bed by warming the room. They expected to be warm in bed by warming the bed.

And so there are the bottles. Margaret's is stoneware, a heavy, round-shouldered pottery jar with a screw stopper, filled from the kettle in the kitchen, and wrapped in flannel so it will not scorch. Sarah filled two of them half an hour ago. One goes into the foot of each bed, between the sheets, where it will make a pool of warmth for small feet. In older houses, and in the country, people used a warming pan instead, a covered brass pan full of hot coals on a long handle, that a servant slid between the sheets and moved about to warm them. Some families wrapped a hot brick in an old piece of flannel. Each house had its way.

You take the second bottle from Margaret, because her hands are full, and you feel its weight at once, heavy as a small loaf of stone, and hot right through its flannel. You slide it down between the sheets at the foot of Percy's bed. The sheets are cold, almost damp to the touch, the way unwarmed linen always feels on a winter night. But already, as you draw your hand back, you can feel a patch of warmth spreading out from the bottle into the bedding, and you know that in ten minutes that small round pool will be the best place in the house.

Lizzie pours warm water from a brass can into the basin on the washstand, and the children wash their faces and hands, and, under protest, their necks. Then they are buttoned into long flannel nightgowns, Alice's white, Percy's striped, that reach down to their ankles. Alice's hair is brushed out and plaited for the night. And then both children kneel down by their beds, on the cold floorboards, with their hands together, and say their prayers aloud, while Margaret sits on the end of Alice's bed and listens.

You are sitting on the low toy cupboard by the wall, watching. The nursery smells of soap, warm flannel, coal smoke from the little fire, and the faint candle-grease smell of the brass candlestick on the chest of drawers. The floor is cold through your stockings. The fire behind its wire guard throws a crisscross of shadows onto the ceiling, and the shadows tremble when the flames move. Out of the window, over the backs of the houses, you can see a scattering of lit windows in the terrace behind, each one a little yellow square in the dark, and in some of them, you are sure, other children are kneeling, and other stockings are being hung.

Then the stockings. Percy produces his from the drawer with a great show of not caring. He is nine, and he has told his sister, twice today, that he knows perfectly well who Father Christmas really is. But he ties his stocking to the foot rail of his bed with a double knot, so that it cannot possibly fall, and then he checks it. Alice's stocking is one of her mother's, long and black and woolen, because she asked for it specially, on the grounds that Father Christmas would want plenty of room. Margaret ties it to the bedpost for her, with a piece of red ribbon.

Now, the figure that these children are waiting for was not quite the same figure that we know. In England, for centuries, there had been a character called Father Christmas, or Old Christmas, an old man who stood for the season itself, its eating and drinking and merrymaking. He appeared in folk plays and in pictures, sometimes in a long robe of green or brown, crowned with holly. But he was not, in the old days, especially a visitor of children. He did not come down chimneys. He did not fill stockings.

That figure came across the Atlantic. And our source for it is a poem. On the twenty-third of December, 1823, a newspaper in the town of Troy, in New York State, called the *Troy Sentinel*, printed an unsigned poem called "A Visit from St. Nicholas." It is usually credited to a New York scholar named Clement Clarke Moore, though even that has been argued over. It describes a small, round, merry old man who arrives on Christmas Eve in a sleigh pulled by eight tiny reindeer, comes down the chimney, and fills the children's stockings. And it contains the lines, "The stockings were hung by the chimney with care, / In hopes that St. Nicholas soon would be there." The poem was reprinted again and again, in America and then in Britain. And slowly, over the middle decades of the century, the old English Father Christmas and the American St. Nicholas, or Santa Claus, began to melt together into one person. By 1880 a great many English children would have taken it for granted that Father Christmas came in the night, came by the chimney, and came with gifts.

Historians do not all tell this story the same way. Some see it mainly as an American gift to Britain, carried by that poem and by the pictures that followed it. Others point out that in parts of Europe, and in some English homes, there were already customs of gifts left secretly for children in the night, in shoes or stockings, at Christmas or on the feast of St. Nicholas in early December, and that Britain was ready to adopt the new Father Christmas because something like him already lived in its imagination. Most would agree that by the 1880s the two had become one.

In our own time, many children leave a note, or a glass of milk and a mince pie or a cookie, for the visitor on Christmas Eve, and many homes are warm in every room, and a child might sleep in light pajamas, and never feel the cold of the floor at all. Here, a child kneels in flannel on bare boards, and hurries under the blankets.

And then, the last thing before sleep. Margaret asks Alice whether she would like to sing her hymn.

Alice knows one hymn by heart, all the way through, and it is her favorite. It begins, "Once in royal David's city / Stood a lowly cattle shed." She sings it in a small, high, careful voice, sitting up in bed with the blankets pulled up to her chin, and she gets every word right, and only goes wrong on the tune twice.

You listen from the toy cupboard, and you find that you are holding very still. Her voice is thin and wavering, and it is the only sound in the room except for the fire behind its guard. It rises on the high notes and wobbles there, and comes down again. Margaret mouths the words along with her, without any sound. Percy, in the next bed, pretends not to be listening, and then, on the last verse, cannot help himself, and joins in, a little too loudly, and a little too low.

That hymn was not very old in 1880. It had been written by a young Irish poet named Cecil Frances Alexander, and published in 1848 in a little book called *Hymns for Little Children*. And here is something few people know about it. Every hymn in that book was written to teach a particular line of the Christian creed to small children, in words they could understand. "Once in royal David's city" was written to explain the line about Christ being born of the Virgin Mary. Other hymns in the same small book, written for the same reason, became just as famous: "All things bright and beautiful," and "There is a green hill far away." The book was a great success. By 1880 it had been reprinted many dozens of times, and Alice's copy, with its worn blue cover, sits on the shelf by her bed.

Then there is the tucking in. Margaret pulls the blankets up to Alice's chin and folds the top sheet neatly over them. She kisses her forehead. Alice asks, for the last time today, whether Father Christmas will burn his boots on the parlor fire. Margaret tells her that the fire will be very low by then, and that Father Christmas is an old hand at chimneys, and that she must go straight to sleep, because he never comes to children who are awake. Alice shuts her eyes so tightly that her whole face crinkles.

Percy is reading under his blanket with the candle, or trying to, and is found out, and the candle is taken away. Lizzie places a little squat night-light, a short, thick candle standing in a saucer of water, on the washstand, where it will burn low and safely for hours, so that if either child wakes in the dark, the room will not be entirely black. The saucer of water is there in case it ever tips. Then Lizzie banks the nursery fire down behind its guard, and Margaret turns at the door, and says good night, and God bless, and happy Christmas Eve.

You are the last to leave. You stand in the doorway for a moment and look back. The night-light makes a small, still circle of gold on the washstand. The fire behind its wire guard has sunk to a red glow. Two stockings hang limp and empty at the foot of two beds, one striped, one long and black with a red ribbon. Two small shapes lie under the blankets. One of them is already breathing slowly. You pull the door almost closed, but not quite, the way a child likes it, leaving a thin bar of candlelight from the landing.

Percy, from under his blankets, says good night. Alice, with her eyes still screwed shut, says nothing, because she is being asleep as hard as she can.

On the way down the stairs, Margaret stops on the first-floor landing, where the gas begins again. From here, looking down over the banister, she can see the open door of the front parlor below, and through it, a warm, flickering, orange light on the hall tiles. The fire is still burning, steady and red, exactly as it should be. The evening is not even half over.

## Part Seven — Around Half Past Eight: Below Stairs, the Pudding in Its Cloth

At half past eight, with the children in bed and Florence curled in an armchair in the parlor with a book, Margaret goes down the stone stairs at the back of the hall, into the kitchen, to speak to Sarah about tomorrow. The stairs are narrow and steep and uncarpeted, and the air grows warmer and heavier with every step, until at the bottom it is the warmest air in the house.

Why is the kitchen under the street?

Because that is how the builders of London's terraces, for well over a century, had arranged things. A house like this one was built as a stack of rooms, one on top of the other, on a narrow plot. The family's rooms took the ground floor and the floors above. The servants' working rooms went underneath, in a basement sunk half below the level of the pavement. In front of the basement windows was a small sunken yard, called the area, open to the sky, with iron railings around the top and a steep flight of steps down from the street. That gave the kitchen a little daylight. It also gave the house a separate door, down in the area, for the coalman, the butcher's boy, the milkman, and every other tradesman, so that none of them ever had to come up the front steps. The work of the house went on below. The life of the house went on above.

The kitchen is a large, low room with a stone-flagged floor, whitewashed walls, and a long scrubbed wooden table in the middle, pale with years of scouring. Along one wall is a tall dresser, with rows of blue and white plates. And filling most of the opposite wall is the range.

The range is the heart of this floor, as the parlor fire is the heart of the one above. It is a great black iron cooking stove, built into the old fireplace, with a coal fire burning in the center behind iron bars, an oven on one side, and on the other side a boiler, a tank that heats water for the whole house, drawn off from a brass tap. Over the fire is a flat iron top, where pans and kettles stand. Lizzie blacks the whole range every morning with black-lead polish and a brush, until it shines like a beetle's back, and polishes the steel fittings with emery paper. It is one of the hardest tasks of her day. And Sarah keeps the fire in it going from six in the morning until ten at night, every day of the year.

In our own time, a kitchen is often the brightest and most open room of a home, where everyone gathers. Here, it is a world apart, below the street, and the family's feet pass over it without a thought.

You come down the last step into the warmth. It hits you like a blanket. The kitchen smells of a dozen things at once: roasting fat, scorched sugar, hot iron, soap, cloves, onions, coal, and something deep and rich and fruity, like a cake that has been soaked in brandy for a month. The gas jet over the table hisses. The range ticks and creaks as its iron expands. A kettle is muttering to itself on the hob. Copper pans hang on the wall, catching the light like a row of low suns. And on the table, under a clean cloth, are four dozen mince pies, cooling in rows.

Sarah is at the table, rolling out pastry for tomorrow's sausage rolls. She is a broad, comfortable woman of forty-one, with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, a white cap over her gray-brown hair, and flour on her forearms like a light snowfall. Lizzie is at the stone sink in the scullery next door, washing the tea things in a tin bowl of hot water from the boiler, with a lump of yellow soap.

You stand by the end of the table and watch Sarah work. She rolls the pastry with a long wooden pin, pushing it away from her in smooth, even strokes, turning it a quarter turn, rolling again. She does not measure anything. She does not look at a book. Her hands simply know. Every so often she dips her fingers into the flour bin and scatters a little across the board, and the flour hangs for a moment in the gaslight, like breath on a cold morning. You can feel the heat of the range on the back of your neck. The flagstones under your feet are warm here, near the fire, and cool again a few steps away. A cat you had not noticed is asleep on the rag rug in front of the range, curled into a perfect circle, its tail over its nose.

That cat is a working member of the household. Its name is Tom, and its job is mice. In a London basement in winter, with a larder full of food, there was always work for a cat.

Margaret goes over tomorrow's plan with her cook, as she did yesterday, and as she will again first thing in the morning. Breakfast at eight. Church at eleven, at St Mary's on Upper Street, for the family and for Lizzie, while Sarah stays behind with the bird. Dinner at two o'clock, the great dinner of the year.

The bird is hanging in the larder, a cool, stone-shelved room beside the kitchen, with a small window covered by fine wire gauze to keep out flies. It is a turkey, a fine one, about fourteen pounds, already plucked and drawn. Sarah will stuff it in the morning, with a forcemeat of breadcrumbs, suet, lemon peel and herbs at one end, and sausage meat at the other, and roast it in front of the fire, or in the oven of her range, and baste it every few minutes for two hours.

Here is a surprising thing about that turkey. A century earlier, it was said, turkeys for the London Christmas markets were walked there, in their thousands, from the farms of Norfolk and Suffolk, a journey of more than a hundred miles, driven slowly along the roads in great flocks, over several weeks, by men with long sticks. Some accounts say their feet were dipped in tar and sand to protect them on the way. By 1880, the railways had changed all that. The birds came up to London by train, packed in hampers, to the great poultry markets at Leadenhall and elsewhere, and from there to poulterers like the one on Upper Street. And many London families of modest means still preferred a goose to a turkey, because a goose was cheaper. But in comfortable houses, the turkey was steadily winning.

Sarah's ham is in the larder too, boiled yesterday and now cooling in its own liquor, waiting to be skinned, covered in breadcrumbs and glazed. There is a tongue, pressed under a weight. There is a bowl of brandy butter, pale and sweet and stiff, made this afternoon, for the pudding and the mince pies. There is a jug of cream, which came this morning, and a jar of the mincemeat, half empty now. There is a basket of Brussels sprouts and another of potatoes, and a string of onions hanging from a nail. And on the top shelf, in a row, stand the stone jars of preserves from the summer, each with its paper cover tied on with string and labeled in Margaret's hand. Raspberry, 1880. Damson, 1880. Gooseberry, 1879.

You walk along the shelves slowly, reading the labels. You are looking at a whole year of work, stored up in a cold room under the street, for this one week.

And then there is the pudding.

It hangs from a hook in the ceiling of the larder, in the coolest corner, like a great round cannonball wrapped in a cloth. The cloth is a square of strong white calico, floured on the inside, gathered tightly round the pudding and tied at the top with string. The pudding inside is almost black. It has been hanging there for five weeks.

You step into the larder, and it is cold at once, after the heat of the kitchen, and you can see your breath in the gaslight from the doorway. You reach up and touch the pudding through its cloth. It is hard and heavy and slightly damp, and when you press it with a finger it gives just a little, like a firm cheese. It smells, even through the calico, of brandy, spice and dark fruit. It turns slowly on its string, and then stops.

You step back out into the warm kitchen, and the heat closes round you again, and your cheeks prickle. You notice, now, the great iron pot standing ready at the back of the range, scrubbed and empty, with its lid beside it. That is the pot the pudding will go back into tomorrow. You notice the long wooden spoon hanging on a nail by the dresser, darker at its bowl than at its handle, stained by years of stirring. And you notice that Sarah, without stopping her rolling, glances once toward the larder door, the way you might glance at a sleeping child, to be sure it is still there.

Our source for this pudding is one that Sarah knows well, because a copy of it sits on the dresser shelf, its spine cracked, its pages spotted with grease. It is Mrs. Beeton's *Book of Household Management*, published in 1861, nineteen years before this night. Among the recipes for plum pudding in that great book is one headed simply "Christmas Plum-Pudding (Very Good)." It begins with a pound and a half of raisins, half a pound of currants, half a pound of mixed peel, three-quarters of a pound of breadcrumbs and three-quarters of a pound of suet, with eight eggs and a wineglassful of brandy. And it gives the cooking time this way: "5 or 6 hours the first time of boiling; 2 hours the day it is to be served." Mrs. Beeton also advised her readers to make five or six puddings at once, because, as she put it, "they will keep good for many weeks."

That is the heart of it. And so now we can answer our second question. Why was the Christmas pudding made five whole weeks before Christmas, and then left hanging in a cloth?

There were three good reasons, and any good cook knew them. The first is that a rich pudding like this does not spoil. It is full of sugar and suet and spirit, and it has been boiled for hours, right through, and sealed inside its floured cloth. Hung in a cool, airy larder, it will keep for weeks, and even for months. The second reason is that it improves as it waits. The fruit soaks up the brandy and the spices, the flavors mingle and deepen, and the whole pudding grows darker, richer and mellower, the longer it hangs. A pudding boiled the day before would taste raw and sharp beside it. And the third reason is the simplest. On Christmas Day, Sarah has a turkey to roast, a ham to glaze, sauces to make, vegetables to boil, and a dining room full of hungry people upstairs. The last thing she wants is a six-hour boil on top of all that. By making the pudding five weeks ahead, the long, hot, steamy work was done in the quiet of late November. Tomorrow, the pudding simply goes back into the pot for its second boil, two hours, while the turkey roasts. Then it is turned out onto a dish, crowned with a sprig of holly, doused in warm brandy, lit, and carried blazing up the stairs.

And the day it was made was not chosen at random. This year it was made on Sunday the twenty-first of November, the last Sunday before Advent. On that Sunday, in parish churches across the Church of England, the collect, the short prayer of the day, from the old Book of Common Prayer, began with the words, "Stir up, we beseech thee, O Lord, the wills of thy faithful people." The day came to be known as Stir-up Sunday. And in many homes, the idea of stirring up the wills of the faithful turned, with a smile, into stirring up the pudding. That Sunday afternoon, everyone in this house came down to the kitchen, and took a turn with the big wooden spoon in the great earthenware bowl, stirring from east to west, in honor of the wise men who came from the east, and making a silent wish. Even Edward. Even Alice, who needed both hands.

Historians are not certain how old that custom really is. Some food historians say it began in Victorian times, as a gentle joke on the words of the prayer, and spread from family to family. Others think that the strong link between Stir-up Sunday and the pudding, with the family stirring and wishing, became widespread a little later, around the start of the twentieth century, and was told backward as an old Victorian custom afterward. What is certain is that the prayer was read on that Sunday, that Victorian cooks made their puddings weeks ahead, and that, in this house at least, the two came together very neatly.

Margaret thanks Sarah for the mince pies, and admires the sausage rolls, and asks after the ham. Then she gives her the small parcel from the tree, which Lizzie carried down earlier and Sarah has politely not opened. Inside is a new shawl, soft and dark green, in fine wool. Sarah unfolds it, and holds it up to the light, and says it is far too good for the likes of her, and puts it on over her shoulders at once, flour and all, and wears it for the rest of the evening.

Lizzie comes in from the scullery, drying her red hands on her apron. Her eyes are a little pink, still. Sarah has been watching her all day. Sarah was a young housemaid once herself, in a house in Hackney, far from her own mother, and she remembers what a first Christmas in service feels like. She has made Lizzie a cup of tea with two sugars, and set a mince pie beside it, and told her that she is to sit down for ten minutes and eat it, and that this is an order.

So Lizzie sits, in the wooden chair by the range, with her tea, and her new gray gloves in her apron pocket, and the warmth of the iron against her side, and she eats her mince pie in very small bites to make it last.

Then she gets up, and fills the brass coal scuttle from the bin by the area door, and carries it carefully up the stone stairs, with both hands, to the front parlor, where she kneels on the hearthrug and feeds the fire, lump by lump, until it burns up bright and high again, and the shadows of the tree leap on the wall.

## Part Eight — Around Half Past Nine: The Waits at the Door

The clock on the parlor mantel has struck nine, and Florence has been granted, after a short and dignified appeal, one more half hour. She is sitting on the hearthrug now, at her father's feet, close to the fire, with her book open in her lap but not really reading it. Margaret is at her work table, sewing a last button onto Percy's best jacket for church. Edward is in his armchair with the evening paper.

And then, from outside, faint at first, comes music.

Who were the waits?

That is what the family calls the people outside, and it is an old, old word. Centuries before, in the Middle Ages, the waits had been the official watchmen and musicians of a town, paid by the town itself. They wore its badge and livery, they played their instruments to mark the hours of the night, and they performed on great public occasions. London had its waits, and so did York, Norwich, Bristol and many other towns. Over time, they became known especially for playing in the streets at Christmas, going from house to house in the weeks before the feast, and collecting money from the householders as they went. In 1835, a new law reorganizing the old town corporations swept most of the official waits away. But the name, and the custom, survived. By 1880, the waits meant anyone who came round at Christmas time, singing carols or playing tunes at the doors, in the hope of a few coins and a warm drink. It might be a little band of working men with a fiddle and a cornet. It might be a church choir. It might be a group of children with a lantern.

Tonight it is a choir. You can hear it more clearly now, coming down the street, four or five houses away, stopping, singing, moving on. Men's voices and boys' voices together, and under them a single wheezing instrument that might be a concertina, keeping them roughly in tune. The tune is one everybody knows. "God rest you merry, gentlemen, let nothing you dismay."

You go to the parlor window and lift the edge of the heavy curtain. The glass is icy against your fingertips, and your breath makes a small gray cloud on it, which you wipe away with the side of your hand. Out in the street, under the gas lamps, you can see the little group now, a huddle of dark shapes with one swinging point of light among them, standing at the foot of a neighbor's steps. A door is open there, and a bar of yellow light falls across the pavement onto their boots. The music comes to you muffled through the glass, softened and blurred, as if it were coming from much further away than it really is. And then it stops, and you hear a man's voice, and a laugh, and the music starts again, a little closer.

Florence is on her feet at once. Edward folds his paper. Margaret puts down her sewing. Few people in a house like this would think of letting the waits pass without hearing them properly. Lizzie, who has heard the music from the kitchen, comes running up the stairs, and Sarah, more slowly, behind her, with her new green shawl round her shoulders. Edward opens the front door.

You step out onto the top of the front steps with them. The cold is astonishing after the parlor. It grips your cheeks and your ears at once, and it gets into your nose, so that every breath stings slightly, and smells of frost and soot and horse. The sky above the rooftops is dark and clear, with a faint haze of smoke low over the chimneys, and a few hard bright stars. Your breath comes out in a white cloud and drifts away under the gas lamp. The iron railings beside the steps glitter with frost. The pavement below is shining wet and black.

The choir has stopped at the next house but one, and now it comes to yours. There are seven of them. Four men in heavy overcoats and mufflers, two boys in caps and short jackets, their faces pink with cold, and an older man at one end with the concertina, its leather bellows folding and unfolding in his gloved hands. One of the boys holds a lantern on a pole, a tin lantern with a candle inside, and its light swings over the faces as they sing. They are from the choir of a chapel two streets away, and they are collecting for the coal fund for poor families in the parish.

That small detail, the coal fund, mattered a great deal. Christmas in Victorian London was a time of real charity, as well as feasting. Churches and chapels collected money for coal, blankets and food for the poorest families of their parishes. Many charities gave out Christmas dinners, in halls and schoolrooms, by the hundred. Workhouses served their inmates roast beef and plum pudding on Christmas Day, often paid for by local subscribers, and the newspapers reported it with satisfaction. For a family like the Hales, a few shillings into a collecting tin on the doorstep was not simply politeness. It was part of what Christmas was understood to be for, a moment in the year when the comfortable were expected to remember the cold.

They sing to the Hales. First "God Rest You Merry," all the verses. Then "The First Nowell." Then "Good King Wenceslas," with one of the boys singing the page's part alone, in a high, clear, trembling voice that rings off the house fronts across the street.

Here is a curious fact about that last carol. "Good King Wenceslas" was not ancient at all. It had been written only twenty-seven years before this night, in 1853, by an English clergyman and scholar named John Mason Neale. And the tune he set it to was not a Christmas tune. It came from an old Latin song in a book of Swedish and Finnish school songs printed in 1582, and in that book, the song was about spring, about the flowers coming back and the earth turning green. Neale took that cheerful spring melody, and fitted to it his new story of a good king walking out through deep snow on the feast of Stephen, the day after Christmas. Children loved it almost at once.

And "God Rest You Merry" and "The First Nowell" had their own story. They were old carols, much older than Wenceslas, sung for generations in country parishes and on printed broadsheets sold in the streets. But by 1871, nine years before this night, two Oxford men, a clergyman named Henry Ramsden Bramley and an organist named John Stainer, had published a collection called *Christmas Carols New and Old*. Bramley edited the words. Stainer arranged the music, in clear four-part harmony that any parish choir could learn. The book was a great success, and it was reprinted again and again, and it put many of these carols into the hands of church and chapel choirs all over the country, in the very form, in many cases, in which they are still sung. Somewhere in the pocket of the choirmaster at the bottom of these steps tonight, very likely, is a worn copy.

Historians argue gently about where the carol revival really came from. Some give most of the credit to clergymen and scholars like Neale, Bramley and Stainer, who collected and printed the old carols, cleaned them up, and taught them to choirs, at a time when, they believed, the old songs were dying out. Earlier collectors had begun the work, too, in the 1820s and 1830s. Others point out that country people had never stopped singing carols at all, in their own versions, in village churches and in the street, and that the scholars were rescuing something that was still very much alive, and at the same time changing it, making it more polished and more respectable. Both are probably true. The carols on these steps tonight are old and new at the same time.

You are standing close beside Florence on the top step, and you can feel her shiver, half with cold, half with something else. She is listening to the boy who sings the page's part. He is about her own age. His cap is too big for him, and his ears are bright red, and he does not look at anyone while he sings. He simply looks up, at the gas lamp, at the stars, and sings, and the sound goes out over the street, and the whole terrace seems to be listening. You notice faces at windows across the road, and a door opening two houses down, and a woman standing in it with a shawl clutched round her.

When the last verse is done, there is a little moment of quiet. Then Edward goes down the steps, and shakes the choirmaster by the hand, and puts two shillings into the collecting tin, which is a generous sum. Margaret has sent Lizzie running down to the kitchen, and now she comes back up the area steps, below the railings, with a tray of hot elderberry wine in thick glasses and a plate of Sarah's mince pies. The men take them gratefully. The two boys get hot milk instead, with a spoonful of sugar in it, and a mince pie each, and they eat them in the street, in the lamplight, very fast.

Sarah has stayed at the top of the area steps, by the railings, wrapped in her green shawl. She is humming along with nobody, the tune of Wenceslas, under her breath. Later, she will tell Lizzie that when she was a girl, in Walthamstow, the waits used to come round with a fiddle and a serpent, a great curling wooden horn like a snake, and that you could hear them from the end of the lane.

Lizzie, too, is standing in the area below with the empty tray against her chest, looking up at the singers through the railings. In her village in Suffolk, on Christmas Eve, the boys from the chapel used to go round the farms with a lantern and sing at every door, and her father would give them a penny each, and her mother would give them a slice of cake. She has not thought about it all day, on purpose. Now she thinks about it. And instead of making her sad, as she was afraid it would, it makes her feel, oddly, a little less far from home. The same carols. The same lantern. The same cold. Only the street is different.

You see her face in the lamplight, turned up, and you see her lips moving with the words, and you see her stop, and wipe her nose on the back of her new gray glove, and smile.

The choir sings one more, as a thank you. It is the gentlest of the four, a carol for children, and the men sing it softly. Then the choirmaster touches his hat, and wishes the house a happy Christmas, and the little group moves away up the street, with the lantern swinging, and stops at the next door, and begins again, more faintly. You stand on the top step and listen until you can barely hear them, and your fingers are numb, and your nose is running a little, and you do not mind.

Edward is quiet beside you, his hands deep in his coat pockets. You glance at him and see that he is not really looking at the choir at all. He is looking down the length of the street, at the long row of lamps, and you can tell that he is somewhere else. When he was a boy, in a narrow house in Clerkenwell, much smaller than this one, his father used to take him out onto the step on Christmas Eve to hear the waits, and lift him onto his shoulders to see the lantern. His father was a watchmaker, and worked by a window all day, and his hands were always cold. Edward has not thought of those hands for years. He thinks of them now, and of the lantern, and of the sound of a fiddle a long way off in the frost.

Margaret slips her arm through his. She does not say anything. She does not need to.

Edward puts his hand on Florence's shoulder. It is time to go in.

The hall is warm, and bright, and smells of the tree. Edward closes the front door and slides the bolt, top and bottom. Florence lingers at the foot of the stairs. Then, without being asked, she says good night. She kisses her mother. She kisses her father. And she climbs the stairs with her own candle, in her own time, like a grown woman, which tonight she very nearly feels she is. On the second floor, she will look in, without a sound, at Percy and Alice, both asleep now, and see the two empty stockings at the bedposts. And she will smile in the dark, and go on to her own small room at the front of the house, and undress quickly in the cold, and lie in bed listening to the faint, faraway sound of the waits, still singing, several streets away.

Downstairs, Margaret and Edward go back into the parlor. The cold of the doorstep is still in their clothes, and they both, without speaking, go straight to the fire, and stand in front of it side by side, and hold their hands out to the heat. The coals are deep and glowing. A small blue flame plays along the top of one lump, and then another. And the warmth of it creeps slowly back into their fingers.

## Part Nine — Around Half Past Ten: Christmas Boxes and the Quiet Hour

By half past ten, the house has begun to grow quiet. The waits have long since gone. The street outside is almost silent, except for the occasional clip of a late cab horse, and the slow, measured footsteps of the policeman on his beat, passing under the window, pausing, and passing on. In the parlor, the gas is turned low, and Margaret and Edward sit together at the round table near the fire, with a small pile of envelopes between them.

Why is the day after Christmas called Boxing Day?

The answer is in those envelopes. For a very long time, in England, it had been the custom to give a small gift of money, a Christmas box, to the people who had served a household through the year. The tradesmen's boys who carried the parcels. The postman. The milkman. The dustman who took away the ashes. The lamplighter who lit the street. The sweep who cleaned the chimneys. Some of them had once carried actual boxes, of wood or clay, from door to door, to be filled with coins. The gifts were given on the first working day after Christmas, and so that day became known as Boxing Day. And in great houses, it was often the day when the servants themselves were given their boxes, and a little time off.

If you look over her shoulder, you can see the list in front of her, written in her neat hand, and she and Edward are dividing the coins into envelopes, and writing a name on each one. The postman, who knocked twice today, three shillings. The milkman, two. The butcher's boy and the grocer's boy, a shilling each. The crossing sweeper on the corner of Upper Street, who clears a path through the mud for ladies' skirts, sixpence. The dustman. The lamplighter. You can feel the weight of the coins as you hold an envelope up. Each little envelope is a small thank you for a year.

This year, the twenty-sixth of December falls on a Sunday. And so the boxes will be given out on the Monday instead, the twenty-seventh, which this year is a public holiday. And that holiday is quite new.

Here is our source. In 1871, nine years before this night, Parliament passed a short law called the Bank Holidays Act. It named four days in the year, in England, on which the banks would close, and with them most offices and many shops. One of those days was the day after Christmas, the twenty-sixth of December. A few years later, a second short law added that when that day fell on a Sunday, the holiday would be kept on the Monday after instead. That is exactly what has happened this year. Christmas Day and Good Friday were already holidays by old custom, and did not need the law. But Boxing Day, as a day off for the clerks of London and the whole working country, was made official by that act.

The man who brought the act into Parliament was a banker named Sir John Lubbock, who was also, as it happens, a naturalist, an archaeologist and a friend of Charles Darwin, and who spent much of his spare time studying ants. He believed, rather firmly, that working people needed more rest. And here is the surprising detail. The new holidays were so popular, and so closely linked with his name, that for some years ordinary people called them "St. Lubbock's Days," as if he were a kind of saint of the holiday. Edward, who will have Monday off as well as tomorrow, has a great fondness for Sir John Lubbock.

There is one more envelope, set apart from the others, and it is not a Christmas box. Christmas Day was one of the four quarter days of the English year, the days on which rents fell due and servants' wages were often paid. Tomorrow, in the morning, Margaret will give Sarah and Lizzie each a quarter's wages. Lizzie's is a few pounds. It is more money than she has ever held at one time in her life. She will send a good part of it home to Suffolk, in a letter, on Monday.

Historians who study servants' lives describe their Christmases in very different ways. Some point to the warmth of many households at this season, the gifts, the extra food, the shared customs, the way servants and family sometimes gathered round the same tree. Others point out that Christmas was also the hardest week of the year below stairs, with the most cooking, the most cleaning and the longest hours, and that for a servant, the family's holiday was very often her own busiest day. Both pictures appear in the letters and memories that servants left behind. A great deal depended on the house.

In this house, at least, the kitchen has gone quiet. At about a quarter past ten, Sarah banked down the range, raking the small coals together at the back, closing the dampers, so that it would smolder slowly all night and be easy to draw up into a blaze in the morning. She checked the larder door. She put the cat out into the area, and then, when it complained, let it in again. And then she and Lizzie came up the back stairs, past the parlor door, each with a candle, and wished the master and mistress good night, and a happy Christmas, and climbed on up through the house, past the first floor, past the second, to the attic at the very top, under the roof.

You go up with them. You follow the two small candle flames up and up the narrowing stairs, which grow steeper and plainer as they climb, the carpet giving way to bare painted boards, the walls to plain distemper. The air grows colder with every flight. At the top, under the slope of the roof, there is a small landing and two doors. Behind one is the box room, full of trunks. Behind the other is the room that Sarah and Lizzie share.

It is a small room with a sloping ceiling and a little window in the roof, through which you can see, if you stand on tiptoe, the chimney pots and the stars. There are two narrow iron beds, a washstand with a jug and basin, a chest of drawers, a chair, and two pegs on the back of the door. There is a little fireplace, but no fire in it. On the wall, Lizzie has pinned a colored picture of the Queen, cut from a magazine, and a Christmas card from her mother, with a robin on it. The room is very cold. Your breath shows faintly in the candlelight. Sarah's stone hot-water bottle is already in her bed, filled from the kitchen kettle before she came up, and so is Lizzie's.

They undress quickly, with their backs to each other, out of long habit, and pull on their nightgowns, and climb into bed, and draw the blankets right up. Sarah says her prayers in a low murmur, lying down, because her knees are not what they were. Lizzie says hers kneeling, on the cold boards, quickly, and then leaps into bed and curls round her hot-water bottle like a cat. Sarah blows out the candle. And in the dark, under the roof, Lizzie can hear the wind going softly round the chimney stacks, and somewhere very far away, a church clock striking the half hour.

Downstairs, in the parlor, the envelopes are finished, and stacked in a neat pile on the corner of the table. Edward leans back in his chair and stretches his legs out toward the fire.

For a while, neither of them speaks. Margaret has taken up her sewing again, though there is nothing left to sew, and simply holds it in her lap. Edward watches the fire. They are thinking, as married people often do on Christmas Eve, about other Christmas Eves. Their first, in two rented rooms in Holloway, fourteen years ago, with a fire that smoked and a goose so small they laughed at it. The Christmas when Florence was a baby, and slept through the whole of it in a basket by the hearth. The Christmas when Percy, aged four, ate a whole sugar mouse in one go and was sick on the stairs. Margaret says something about that, and Edward laughs, softly, so as not to wake anyone, and the sound of it is warm in the quiet room.

You sit and listen to them, and watch the firelight on their faces, and you feel how comfortable this silence is, the silence of two people who do not need to fill it.

He tells Margaret that this is the hour, on Christmas Eve, when his father used to tell ghost stories. It was a thing every family did, he says, or every family he knew as a boy. You sat round the fire, late, with the lamps turned down, and the grown-ups told each other tales of headless horsemen and gray ladies and phantom coaches, until everyone was too frightened to go up to bed.

He was quite right that it was a custom. Eleven years after this night, in 1891, the humorist Jerome K. Jerome would begin a little book of comic ghost tales called *Told After Supper* with exactly that thought. "Whenever five or six English-speaking people meet round a fire on Christmas Eve," he wrote, "they start telling each other ghost stories." It was such a well-known habit that he could make fun of it, and expect everyone to laugh.

Margaret tells Edward that if he tells her a ghost story tonight, she will make him go up and check on the children alone. Edward says that his father's best story was about the ghost of a lost umbrella that haunted the cloakroom of a railway station, and Margaret laughs, and says that is not a ghost story at all, and Edward says that his father's ghost stories were never very frightening, which was why he liked them.

You are sitting in the armchair across the hearth from them, in the warm. Your feet are on the edge of the brass fender, and you can feel the heat through the soles of your shoes. The room is dim and golden and very still. The gas has been turned down to a whisper. The tree is dark, a tall shadow by the window, with here and there the faint wink of a glass bird. The fire has burned down from its high evening blaze into a deep, clear, steady glow. Every lump of coal is red right through, rimmed with gray ash, and when a draft comes under the door, the whole bed of coals brightens, and then dims, like something breathing in its sleep. It makes a faint sound, a tiny ticking and settling, and now and again a soft hiss of escaping gas from a coal, and a little thread of yellow flame that dances up and vanishes.

You can hear the clock on the mantel. You can hear the faint creak of the house as it cools. You can hear, outside, the policeman's boots, going slowly away up the street.

That policeman is part of the night, too. He is a constable of the Metropolitan Police, in a dark blue tunic buttoned to the throat and a tall helmet, with a lamp on his belt, and his beat takes him round these few streets, again and again, all night long, at a slow and steady pace. As he passes each house, he glances at the windows, and at the area gates, and now and then he climbs a step and tries a front door, gently, to be sure it is fastened. If he finds one open, he knocks and wakes the house. On Christmas Eve, the police of London kept their beats exactly as on every other night of the year. For the families asleep behind those doors, the sound of his boots was one of the most comforting sounds there was.

You get up, after a while, and go with Edward on his last round of the house, because every householder made one. He takes a candle. He goes down to the kitchen first, where the range is a dull red glow behind its bars, and the cat opens one eye from the rug and shuts it again. He checks the bolt on the area door, top and bottom, and the bar across the scullery window. He turns off the gas at the bracket over the table, and the kitchen sinks into a warm, red, breathing darkness, lit only by the range. You can smell the pastry and the brandy and the spices, still hanging in the air. Back up the stone stairs. He checks the bolts on the garden door, at the end of the back passage. He turns off the gas in the dining room, where the little fire has gone out to ash, and the crackers' torn paper still lies in a heap on the sideboard. He checks the front door, which he has already bolted, and checks it again. And then he comes back into the parlor, and sets his candle on the mantelpiece, and sits down.

The whole house is closed now. Closed and quiet and warm, with its people asleep above, and its fire burning below, and the cold, starry night held outside its walls.

Margaret looks up at the clock. It is almost eleven. There is one more task tonight, and it is the most important of all, and it cannot begin until the whole house is asleep.

Edward gets up, and takes the poker, and gently draws the coals of the fire together into a neat, compact heap in the middle of the grate, so that they will burn slowly and long. Then he adds just two small lumps, and no more, and sits back down beside his wife, and they wait.

## Part Ten — Around Half Past Eleven: Footsteps on the Stairs

At half past eleven, the clock on the parlor mantel gives a single soft chime, and Margaret and Edward look at each other, and stand up together without a word. The house above them has been silent for nearly an hour. It is time.

Where had the presents been hidden all this while?

In the bottom of Margaret's wardrobe, in the front bedroom on the first floor, under a folded winter shawl and a hatbox, where they have been since the middle of November. Children in every age have searched every cupboard in the house in December. Percy has searched this one. But a mother's wardrobe, full of her dresses and her scent and her private things, was a place a well-brought-up boy of nine would not quite dare to dig to the bottom of. And so the presents have lain there safe, wrapped in brown paper and tied with string, for five weeks, almost exactly as long as the pudding has hung in the larder.

Margaret and Edward take off their shoes in the parlor, and leave them side by side on the hearthrug, in front of the fire. They go up the stairs in their stockinged feet, Margaret first, carrying a candle shaded by her hand, Edward behind her. They know which stairs creak. The fourth one from the bottom. The second one from the top. They step over both.

You go up behind them. The stairs are dark, and you can see only the soft glow of Margaret's candle, leaking between her fingers, and the edge of her gray skirt, and her shadow moving huge and slow along the wall. The carpet is soft and cold under your feet. The house smells of cooling coal smoke, of pine, and very faintly of mince pies. Somewhere above, very faint, you can hear a child breathing, slow and deep. You find that you are holding your own breath, and you let it out slowly, without a sound.

In the front bedroom, Margaret kneels at the wardrobe and lifts out the shawl, and the hatbox, and then, one by one, the parcels. Edward holds them. There are not a great many. A family like this one did not heap its children with presents, as later generations would. A stocking was meant to be a stocking, not a sack.

So what goes into a stocking, in a house like this, in 1880? At the very toe, where it is hardest to reach, an orange, round and bright and smelling of summer. Then a handful of nuts, walnuts and hazelnuts and a Brazil nut or two, still in their shells. A few sweets in a twist of paper. A bright new coin, a shilling for Florence, a sixpence for Percy, a silver threepenny bit for Alice. And at the top, one or two small presents, wrapped, the real treasures. And then, beside the bed, or at its foot, perhaps one larger gift that would not fit.

Why an orange? Partly because, in a London December, an orange was still a small luxury, bright and sweet and foreign, a little piece of a warmer country. And partly, some families said, because of an old story about St. Nicholas, who was supposed to have thrown three balls of gold through a poor man's window by night, to save his three daughters from ruin, and the gold, the story went, landed in their stockings, hung up by the fire to dry. Nobody can say for certain how old that link between the orange and the gold really is. But the orange in the toe was already a familiar thing by this time, in many houses, and the children of this one would have been astonished to find it missing.

For Percy, there is a wooden spinning top, painted red and yellow, with a string to whip it, and a box of lead soldiers, a whole company of them, in scarlet coats, lying in rows in their paper box. For Florence, there is a book with a gilded spine, a story she has been wanting, and a little sewing case lined with blue silk, with scissors, a thimble and a needle book. And for Alice, there is the doll.

She is a wax doll, about the length of a man's forearm, with a soft cloth body, glass eyes that are blue, and real fair hair set into her wax head, a few strands at a time. She wears a white muslin dress with a pink sash. Margaret dressed her herself, in the evenings, and made her a little petticoat and a little cloak to match. She came from the toyshop on Upper Street, but she was made, almost certainly, in Germany, like so many of the toys of London in those days. The dolls, the tin soldiers, the Noah's arks, the spinning tops, and the glass birds on the tree downstairs, a great many of them came from the workshops of German towns, made by families working at home, and carried to London by ship.

You are kneeling on the rug beside her, and you can see the doll's face clearly now, pale and smooth in the candlelight, with a faint pink in her wax cheeks, and two tiny painted teeth showing between her lips. Her glass eyes catch the flame and shine. Her hair is soft as thistledown when you touch it. You can smell the wax, a faint, clean, honeyed smell, like a church. Margaret has tied a tiny ribbon in the doll's hair, the same red as the ribbon on Alice's stocking. You notice that, and you notice that she does not say why.

Margaret looks at the doll for a moment in the candlelight. Then she wraps her in her paper again.

Historians who have studied Victorian childhood do not agree on how far parents kept up the pretense of Father Christmas. Some memoirs describe children who believed in him completely until the age of eight or nine, and were heartbroken to find out. Others describe families who treated him as a happy game, a story that everyone in the house knew was a story, and played anyway, with the children in on the joke from the start. Many families, no doubt, did a little of both, depending on the child. In this house, the rule is simple. Alice believes. Percy says he does not, and quietly hopes he is wrong. And Florence knows.

They climb the second flight even more slowly than the first. Edward carries the parcels stacked against his chest, with his chin on the top one to keep it steady. Margaret goes ahead with the candle, and stops on every third step to listen. You climb behind them with your hand on the cold banister, and you count the steps under your breath without meaning to. The house seems larger in the dark than it ever does by day. The landing, when you reach it, is very still, and the only light on it is the faint gold glow from the nursery door, left a little ajar.

The second-floor landing. Margaret pauses at the nursery door, which has been left a little open, as children like it. She listens. The night-light is still burning on the washstand in its saucer, a small, low, steady flame. She can hear two children breathing, one slow and deep, one a little lighter. She nods to Edward. They go in.

You stand in the doorway and watch. Everything happens in silence, and very slowly. Margaret unties Alice's long black stocking from the bedpost, and holds it open, and Edward drops in the orange, very gently, so that it rolls down to the toe without a sound. Then the nuts, one small handful at a time, so they will not rattle. Then the sweets. Then the coin. Then, at the top, the doll, wrapped in her paper, with just her small wax feet showing above the rim. Margaret ties the stocking back on the bedpost with the red ribbon, in exactly the same bow as before.

Then Percy's. The top, the soldiers, the orange, the nuts, the sixpence. The stocking bulges at the foot of his bed, all lumps and corners.

Alice stirs. She turns over, and murmurs something, and one small hand comes out from under the blankets, and lies open on the pillow. Margaret and Edward freeze, absolutely still, side by side in the half dark, like two people in a painting. You freeze too. For a long moment nobody moves. Then Alice sighs, very deeply, and is still again, and her breathing slows. And Margaret, very gently, tucks the small hand back under the blankets, and they both back out of the room.

Florence's room is at the front of the house. Her door is closed. Margaret opens it slowly and slips in, alone, and fills the stocking at the foot of the bed by feel, in the dark, without the candle. The book. The sewing case. The orange. The nuts. The shilling. She ties it on again, and turns to go.

And from the bed, very softly, in the dark, a voice says, "Thank you, Mama."

Margaret stands still. Then she goes back to the bed, and bends down, and kisses her daughter's hair, and whispers to her to go to sleep. And Florence, who has been lying awake for two hours with her eyes open, listening for exactly this, turns over, and is asleep, really asleep, almost before her mother has closed the door.

You were standing just outside the door, and you heard it too. It was not loud. It was hardly more than a breath. But you heard in it everything Florence has felt all evening, behind the closed parlor door, on the cold doorstep with the waits, on the stairs with her own candle. She has crossed over tonight, from the side of the children who wait for the magic to the side of the grown-ups who make it. She is not sorry. She is only grateful. And she wanted her mother to know.

Back on the landing, Edward is waiting with the candle. They look at each other, and Margaret is smiling and her eyes are wet, and Edward does not ask why.

And at that moment, from somewhere out across the dark rooftops of Islington, the bells begin.

They are the bells of a church tower, perhaps St Mary's on Upper Street, perhaps another, ringing in Christmas at midnight. First one bell, then two, then the whole peal together, tumbling over each other in their long changing patterns, faint and silvery through the closed windows and the frosty air. Then, further off, another tower answers, and then another, very faint, so that the whole sleeping city seems to be calling softly to itself across the dark.

Our source for this moment is a poem that both Margaret and Edward would have known. Thirty years before this night, in 1850, Alfred Tennyson published a long poem called *In Memoriam*, written over many years in grief for a dear friend who had died young. It was enormously loved by Victorian readers, and the queen herself found great comfort in it after the death of Prince Albert. In the middle of it, the poet describes a Christmas Eve, when the bells ring out in the darkness. "The time draws near the birth of Christ," he wrote. "The moon is hid; the night is still; / The Christmas bells from hill to hill / Answer each other in the mist." That is almost exactly what you can hear now, from this dark landing in Canonbury, with the bells answering each other across the rooftops of London.

You go to the landing window, and press your forehead to the cold glass. There is no mist tonight, only a faint haze of smoke over the chimneys, and the stars above it. The moon is still hidden, below the rooftops to the east. Below, the street lamps burn in their long double row, and the street is empty and silver with frost. Somewhere down there, the policeman stops under a lamp, and looks up, and listens to the bells.

Up in the attic, under the roof, Lizzie is awake too. She has been lying in her narrow bed, curled round her cooling hot-water bottle, listening to the wind in the chimneys and thinking about Suffolk. And now she hears the bells. And she thinks, as everyone in the house is thinking, in their own way, that it is Christmas now. That it has come. In the bed across the little room, Sarah is snoring, very softly, like a kettle a long way off. Lizzie listens to the bells until the last of them stops. And then she pulls the blanket over her ear, and closes her eyes, and finds that she is not sad any more, only tired and warm, and she falls asleep.

You stay at the landing window a little longer. The glass is cold against your forehead, and the cold is pleasant now, after the warmth of the rooms. The bells go on, rising and falling, one tower fading as another begins. You can feel them, almost, as much as hear them, a faint trembling in the night air, very far away. Behind you, on the landing, the candle flame stands up straight and still. Behind the nursery door, two children breathe in the dark. Behind the front door, at the foot of the bed, a book and a sewing case wait in a stocking for the morning.

Margaret and Edward go down the stairs together, step by step, in their stockinged feet, missing the second stair from the top and the fourth from the bottom. In the parlor, their shoes are waiting side by side on the hearthrug, warm from the fire.

And so here is the answer to our third question. Who was awake in this house, long after everyone else had gone to sleep? Father Christmas was. And Father Christmas, on this night, in this house, as in so many houses across London and across the country, was a mother and a father in their stockinged feet, with a candle and an armful of brown paper parcels, stepping over the creaking stairs, holding their breath at a child's murmur, and tying a red ribbon back in exactly the same bow. For an hour or so, on this one night of the year, the two most important people awake in the house were the ones whose names no child would ever think to thank. Florence did, in the dark. And a young maid in the attic was awake to hear the bells. And all the while, downstairs in the parlor, there was one more thing that stayed awake all night, and never once closed its eyes. The fire.

## Part Eleven — Around Half Past Twelve: The Fire Is Banked

It is past midnight now. It is Christmas Day. The bells have stopped, and the silence they leave behind is deep and soft, like snow after a long fall.

How do you keep a coal fire alive until morning?

You put it to bed, the way you would put a child to bed. And that is what Edward is doing now.

He kneels on the hearthrug in his stockinged feet, in front of the parlor grate, with Margaret's candle on the mantelpiece above him. The fire has burned down to a low, clear bed of coals, glowing red and orange, with a fine gray skin of ash on the top. He takes the small shovel from its stand. With it he gathers the coals gently together, into one close heap at the back of the grate, so that they keep each other warm. Then he takes a shovelful of small coal from the scuttle, the fine coal and the dust at the bottom, and he lays it carefully over the top of the glowing heap. Then a little of the gray ash from under the grate, sprinkled over that, like a blanket over a quilt.

The bright glow goes dim at once. The flames disappear. For a moment it looks as though he has put the fire out.

But he has not. Under that dark blanket, the coals are still burning, very slowly, starved of air, just enough to stay alive. They will smolder there all night, quietly, giving off a faint, steady warmth. And in the morning, when Lizzie comes down, she will only need to rake the ash away, and give the coals a little air, and lay on a few fresh lumps, and the fire will wake up and blaze again, as if it had never slept.

You are kneeling beside Edward on the hearthrug. You can feel the warmth of the hearth on your face and your hands, gentler now, softer. The smell is gentle too, a dry, dusty, smoky smell, like a warm stone. If you hold your hand just above the banked coals, you can feel the heat rising through the ash, patient and even. If you look very closely, between two dark lumps, you can see a single thread of red, glowing like a vein.

Edward stands the brass fireguard in front of the grate, a tall mesh screen on little feet, so that no coal can roll out onto the rug in the night. He sets the poker and the tongs and the shovel neatly back in their stand. He brushes the hearth with the little brush. And then he stands up, slowly, a little stiffly, because it has been a long day, and he looks at the room.

You look at it with him. The gas is out. There is only the candle on the mantelpiece, and the faint red breath of the fire behind its guard. The tree stands dark by the window, tall and quiet, with its thirty cold candles and its sleeping glass birds. The cards along the mantelpiece lean in their row, faintly gilded by the candlelight. The holly on the picture frames is black against the walls. On the round table, the little pile of envelopes waits for Monday. On the hearthrug, there are two pairs of shoes.

Margaret picks up her shoes, and Edward picks up his. Margaret takes the candle. At the door, they both turn back, the way you do when you are leaving a room you love, and look at it once more.

The room is warm. The fire is safe. The tree is dark. Everything that needed doing has been done.

Edward pulls the door gently to behind them, but not quite closed, so that a little of the warmth can drift out into the hall during the night, and rise up the stairs.

On the stairs, they go slowly. They are very tired now, and very content. They do not need to step over the creaking stairs any more, but they do anyway, out of habit, and Margaret laughs at herself, without a sound.

At about twenty to one, while they are climbing, the moon comes up over the rooftops to the east. It is a half moon, waning, pale and clear, and it rises slowly above the chimney pots of the terrace behind, and lays a thin silver light across the slates, and the frosty gardens, and the back windows of the sleeping houses. The moon that Tennyson said was hidden is hidden no longer. Through the landing window, as they pass, it lights the side of Margaret's face.

Our source for this hour is a novel that was published eight years before this night, in 1872, by a young writer who had grown up in Dorset, not far from the town where Margaret was a girl. His name was Thomas Hardy. The book was called *Under the Greenwood Tree*, and it was the story of a little country choir, the old church musicians of a village, and it begins with them setting out on their rounds to sing carols on Christmas Eve. Hardy opened his story with this line: "On a cold and starry Christmas-eve less than a generation ago." Margaret read that book when it came out, and she thought of her grandmother's farmhouse, and of the old choir with their fiddles, who used to come to the door when she was small. Tonight, after the waits, she has been thinking of it again.

People who study this time have argued, gently, about how much of the Victorian Christmas was real and how much was longing. Some say that writers like Hardy were looking back at a Christmas that was already disappearing, and made it sweeter in memory than it had been in life. Others say that the warmth they described was quite real, and that families like this one found it, in their own way, in their own parlors. Perhaps it was always a little of both, a real warmth, and a little longing, together.

In the front bedroom, Margaret sets the candle on the dressing table. The room is cold, but the bed is not, because Lizzie put a stone bottle into it before she went up to bed, wrapped in flannel, and it has made a deep warm hollow in the middle of the mattress.

Margaret takes the pins out of her hair, one by one, and lays them in a little china dish. Her hair comes down over her shoulders, long and brown, with a few threads of gray in it now that she does not mind. She unhooks her dress, and steps out of it, and lays it over the chair. She puts on her long white nightgown, and a knitted shawl over it, and climbs into bed, and slides her feet down to the warm place, and sighs.

Edward winds his watch and lays it on the table by the bed. He says his prayers, briefly, kneeling. Then he blows out the candle, and the room goes dark, except for the faint silver of the moon at the edge of the curtain.

They lie side by side. They talk for a little while, in whispers. About Alice and the sugar mouse. About Percy and the whistles. About Florence, and what she said. Margaret tells Edward about it, and he is quiet for a moment, and then he reaches for her hand under the blankets, and holds it.

Margaret's voice grows slower, and further apart. She says something about the turkey. She says something about church. She begins to say something about the brooch, because she has guessed, and then she does not finish it.

She is asleep.

Edward lies awake a little longer, listening to the house. He hears the faint creak of a floorboard settling somewhere above him. He hears, very far off, a single cab going home through the empty streets, the clop of hooves fading away. He hears his wife's slow breathing beside him. He thinks of his father's cold hands, and the lantern, and the fiddle in the frost. And he thinks of the parlor below, and the fire banked in the grate, safe behind its guard, keeping its little red heart alive under the ash.

His eyes close.

You go softly along the landing, and up the stairs, one more time, to look in on everyone.

The nursery door is still a little open. The night-light burns low in its saucer on the washstand, a small, steady, golden flame no bigger than a fingertip. The nursery fire behind its wire guard has sunk to a dull red glow. In the bed by the wall, Percy lies on his back with one arm flung up over his head, and his mouth a little open, breathing slow and deep. His stocking hangs at the foot of his bed, fat and lumpy, a corner of the box of soldiers poking out at the top. He does not know it is there. In the morning, he will pretend that he is not excited.

In the other bed, Alice lies curled on her side, small as a kitten, with her fair plait across the pillow, and one hand tucked under her cheek. Her blue glass ring is still on her thumb. At the foot of her bed, the long black stocking hangs heavy now, with a pair of small wax feet just showing at the top, and a red ribbon tied in a bow. Her breathing is so soft you can hardly hear it. Once, she smiles in her sleep.

You stand in the doorway and listen to the two of them breathing, the slow and the soft, the deep and the light, in and out, in and out, like the sea a long way off. Then you draw the door almost closed again, and leave them.

Florence's room is at the front. Through the half-open door, you can see the moonlight lying across her bed in a pale stripe, and her face on the pillow, calm and grown-up and very young, all at once. Her own stocking waits at the foot of her bed. Her new pencil case is on the chair, beside her book. She is sleeping the deep, still sleep of someone who has had a very long and very happy day.

Up again, to the top of the house. The attic stairs are steep and narrow and cold, and the moonlight comes in through the little roof window, silver on the bare boards. In the small room under the slates, Sarah sleeps on her back in her narrow bed, with her new green shawl spread over the top of her blankets for extra warmth, snoring very softly, comfortably, like a cat by a stove. Across the room, Lizzie lies curled tight round her hot-water bottle, with the blanket pulled over one ear and her new gray gloves on the chair beside her, folded together, as if they were holding hands. On the wall above her bed, the robin on her mother's card looks down at her in the moonlight. Her face is peaceful. She is not homesick now. She is only asleep.

And down again, all the way down, past the sleeping floors, past the warm parlor door, down the stone stairs, to the kitchen. The range glows faintly behind its bars, banked for the night. The cat Tom is a dark curl on the rag rug in front of it, his tail over his nose. The mince pies sit in their rows under their clean cloth. In the cold larder, in the dark, the pudding hangs from its hook in its floured cloth, turning very slightly on its string, waiting for its moment tomorrow. Everything is ready.

You climb back up the stone stairs, slowly, and stop in the hall.

You are alone now, in the dark hall, the only one still awake. The house is quite still around you. Beside you, through the half-open parlor door, there is the faintest warmth drifting out into the hall. Above you, all the bedrooms, full of sleep. And down in the grate, behind the brass guard, under its blanket of dust and ash, the fire is still burning, very quietly, all on its own.

## Part Twelve — Around Four in the Morning: The Small Hours, and the First Gray Light

The hours go by softly now. One o'clock. Two. Three. The clock on the parlor mantel strikes each of them in the dark, to an empty room, with its small silvery chime, and nobody hears it.

What does London sound like at four o'clock on Christmas morning?

Almost nothing at all.

You are sitting in the parlor, in the armchair by the hearth, with a blanket round your shoulders, and you are listening. There are no omnibuses now. No cabs. No carts. No barrows, no bells, no voices calling the price of holly. The great city, which roared all through the afternoon, has fallen into the deepest hush of its whole year. If you listen very hard, you can hear the faint tick of the clock. You can hear the house breathing, the small creaks and sighs of old timbers in the cold. And once, very far away, so faint you are not sure you heard it, the slow footsteps of the policeman on his beat, going by in the frosty street, and fading.

That is all. And it is enough.

If you could drift up out of the armchair, and through the window, and out into the street, you would find it silver and empty. The frost has thickened on the iron railings, and on the stone steps, and on the round iron lid of the coal hole in the pavement, so that its little cast pattern stands out white. The holly wreaths on the doors along the terrace are stiff with it. The gas lamps burn in their long row, each in its own small circle of light, and nothing moves under any of them. At the corner of the street, the red pillar box stands alone, with a little cap of frost on its top, and inside it, in the dark, a heap of Christmas cards lies waiting for the morning.

Above the rooftops, the half moon has climbed high now, small and white and far away. It pours its thin light down over the slates and the chimney pots, over a thousand banked fires and a thousand sleeping houses. Every chimney breathes a faint gray thread into the still air. Every one of those houses, tonight, has its own small story. Its own stockings. Its own pudding in its cloth. Its own fire, kept safe till morning.

And then you drift back in, through the glass, into the warm, and settle into the armchair again, and pull the blanket closer.

In front of you, behind the brass guard, the fire sleeps under its blanket of ash. It gives no light that you can see, at first. But as your eyes grow used to the dark, you begin to make out the faintest glow, deep in the grate, a dull red warmth between the dark coals, like the glow of a hand held over a candle. You can feel it on your face, gentle and even. It is the only warm thing awake in the house.

The tree stands by the window, dark and still. A little moonlight from the gap in the curtains falls across one of its branches, and a glass bird shines there, faintly silver. The tinsel does not move. The sugar mice hang still on their strings. The wax angel at the top is a pale shape against the dark.

Our last source of the night is a poem. In January 1872, eight years before this night, a magazine printed a new Christmas poem by the London poet Christina Rossetti. She gave it the plainest of titles, simply calling it a Christmas carol. It begins with a cold, still world, very like the one outside these windows tonight. "In the bleak mid-winter / Frosty wind made moan, / Earth stood hard as iron, / Water like a stone." And it ends, after the shepherds and the wise men and the angels, with a quiet question and a quiet answer. "What can I give Him, / Poor as I am? / If I were a shepherd / I would bring a lamb; / If I were a Wise Man / I would do my part; / Yet what I can I give Him, / Give my heart."

That is a good poem to hold in your mind, in a dark room, at four o'clock in the morning, beside a sleeping fire.

People who think about this night, and nights like it, sometimes wonder whether we remember the Victorian Christmas too kindly, and forget the cold and the work and the hard edges of it. Others say that we are right to remember its warmth, because the families who lived it remembered that too, and wrote about it, and kept it. Perhaps both are true, here, in the dark. The hard work of the day is done. The warmth is what is left.

You think about the long day that has passed in this room. The afternoon light going gray at the window. The lamplighter's pearls. The postman's double knock, and the robins on the cards. The locked door, and the glass birds coming out of their tissue paper. The thirty small flames, lit from the top down, and put out from the bottom up. Alice's silent face in the doorway. The blue fire of the snapdragon. Percy's two whistles. The hymn in the nursery. The pudding in the cold larder, and Sarah's floury arms. The choir on the frosty steps, and the boy with his red ears. The envelopes. The ghost of the umbrella. The bells. And the whisper in the dark.

All of it is quiet now. All of it is folded away, like the glass birds in their tissue paper, to be kept.

In the hall, in the drawer of the little table, a small parcel in brown paper is waiting too, with a silver sprig of holly inside it. In the dining room, the torn paper of the crackers lies in a soft heap on the sideboard. In the kitchen, under a cloth, the mince pies sit in their rows. Everything in this house is waiting, the way a house waits on Christmas morning, patiently, in the dark.

You let your eyes close for a moment.

Upstairs, in the front bedroom, Margaret is asleep, on her side, with one hand under her cheek, and her hair spread across the pillow. Her breathing is long and slow and quiet. She is not thinking about the turkey any more. She is not thinking about anything.

Beside her, Edward is asleep on his back, very still, with his watch ticking softly on the table by the bed. His father's cold hands, and the lantern in the frost, have drifted away into ordinary dreams.

In the nursery, the night-light has burned down very low in its saucer, and its flame is small and blue at the bottom, and steady. Percy has turned over in his sleep, and lies with his face in the pillow and one foot sticking out from under the blankets. Alice has not moved at all. She is still curled on her side, small and warm, with her hand under her cheek, the blue glass ring on her thumb. The two stockings hang heavy and quiet at the foot of the two beds.

In the front room on the second floor, Florence sleeps in the pale stripe of moonlight, which has moved across her bed in the night, and now lies across the floor. Her face is calm. Her stocking waits.

At the very top of the house, under the frosty slates, Sarah snores softly in her narrow bed, warm under her green shawl. And across the small room, Lizzie sleeps curled round her cooling stone bottle, her gray gloves folded on the chair. Margaret told her last night that tomorrow, being Christmas, she may lie in until seven. She does not know yet how glad she will be of it.

Down in the kitchen, the range sighs in its sleep, and the cat sleeps beside it.

And you, in the armchair by the hearth, can rest too. There is nothing left to do tonight. Nothing to buy, nothing to light, nothing to watch over. The candles are out. The doors are bolted. The children are asleep, and their stockings are full. The fire is banked and guarded, and it will keep itself.

So let your breathing slow, the way theirs has. In, and out. Let the blanket be heavy and warm on your shoulders. Let your hands lie loose in your lap. Feel the faint, steady warmth of the hearth on your face, and the soft dark of the room all around you, and the quiet of the whole sleeping city beyond the curtains. You are safe here. You are warm. You can stay as long as you like.

The clock ticks. The fire breathes under its ash. The house sleeps.

The hours go on. Four. Five. Six.

And then, at last, toward seven o'clock, something changes. It is very slight. Through the gap in the parlor curtains, the sky over the rooftops to the east, which has been black all night, begins to thin. It is not light yet. It is only a little less dark. A deep, cold blue, and then a gray, very faint, along the line of the chimney pots, as if someone had breathed on a dark window. The first gray light of Christmas morning, creeping over London.

In the street, the gas lamps still burn in their long double row. Soon the lamplighter will come back, with his long pole over his shoulder, and put them out, one by one. But not yet.

In a little while, Lizzie will wake in her attic, and stretch, and remember what day it is, and smile. She will dress quickly in the cold, and come down through the sleeping house, and kneel on the parlor hearthrug, and lift the brass guard away, and rake the ash gently off the coals, and give them a little air. And the fire, which has been sleeping all night under its blanket, will wake, and glow, and catch, and blaze up, bright and warm, for Christmas Day.

In a little while after that, Alice will open her eyes, and reach out one toe toward the foot of her bed. And then the whole house will wake at once.

But not yet. Not yet.

For now, everyone is still asleep.

Margaret is asleep, warm in the front bedroom, with her hand under her cheek. Edward is asleep beside her, his watch ticking softly in the dark. Florence is asleep in the gray light, her stocking at the foot of her bed. Percy is asleep with his foot out of the blankets, and Alice is asleep with her blue glass ring, and her doll waiting for her in a long black stocking tied with a red ribbon. Sarah is asleep under her green shawl, and Lizzie is asleep with her gray gloves folded beside her, a long way from Suffolk, and not lonely at all.

The house is quiet. The house is warm. The night has passed safely, every hour of it.

And down in the front parlor, in the gray first light, behind the brass guard, under its soft blanket of ash, the coal fire is still glowing, red and patient and alive, just as it was when we found it yesterday afternoon. It has kept the house warm all night. It has kept its little red heart burning in the dark. And it is waiting, quietly, for the morning.

If you enjoyed spending this Christmas Eve with the Hales, a like or a subscription would mean a great deal, but only if you truly enjoyed it. And if there is a time or a place you would love to spend a night in, leave it in the comments, because your ideas truly help.

Next time, we will visit a very different kind of house, on a very different kind of night, somewhere much colder and much further from London.

Sleep well, friend. Good night.
