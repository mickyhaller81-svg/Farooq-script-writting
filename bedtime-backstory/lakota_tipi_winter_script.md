## Part One — Around Three in the Afternoon: Smoke Above the Cottonwoods

It is January on the northern Plains, in the year 1870, and the snow on the high ground is blue in the long afternoon light. Below the bluffs, in the bend of a frozen creek, a ring of tipis stands among bare cottonwood trees. From each one, a thin thread of smoke rises straight up into the still air. Inside one of them, a small fire of dry wood burns at the very center of the floor. And the air outside is so cold that the snow squeaks under every moccasin that passes the door.

Not the tipi of the picture books, all feathered headdresses and drums and painted faces in the firelight. Just one ordinary Lakota family, on one ordinary winter night, in one ordinary home of poles and hide.

And without a few old tricks, you would not have lasted until morning, not on these open plains, in the middle of the month the Lakota call the Moon of Hard Times, with the cold coming down off the high country like water running downhill.

So tonight I would like you to carry three small questions with you. The first is this. Why was a tipi, a tent of poles and buffalo hide, warmer on a night like this than a log cabin with walls a foot thick? The second. With no paper calendar on any wall, how did a family tell one year from another, so that a grandfather could say exactly which winter his grandson was born in? And the third. Of all the miles of river in this country, why did the camp choose this one exact bend of this one small creek to spend the winter in?

These are not three separate questions. They are three ways of looking at the same small fire, on the same long night, and we will come to each of them in its own time.

This family never quite existed under one name, but every detail of their night is real.

So let your head grow heavy now. Let your shoulders loosen and drop. Your eyes may close whenever they like, and the fire will keep burning without you. Let us go back, gently, to a cold, bright afternoon in a sheltered valley, and to a door of hide that faces the morning sun. So let us begin.

The door is a flap of buffalo hide, laced onto the cover of the tipi at its lower edge, and held stiff by a short stick sewn across its top. It faces east, toward the sunrise, as the doors of nearly all the tipis in this camp face east. When you push it aside and step over the low sill, you have to stoop, because the door is only about as tall as a child. And then you straighten up, and you are inside.

The first thing you notice is the warmth. The second is the light. It is softer than you expect, a pale gold light, because the afternoon sun is shining through the hide cover itself, which has been scraped and smoked until it glows like the shade of a lamp. The third thing you notice is the shape. There are no corners. The floor is a circle about sixteen feet across, and the walls lean in toward you as they rise, and above your head the long, peeled poles gather together into a dark knot where the smoke goes out into the sky.

And in the middle of everything, the fire.

It is a small fire, smaller than you would build if this were your own home. A few split sticks of cottonwood and ash are laid in a shallow pit, ringed with stones, and the flames are low and clear and nearly smokeless. A woman is kneeling beside it, feeding it one stick at a time.

Her name is Her Good Road. She is thirty years old, and this tipi is hers. That is not a figure of speech. Among the Lakota, the home belonged to the woman who made it. She tanned the hides for the cover, she cut them and fitted them and sewed them together, or gathered her friends and relatives to help her sew them, and she put the tipi up and took it down every time the camp moved. If you asked whose lodge this was, the answer would be her name.

Her husband is Two Hawks. He is thirty-four, and he is a hunter and a keeper of horses, and at this moment he is outside, down among the trees, cutting cottonwood branches for the ponies with a steel axe that he traded for three summers ago. You can hear it, if you listen, a dull knock carried a long way through the frozen air.

On the far side of the fire, in the place of honor at the back of the lodge, facing the door, sits an old man with a buffalo robe drawn up around his shoulders. He is Swift Cloud, the uncle of Her Good Road, her mother's elder brother, and he is sixty-seven years old. He has seen a great many winters, and he remembers every one of them by its name. We will learn why that matters later tonight.

To his left, nearer the door, an older woman is working a piece of soft hide across her knee. She is Plenty Robes, the mother of Her Good Road, and she is sixty-two. Her husband died some years ago, and she lives here now, with her daughter's family. She knows more stories than anyone in the camp, and more ways of doing small things well.

And then there are the children. A girl of thirteen, Pretty Shell, sits beside her grandmother, learning. A boy of ten, Young Badger, is outside somewhere with his friends, and will come in later, red-cheeked and soaked to the knees. And in a cradle of quilled hide, propped against a backrest near her mother, a baby girl of eight months is sleeping with her fists by her face. Her name, for now, is Little Star. Children's names could change as they grew, and she may carry another name one day.

So that is the whole household. Seven people under one roof of hide.

What day is it, and where exactly are we?

By the calendar of the soldiers and traders, it is the middle of January, 1870. By the reckoning of the people in this camp, it is the moon the Lakota call Wiótheȟika Wí, the Moon of Hard Times, the coldest and leanest moon of the winter. Some families called this moon by another name, the moon when the trees crack in the cold, because on the hardest nights the frozen sap inside the cottonwoods could split the trunks with a sound like a gunshot.

And we are in the valley of a small creek that runs down into the White River, in what is now the southwestern part of South Dakota. The family belongs to the Sicangu, one of the seven great divisions of the Lakota people. French traders, long before, had called them the Brulé, the Burnt Thighs, a name taken from an old story of a prairie fire, and many English-speaking visitors still used it. The Lakota themselves, together with their relatives the Dakota and Nakota, were the people the outside world called the Sioux.

This was a moment of uneasy peace. In the year 1868, at Fort Laramie, leaders of the Lakota and other nations had signed a treaty with the United States. It set aside a vast territory west of the Missouri River as Lakota land, including the Black Hills, and it promised that no outsiders would settle there without the people's consent. Not every leader trusted it, and the years ahead would bring great hardship. But tonight, in this valley, there are no soldiers and no settlers anywhere in sight. There is only the camp, the creek, the trees and the cold.

Now here is something that may surprise you. In this same winter, somewhere among the Sicangu camps, perhaps not very far from this one, there was a baby boy just over a year old. He had been born the winter before, in a cold month, the first son of a man named Standing Bear. His childhood name was Ota K'te, which means Plenty Kill. When he grew up, he went east to a school in Pennsylvania, and took the English name Luther. And when he was an old man, living far away in California, he wrote down what he remembered of the life he had been born into.

His first book was called My People the Sioux. It came out in 1928. In it, Luther Standing Bear told his readers that he had been born in the old way, with no paper and no date. His people reckoned his birth by the season, in the cold moon when the bark of the trees cracks, and by the name of that year. Only later, he wrote, did he work it out for himself in the white man's calendar, and settle on December, 1868.

That means that in the month we are visiting tonight, the boy who would become Luther Standing Bear was a baby of about thirteen months, wrapped in a robe, in a tipi much like this one, with a fire much like this one burning at its center. He would remember none of this particular winter. But he would remember the winters that came after it, and the tipi, and the fire, and his mother and father, and he would write them down, so that we can hear his voice tonight.

He is not a member of our household. He belongs to his own family, in his own lodge. But he will walk beside us all through this night, as a kind of guide, and where his own words help us, we will let him speak.

Historians do not quite agree on where he was born. Some accounts place his birth at the Spotted Tail Agency, on what later became the Rosebud Reservation, among his father's Sicangu people. Others say Pine Ridge, the home of the Oglala, his mother's people. Standing Bear himself did not give a map reference, only a season and a year. Either way, he was born into the same rolling country of creeks and bluffs that we are visiting tonight.

Back inside the tipi, you are standing just inside the door, a little to one side, the way a guest should. The fire is in front of you, low and bright. The smoke rises straight up, thin as a feather, and slips out through the opening at the top, where two flaps of hide stand open like a pair of ears against the sky. The air smells of wood smoke and tanned hide and sage, and faintly of the dried meat hanging in bundles from the poles above. Under your feet, the ground has been cleared of snow and spread with grass and robes, and it is dry and soft. Your face is warm. Your back, near the door, is still a little cold.

Her Good Road looks up and nods to you, and moves aside a parfleche, a folded box of stiff painted rawhide, so that there is room for you to sit.

And you notice that the walls here are not just the outer cover. All around the lower part of the tipi, from the ground to about the height of a man's shoulder, there is a second wall, a lining of soft hide, tied to the poles on the inside. It is painted with small figures in red and black, horses and people and the tracks of animals. Behind it, between the lining and the outer cover, you can see that there is a gap, a hand's width or more, packed in places with dry grass.

This inner wall is called the liner. Tonight, it is going to matter a great deal. It is, along with the fire, the thing that will keep this family alive until morning. And before this night is over, we will understand exactly why.

In our own time, if we want to know how cold it is outside, we look at a number on a screen. Here, nobody needs a number. Her Good Road knows how cold it is by the sound of the snow under Young Badger's feet when he runs past, and by how straight the smoke stands above the lodge, and by the way the cottonwoods creak along the creek.

She feeds the fire one more stick, and sets a stone a little closer to the flames, for later. Then she sits back on her heels and listens to the far knock of her husband's axe among the trees.

Swift Cloud speaks without looking up. He says that the smoke is standing straight tonight, and that means no wind until morning, and a hard, clear frost. Plenty Robes says that she knew that already, because her knees told her so at noon. Pretty Shell laughs, and her grandmother pretends to scold her, and goes on working the soft hide across her knee.

In the cradle, Little Star stirs, and opens her eyes, and looks up at the poles crossing far above her, and the small square of blue sky at the top, where the smoke goes out. Then she closes her eyes again.

Outside, the afternoon light is turning from white to gold. It is still a long time until dark. There is wood to cut and water to fetch, and ponies to see to, and a meal to make. And in the center of the floor, the fire burns low and steady, as it has burned all day, as it will burn all night, the small bright heart of the lodge.

## Part Two — Around Four O'Clock: Wood, Water and a Hole in the Ice

The sun has slipped lower now, and it hangs a hand's width above the western bluffs, small and white and without much warmth in it. The shadows of the cottonwoods have stretched right across the creek and up the far bank, long and blue on the snow.

How do you fetch water for a whole family, when the creek beside your home is frozen as hard as stone?

Pretty Shell knows the answer, because it is her job this afternoon. She has a small hatchet tucked into her belt and a kettle of brass in each hand, and she is walking down the trodden path from the tipi to the creek, with her grandmother a few steps behind her.

Every winter camp had paths like this one, worn into the snow by many feet, from the lodges down to the water. And every morning and every afternoon, someone went down them to open the ice. A hole cut the day before would freeze over again in the night, sometimes with ice as thick as a hand, and it had to be broken open with an axe or a hatchet, or with the sharpened end of a stick. Then the kettles were dipped in, carefully, so that nobody's sleeve got wet, and carried back up the slope to the lodge.

Before the traders came, the people carried water in bags made from the stomach or the bladder of a buffalo, cleaned and dried. They boiled food in these, too, by dropping hot stones into the water until it bubbled. By 1870, nearly every Lakota household had at least one kettle of brass, copper or iron, traded for buffalo robes, and these had made the cooking a great deal easier. But the old ways were still known. Plenty Robes remembers boiling soup with stones when she was a girl, and she can still do it, and sometimes, to show her granddaughter, she does.

And when the ice is too thick, or the hole is too far, there is always snow. A kettle packed full of clean snow and set by the fire will give you water, though it takes a great many kettles of snow to make one kettle of water, and the water tastes a little flat.

Down at the creek, Pretty Shell kneels on the ice and chops at yesterday's hole. The ice is milky on top and clear underneath, and when the hatchet breaks through, dark water wells up into the hole and steams faintly in the cold air. She dips the first kettle, and then the second, and stands up, and the cold of the water bites through the brass into her fingers.

Wood is the other daily work of a winter camp, and there is more of it than you would think.

A tipi fire is small, but it burns all day and all night, all winter long. And so every day the women and girls went out with axes and with carrying straps, and brought back bundles of dead branches and fallen wood from along the creek. They chose their fuel with care. Dry cottonwood burned quickly and cleanly, with little smoke, but gave off its heat fast. Ash and box elder burned hotter and longer. Green wood smoked and spat, and nobody wanted it inside. A good wood-gatherer knew every dead tree within a long walk of camp, and which of them would burn well.

Where there were no trees at all, out on the open grassland, people burned dried buffalo dung instead, gathered from the prairie in great baskets. It burned with a hot, steady flame and very little smoke, and many travelers who tried it were surprised to find that it barely smelled at all. But here in the creek bottom, among the cottonwoods, there is wood enough, and that is part of why the camp is here. We will come back to that later tonight.

Now here is a surprising detail, and it comes from a painter.

In the summer of 1832, a young man from Pennsylvania named George Catlin traveled up the Missouri River on one of the first steamboats to go so far into the Plains. He had given up a career as a lawyer to paint the native peoples of the West, and over the next few years he painted hundreds of portraits and scenes. Along the upper Missouri that summer he painted a picture he called Sioux Encamped on the Upper Missouri, Dressing Buffalo Meat and Robes. It shows a camp of tipis along a riverbank, with women at work everywhere, scraping hides pegged out on the ground, and racks of meat drying in the sun.

What strikes you, looking at it now, is how much of the picture is work. Not hunting, not dancing, not war. Work. The kind of work that Her Good Road and Plenty Robes do every day of their lives.

Catlin wrote long letters about what he saw, and later he gathered them into a book, published in London in 1841. In that book, he wrote with real admiration about the lodges of the Plains. He painted one great Crow lodge and called the painting Crow Lodge of Twenty-five Buffalo Skins, because that is how many hides had gone into its cover. He wrote that it stood about twenty-five feet high when it was raised. And he wrote that the Crow, of all the tribes in that region, made the most beautiful lodges, dressing the hides almost white and ornamenting them with porcupine quills.

A lodge as large as that was unusual, a lodge for a great man or for gatherings. Most family tipis among the Lakota took far fewer hides, perhaps twelve to sixteen of them, making a home about fifteen or sixteen feet across at the floor. Our family's tipi is one of those, an ordinary, well-made home of fourteen hides. Her Good Road made it herself, four summers ago, with the help of her mother and a group of women friends, and she knows every seam in it.

Historians have argued about George Catlin for a long time. Some say that he romanticized the peoples he painted, that he worked fast and sometimes from memory, and that he believed, wrongly, that they were all about to vanish, which colored everything he wrote. Others point out that he was one of very few outsiders to see these camps at all, before so much was changed, and that his paintings of ordinary things, camps and lodges and work, are among the most valuable records we have. Both views, perhaps, are true at once.

You are walking now behind Pretty Shell and her grandmother, back up the slope toward the camp. The path is narrow and packed hard, and you have to place your feet carefully. The snow on each side is knee-deep and crusted, and it gives off a faint blue glitter in the low sun. Ahead of you, the tipis stand in a loose ring among the trees, perhaps twenty of them, their tops dark with smoke, their covers glowing a warm cream color where the sun still touches them. Between them, dogs lie curled in the snow with their tails over their noses. A pony stands with its head down, waiting. Somewhere a child is laughing. Somewhere else, a woman is singing under her breath as she works, a song without any words that you can catch. Near the edge of the trees, two women are walking back toward the lodges with great bundles of dead branches on their backs, held by straps across their foreheads and shoulders, bent forward a little under the weight. A dog trots ahead of them with a smaller bundle tied to a little frame of poles that drags behind it on the snow, the way dogs carried loads on these plains long before there were horses.

The cold is sharp in your nose, and your breath goes up in a cloud. The air smells of smoke from twenty fires, all mingling together, cottonwood and ash and a little sweet pine. And beneath it there is a clean, mineral smell, the smell of the frozen creek and the snow.

Pretty Shell's arms are aching from the kettles. Her grandmother does not offer to take one, because that would be a kind of insult, and Pretty Shell would not want it. But she walks a little slower, so that the girl can keep up.

In our own time, water comes from a tap in the wall, as much as we want, hot or cold, at any hour of the day or night. Here, every drop that this family drinks, or cooks with, or washes in tonight, has been carried up this slope in a girl's hands.

At the top of the path, Young Badger comes running out from between two lodges, with three other boys behind him. His face is bright red with cold and laughter, his leggings are caked with snow up to the thigh, and he is carrying a sled made from the curved ribs of a buffalo, lashed together with rawhide, with a seat of hide stretched across them. The boys have been sliding down the bluff behind the camp all afternoon, as far out onto the ice of the creek as they can go.

He sees his sister and his grandmother, and he stops, and stands up straight, and walks past them slowly and politely. Then, as soon as he is past, he runs again.

Plenty Robes watches him go, and shakes her head, and smiles.

Back at the tipi, Pretty Shell sets the kettles down just inside the door, in the place where the water always goes. Her mother takes one of them and hangs it from a tripod of sticks over the fire, a little to one side, where it will warm slowly without boiling.

Plenty Robes sits down again in her place and picks up the piece of hide she left on her knee. It is the skin of a young deer, and she has been working it for days. You watch her hands. She folds it, and stretches it, and pulls it back and forth over the edge of a twisted rawhide rope that is tied to one of the poles, back and forth, back and forth, the way you might dry your back with a towel. With every pull, the hide grows a little softer and a little paler.

This was the last and longest part of tanning, and there was no shortcut. A hide was first staked out on the ground and scraped clean of flesh with a tool of bone or iron. Then it was scraped again, to make it even. Then it was soaked and rubbed with a paste made from the brains of the animal, mixed with fat and liver, which worked into the skin and kept it from drying stiff. And then came this, the pulling and stretching and working, hour after hour, until the hide was as soft as cloth. A buffalo robe for winter, with the thick hair left on, was the hardest work of all, because the hide was so heavy that it took the whole strength of a woman's arms and back. The best robes of all came from buffalo killed in early winter, when their coats were thickest.

Plenty Robes holds out the deerskin to you, and you take it in your hands. It is warm from her knee. It is softer than you expected, softer than any leather you have ever touched, almost like flannel, and it smells faintly of smoke. She has hung it over the fire twice already, high in the smoke, because a hide that has been smoked will dry soft again after it gets wet, while an unsmoked hide will dry as hard as a board. You fold it the way she folded it, and hand it back, and she nods, and goes on working.

Her own name, Plenty Robes, is a kind of praise. A woman who made many fine robes was honored for it, and among the Plains peoples some women kept count of the hides they had finished, with small marks cut into the handles of their scrapers, the way another person might keep a tally of good deeds. Plenty Robes has made more of them than she can easily remember. The one that covers Swift Cloud's shoulders tonight is one of hers.

Down among the trees, the axe has stopped. Two Hawks is coming back up from the creek, dragging a great bundle of cottonwood branches behind him on a rope, with the bark still on them, fresh and pale where the axe has cut. They are not for the fire. They are for the horses. He leaves them in a heap near the pony herd, and the ponies come at once, and begin to strip the bark with their teeth.

Two Hawks stamps the snow from his moccasins at the door, and stoops, and comes inside. He brings the cold in with him, a wave of it, and the fire leans away from the door and then straightens again.

He sits down in his usual place, and holds his hands out to the flames. Swift Cloud asks him how the ponies look. Two Hawks says that they are thin, but not too thin, and that the bark is good this year. The old man nods, slowly, as if this were exactly the answer he expected, and pulls his robe a little closer.

Her Good Road lifts the door flap for a moment and looks out at the sky to the east, where it is already turning a deep, cold blue. Then she lets the flap fall, and kneels, and lays two more sticks of ash across the fire, and the flames rise up to meet them, clear and steady, the warm gold center of the lodge.

## Part Three — Around Five O'Clock: The Moon of Hard Times Rises

The sun goes down behind the western bluffs at about ten minutes to five. For a little while the snow on the high ground glows pink, and then gray, and then a deep and quiet blue. And almost at the same moment, on the other side of the sky, something else is coming up.

It is the full moon. Tonight, the seventeenth of January, 1870, the moon is full, and it rises over the eastern ridge less than half an hour after the sun has set, enormous and pale gold, as if the sky had decided not to be dark tonight after all. The Moon of Hard Times, at the very height of its strength.

Why are there two flaps of hide standing up at the top of every tipi, and why does Two Hawks keep looking up at them?

He is outside again now, standing a little way from the door, with his head tipped back. A faint breath of air has begun to come down the valley from the north, the way the cold air does at dusk, sliding down off the high ground into the creek bottom. You can barely feel it on your face. But Two Hawks can see it, because the thin column of smoke above the lodge is no longer standing straight. It has begun to lean.

The two flaps at the top of a tipi are called the smoke flaps, or the wind flaps. Each one is a wing of hide, part of the cover itself, on either side of the smoke hole. At the tip of each wing there is a small pocket, and into each pocket fits the end of a long, thin pole that stands outside the tipi, leaning against the back. By moving these two outside poles, you can open the flaps wide, or close them, or set one higher than the other.

Luther Standing Bear explained this in the chapter of his book that he called simply The Tipi. He wrote that the flaps worked as windbreaks. When the wind came from the north, the flap on the north side was raised, to stand against it. When it came from the south, the south flap went up. That way, the wind blew across the top of the smoke hole instead of down into it, and drew the smoke up and out, the way you might draw air through a straw. And when it rained, he wrote, both flaps were closed over the hole and tied down to a stake in the ground, so that the lodge stayed dry.

And then he said something that every guest in a tipi learned to look for. A tipi that was set up correctly, he wrote, had no smoke inside it at all. All of it went out through the top. And the fire in the center kept the lodge warm, whatever the weather was doing outside.

That is not a small claim. Anyone who has ever sat beside a campfire knows how smoke follows you around. The fact that a family could keep a fire burning inside a closed tent, day and night, for months, and breathe clear air, tells you how carefully these homes were made and managed.

Two Hawks takes hold of the north pole and walks it a little way around the back of the lodge, so that the north flap rises and turns into the breeze. The smoke above the tipi straightens, and thins, and goes up clean again. He sets the end of the pole into the snow, and stamps it firm.

Now here is a surprising thing about the shape of a tipi. It is not a true cone.

If you could look at it from the side, you would see that it leans. The back of the tipi, the side facing west, is steep, almost upright. The front, where the door is, slopes more gently. The whole lodge tilts slightly backward, like a person leaning into a wind. And this is no accident. On the northern Plains, the strongest winds and the worst storms usually came out of the west and the northwest. The steep back of the lodge braced against them, while the door, on the sheltered eastern side, faced away.

The tilt did something else, too. It moved the smoke hole forward, so that it sat a little in front of the middle of the lodge, and the fire was placed under it, a little toward the door. That left more room at the back, the place of honor, where Swift Cloud is sitting now. And it meant that the long poles crossed above the fire at just the right place for the smoke to rise and slip out.

The frame itself is a marvel of simple engineering. The Lakota began with three poles, tied together near the top and raised into a tripod. Then the other poles, eighteen or twenty of them, were laid into the crotch of the tripod one by one, in a set order, until they made a circle on the ground. The best poles were of lodgepole pine, straight and light, often cut in the Black Hills or traded from people who lived near the mountains, and they could be twenty feet long or more. Then the cover, a great half-circle of sewn hides, was tied to one last pole at the back and lifted into place, and wrapped around the frame, and pinned closed above the door with a row of wooden pins, like the buttons on a coat.

A skilled woman, with one or two helpers, could put up a lodge like this in less than an hour. Travelers who watched a whole camp go up in the evening, after a day's march, often wrote that they could hardly believe how quickly it was done.

Historians and archaeologists do not entirely agree about how old the tipi is. All across the northern Plains, there are rings of stones lying in the grass, often in their thousands, and many of them are clearly the stones that once held down the edges of tipi covers, left behind when the camp moved on. Some of these rings are thousands of years old. Some scholars take them as proof that people lived in hide lodges on these plains for a very long time, long before the horse arrived. Others are more careful, and point out that not every ring of stones need have been a home, and that before the horse, when dogs pulled the loads, the lodges must have been much smaller. Both groups agree, at least, that the great tipis of 1870 grew larger once there were horses to drag the poles.

In our own time, a camping tent goes up with light poles of metal or fiberglass that snap together, and a cover of thin synthetic cloth. It is a wonderful thing for a summer weekend. Very few of us would want to spend a January on the northern Plains inside one.

You are standing beside Two Hawks now, in the blue snow, looking up. The moon has cleared the ridge, and it is so bright that each tipi in the camp throws a pointed shadow across the snow, long and sharp. The covers glow faintly from inside, where the fires are burning, so that the whole camp looks like a ring of paper lanterns set down among the trees. Above each one, a thin gray thread of smoke goes up, and up, and disappears into the moonlight.

The cold has deepened, now that the sun is gone. You can feel it in your teeth, and in the tight skin of your cheeks, and in the inside of your nose, where each breath stings a little going in. The snow under your feet has stopped squeaking and started to creak. Far off, among the cottonwoods, there is a sharp crack, loud as a broken branch, and then silence. A tree has split in the frost.

Two Hawks does not even look toward the sound. He has heard it every winter of his life.

Instead, he looks at the moon. He tells you, in a quiet voice, that it is a good moon for traveling, if a man has to travel, because it is almost as bright as day. He is not going anywhere tonight. But in his mind, you can see, he is somewhere else, out on the open prairie, perhaps on another winter night, years ago, riding home through the snow under a moon like this one.

Then he shakes his head, and laughs at himself, and turns back toward the door.

Inside, the tipi is all gold now, because the only light comes from the fire. Her Good Road has added more wood, and the flames are taller, and the painted figures on the liner seem to move a little in the shifting light, the horses and the people and the small tracks of animals walking around the circle of the lodge.

Pretty Shell has taken Little Star out of her cradle and is holding her on her knee, close to the fire, bouncing her gently. The baby is wide awake, staring at the flames with round, serious eyes, the way babies everywhere stare at fire.

Plenty Robes is cutting dried meat into thin strips with a steel knife, on a board across her lap. Swift Cloud has his eyes half closed, and is humming something to himself, very low.

Two Hawks sits down and pulls off his moccasins, which are damp with snow, and sets them near the fire, propped up on two sticks so that the soles face the heat. He has a second pair beside him, of thick buffalo hide with the hair turned inside, and he pulls these on. They are winter moccasins, and his wife made them. They are cut a little large, so that there is room inside for a lining of fur or a wad of dry grass, and they come up high around the ankle.

Little Star reaches out a hand toward the fire, and Pretty Shell catches it, gently, and folds it back against the baby's chest, and says a soft word to her. The baby looks at her sister, and frowns, and then laughs.

And at the door, the flap moves. A gust of cold comes in, and a small boy with snow up to his knees comes in with it.

Young Badger is home.

He stands just inside the door for a moment, blinking in the firelight, the way you do when you come in from bright snow. Then he remembers himself. He walks around the fire to his place, the long way, going around behind his grandmother rather than stepping between anyone and the flames. Even a boy of ten knew that rule. You did not walk between a person and the fire, and you did not walk between the fire and the old man at the back.

His mother looks at him, at the snow on his leggings and the red of his cheeks, and says only one word, which means, roughly, take those off. He sits and peels off his wet leggings and his moccasins, and his grandmother takes his feet in her hands, one after the other, and looks at the toes, and presses them, and feels them for cold. They are pink and sore, but they are not white. There is no frostbite. Plenty Robes rubs them briskly between her palms, and wraps them in a fold of her own robe, and the boy wriggles and complains that it tickles.

You can see why she checks. On the northern Plains in January, a boy who played too long in wet moccasins could lose a toe to the cold without feeling a thing, and every grandmother knew it. The old remedy was to warm the frozen part slowly, never too fast, and never to hold it straight into the flames.

While his feet thaw, Young Badger tells everyone about his afternoon. He tells them how far his rib sled went out onto the ice, farther than anyone's. He tells them how one of the other boys went through a soft place near the bank, up to his waist, and had to be pulled out by his friends, and was not hurt, only very cold and very angry. He tells them that the moon is up, and that it is the biggest moon he has ever seen.

Swift Cloud opens his eyes at that. He says that every boy says that, every winter, about every moon.

Then he asks the boy if he knows the name of this moon.

Young Badger does. It is the Moon of Hard Times. The old man nods, and asks him why it is called that. And the boy thinks for a moment, and says that it is because it is cold, and because the meat runs low, and because the ponies get thin. Swift Cloud nods again, slowly. That is right, he says. And it is also called that so that nobody forgets to be careful.

The Lakota did not count the year in twelve fixed months, the way the soldiers at the forts did. They counted it in moons, from one new moon to the next, and each moon was named for what happened in it, for what the land and the animals and the people were doing. There was a moon when the geese came back, and a moon when the chokecherries ripened, and a moon when the leaves turned brown. The names differed a little from band to band and from family to family. But everyone knew that this moon, the moon of deep winter, was a time to stay close to home, to share what you had, and to keep your fire burning.

Young Badger hears all this, and nods politely, and looks at the dried meat on his grandmother's board.

She laughs, and gives him a strip of it, and he chews it, and holds his bare feet out toward the warmth, and the fire bows a little in the draft from the door, and rises again, steady and bright and warm, at the heart of the lodge.

## Part Four — Around Six O'Clock: The Second Wall

Full dark has come down over the valley now, though it is a bright dark, silvered all over by the moon. The cold is settling into the creek bottom the way water settles into a bowl. And inside the tipi, Her Good Road is doing something that looks, at first, like tidying.

She is kneeling at the edge of the lodge, at the back, where the liner meets the ground. She lifts the bottom edge of the liner a little, and reaches in behind it, and pushes a fresh handful of dry grass into the gap between the liner and the outer cover. Then she moves along the wall, and does it again. Then again.

Why is she stuffing grass behind the wall, on the coldest night of the month?

To answer that, we have to come back to the first of our three questions. Why was a tipi, a tent of poles and hide, warmer on a night like this than a log cabin?

Picture, for a moment, a log cabin of the 1860s, the kind that settlers were building along the rivers further east, and that soldiers built at the frontier forts. It has four walls of logs, laid one on top of another and notched at the corners. The gaps between the logs are filled with chinking, a packing of mud and clay and sticks. It has a fireplace at one end, built of stone, with a chimney. On paper, it is solid and strong. In a Dakota January, it was often miserable.

The chinking shrank and cracked in the frost, and the wind found every crack. The square corners of the room collected cold air that the fire never reached. The fireplace sat against one wall, so that the people close to it were scorched on one side while the far end of the room stayed frozen. And a great deal of the fire's heat simply went up the chimney, which drew warm air out of the room and pulled cold air in through every gap to replace it. On the hardest nights, a pail of water could freeze solid inside such a cabin, a few steps from a roaring fire.

Now look around you, at the tipi.

First, the shape. A cone, or something close to it, holds the most room on the floor for the least amount of wall and roof. There is less surface for the cold to come through. And there are no corners anywhere, nowhere for cold air to gather and sit.

Second, the fire. It is not against a wall. It is in the very center, so that it warms everyone in the lodge equally, all the way around the circle. Its heat strikes the walls on every side, and the walls lean in toward it, and they throw a good part of that warmth back down onto the people sitting beneath them.

And third, the liner. This is the real secret, and it works in two ways at once.

The liner is a second wall of hide, about five or six feet high, tied to the poles all around the inside of the lodge. The outer cover is staked to the ground, but its bottom edge is never quite airtight. Cold air comes in under it. But instead of blowing across the floor and over the sleeping people, that cold air is caught behind the liner. It rises up through the gap between the two walls, past the top of the liner, and up toward the smoke hole. As it goes, it carries the smoke with it and out into the night, the way the draft in a stove pulls the smoke up the pipe. The fire gets the fresh air it needs to burn cleanly. And the people inside, sitting and sleeping below the top of the liner, are in a pocket of still, warm air, out of the draft entirely.

The other way the liner works is the gap itself. A layer of still air between two walls is one of the best insulators there is, and on the coldest nights the women filled that gap with dry grass, or with old robes, to make the layer thicker. That is exactly what Her Good Road is doing now. And outside, against the bottom of the cover, the snow itself, banked up around the lodge, sealed the edges and kept the wind out from beneath.

So the answer to our first question is this. A tipi was warmer than a log cabin because it had no corners, because its fire sat in the middle instead of at one end, and above all because it had two walls instead of one, with a layer of still air between them and a draft that carried the cold up and away over the heads of the sleepers.

In our own time, a well-built house keeps the heat in with the very same idea. Its walls are packed with insulation, and its windows have two panes of glass with a layer of air sealed between them. The people who sewed tipi liners had no word for insulation. But they understood it perfectly.

This explanation comes to us partly from Lakota and other Plains people themselves, and partly from two people who spent much of their lives learning from them. In 1957, a husband and wife named Reginald and Gladys Laubin published a book called The Indian Tipi. They had lived in tipis themselves, through many seasons, with the guidance of Lakota friends and elders, and their book is still the standard work on how a tipi is made and used. They described the liner as the thing that made a tipi truly livable. Without it, they explained, the wind would blow in under the cover, and on cold nights the moisture from people's breath and from cooking would condense on the inside of the cover and drip down on the beds. With a liner in place, what dew there was ran down the outside of the liner and was carried away to the ground.

They also described the ozan, an inner curtain or canopy that could be hung above and behind a bed, like a second, smaller tent inside the first. Some families used one over the places where the old people and the babies slept, for one more layer of still air.

Historians and tipi makers do not entirely agree on which was the liner's first purpose. Some emphasize warmth, and the great draft that carried the cold up and away. Others say that its first job was to keep things dry, to stop the dew and the frost from dripping, and that the warmth came along with it. A Cheyenne dictionary of the early 1900s, for example, defined the inner curtain as protection from rain, from cold and from drafts, all three together. Perhaps the women who first made them would have laughed at the question.

You are sitting now on a folded robe on the left side of the lodge, near Plenty Robes, with your back to the liner. And you can feel it working. Behind you, if you lean back, the liner is cool and taut, and you can hear, very faintly, a whisper of air moving up behind it. But on your face and your hands, the air is still. The fire is a few feet in front of you, and you can feel its warmth on your shins and your knees and your cheeks. Above your head, where the liner ends, it is cooler, and you can see a faint shimmer in the air there, where the heat and the cold are meeting. And far above that, at the smoke hole, the moon is looking in, a small, bright coin among the poles.

The smoke goes straight up past it. None of it comes down to you. The air you are breathing smells only faintly of wood.

It is time for supper now, and supper is soup.

Her Good Road has been preparing it for an hour. The brass kettle hangs from its tripod over the edge of the fire, and in it, simmering, are strips of dried buffalo meat, soaking back to softness, and pieces of dried wild turnip, the prairie turnip that the women dug in early summer and braided into long strings to dry. Plenty Robes has crumbled in a handful of dried chokecherries, which give the broth a deep red color and a sharp, sweet taste. The steam rises into the firelight and carries up toward the smoke hole.

There is also wasna. This is the food that outsiders called pemmican, and it is the great winter food of the Plains. It is dried meat pounded fine between stones, mixed with melted fat and with crushed dried chokecherries, and packed into bags of rawhide. It keeps for months, even for years. A small handful of it is a meal in itself, rich and filling, and it is exactly what a family wants in the Moon of Hard Times.

Nothing of the buffalo was wasted that could be used. Even the bones were broken up with a stone hammer and boiled in a kettle, and the fat that rose to the top was skimmed off and saved, a pale, sweet grease that went into the wasna and kept for the winter. Plenty Robes has a small bladder of it hanging from a pole above her bed. She takes a little of it now on the tip of her knife and stirs it into the soup, and the broth turns rich and golden on top.

The food is served in bowls carved from wood, and in a few bowls of horn, and with spoons made from the horn of the mountain sheep, steamed and bent into shape. Swift Cloud is served first, as the eldest and the man in the place of honor, and then Plenty Robes, and then the others. Many families, before they ate, set aside a small piece of food as an offering, a gift of thanks given back to the spirits and the ancestors, before anyone took a bite for themselves.

Plenty Robes hands you a bowl, too, because a guest is always fed. You hold it in both hands, and the warmth of the wood soaks into your palms. The broth is dark red and smells of smoke and meat and fruit. You sip it. It is salty and sweet and sharp all at once, and the strips of meat are soft and chewy, and the pieces of turnip are pale and starchy, a little like chestnut. It is the best thing you have tasted all day. A warmth spreads down through your chest and into your stomach, and you understand, suddenly, why the people of this camp talk about food the way they do, with such care and such gratitude.

Because in the Moon of Hard Times, food was never simply there. Every strip of this meat was hunted and butchered and sliced thin and dried in the summer sun on racks of willow. Every turnip was dug out of the prairie with a digging stick, one by one, in the moon when the turnips were ready. Every chokecherry was picked by hand along the creeks in late summer, and pounded, stone and all, and dried in small cakes. This one bowl of soup holds the work of a whole year, and of a whole family.

Luther Standing Bear never forgot what it meant to a boy to bring home food. In My People the Sioux he gave a whole chapter to the day he killed his first buffalo, while he was still only a boy, riding out with the men of his camp. He wrote about it the way a grown man writes about the proudest day of his childhood, carefully, in every detail, the horse and the chase and the moment he knew that he had done it, and the welcome he was given when he came home. For a Lakota boy, that day meant that he had begun to feed his people.

Young Badger has not killed his first buffalo yet. He is only ten. But you can see, watching him eat, that he thinks about it often.

Two Hawks eats slowly, and tells his family about the ponies. One of the young mares is favoring her foreleg a little, he says, and he will look at her again in the morning. And he has seen the tracks of a small group of buffalo, three or four of them, in the snow along the ridge to the west, not more than a day old.

At that, everyone in the lodge looks up.

Swift Cloud asks how old the tracks are, exactly. Two Hawks says a day, perhaps a little more. The old man says nothing for a while. Then he says that it might be worth riding out tomorrow, if the weather holds, and the moon says it will. Two Hawks nods.

Young Badger, very quietly, asks if he may come too.

His father looks at him over the rim of his bowl for a long moment. Then he says that they will see.

The boy knows that this is not a yes. But it is not a no, either. He finishes his soup without another word, and his mother, who has been watching all of this without seeming to, hides a smile behind her hand.

Her Good Road lays a fresh stick of ash on the fire, and the flames rise up, steady and clear, and the shadow of the kettle swings across the painted liner like a slow, dark moon.

## Part Five — Around Seven O'Clock: Quills, Sinew and the Circle

The soup bowls have been wiped clean and stacked by the door. The kettle has been moved to the edge of the fire, where the last of the broth keeps warm for anyone who wants it later. And now, in the long hours between supper and sleep, the lodge turns to its evening work.

How do you sew a pair of moccasins, or a whole tipi cover, without a single spool of thread?

Plenty Robes is about to show you. Beside her, rolled in a scrap of hide, is a bundle of long, pale, stiff fibers, almost like dried grass. This is sinew. It comes from the long tendons that run along either side of a buffalo's backbone, beneath the hump. After a buffalo was butchered, the women cut out these tendons, scraped them clean, and dried them flat. A dried tendon is hard and shiny, like a strip of amber. But when you pound it gently with a stone and then pick at it with your fingernail, it splits into dozens of fine, strong threads.

Plenty Robes pulls one thread away from the bundle and puts it in her mouth. She holds it there for a moment, to soften it. Then she draws it out, wet and supple, and rolls one end into a sharp point between her fingers. As the sinew dries, it will stiffen again, and the point will act like the tip of a needle.

She does not use a needle. She uses an awl, a slender spike, which she keeps in a little case of quilled hide that hangs from her belt. In her mother's day, the awl was made of sharpened bone. Hers is of iron, traded for long ago, set in a wooden handle. She pierces a small hole through the two pieces of hide she is joining, and then pushes the stiff point of the sinew through the hole, and pulls it tight. Then another hole, and another stitch.

She lets you hold a thread of the sinew. It is thinner than you expected, and strong, much stronger than it looks. You pull on it with both hands, as hard as you dare, and it does not break. It is a little damp from her mouth, and it smells faintly of hide. As you watch, the end she rolled to a point begins to dry and stiffen in the warm air near the fire, until it is as sharp and firm as the tip of a quill. She takes it back from you, and threads it through the next hole, and draws it tight, and the two edges of hide close together under her fingers as neatly as the two halves of a shell.

It sounds slow. In her hands, it is not slow at all. And sinew has a wonderful quality that no cotton thread has. When it gets wet, it swells a little, and closes the holes it passes through. And as it dries, it shrinks, and pulls the seam even tighter. A tipi cover sewn with sinew, or a pair of moccasins, grew more watertight in the rain, not less.

Across from her, Pretty Shell is doing finer work. On a scrap of soft deerskin in her lap, she is learning quillwork.

This was the great art of the Lakota women, long before glass beads came from the traders. The quills come from the porcupine, and Plenty Robes keeps them sorted by size in small bags of gut, the long ones from the back and tail, the fine ones from the belly. They were dyed in boiling water with roots and berries and bark, red and yellow and black, and by 1870 with dyes from the traders as well. To work them, a woman held a few quills in her mouth to soften them, as Plenty Robes did with the sinew. Then she drew each one out and flattened it, by pulling it between her teeth or pressing it with her thumbnail, until it lay flat and shiny like a strip of colored ribbon. Then she folded and wrapped the flattened quills around the sinew stitches, one after another, in rows, to make bands of color across the hide.

Pretty Shell's band is uneven. One of her red quills has split. Her grandmother leans over, and looks, and says nothing at all, only takes the girl's hand and shows her again, slowly, how to fold the quill over the stitch and tuck its end under the next one. The second time, it lies flat.

Then Plenty Robes does something you do not expect. She takes a single quill from her bag, a white one, and holds it out to you.

You take it. It is about as long as your finger, light and stiff and hollow, with a dark barbed point at one end, so fine you can hardly see the barbs. She shows you, with a small movement of her hand, to put it in your mouth. You do. It tastes of nothing much, a little dusty, a little like a feather. You hold it there between your lips while you count your breaths, and when you take it out it has gone soft and bendable. Plenty Robes nods toward your teeth. You draw the quill between them, gently, and it comes out flat, a thin shining ribbon, white as bone.

You try to fold it over a stitch the way Pretty Shell did. It splits in half.

Plenty Robes laughs, not unkindly, and takes it from you, and drops it into the fire, where it flares for an instant and is gone. Then she hands you another one. You realize that you are going to be here for some time. And you realize, too, that this is exactly how Pretty Shell learned, years ago, when she was smaller than Young Badger, sitting beside this same grandmother, splitting quill after quill, until one day one of them lay flat.

Now here is something surprising about this work. Among the Lakota, quillwork was not only a craft. It was understood to be a sacred gift. Some Lakota women told that the art had first been given to the people in a dream, and that a woman who dreamed of a certain spirit being might become an especially fine quillworker. Older women who were masters of the craft were deeply respected, and a girl who was learning it was learning much more than how to make something pretty. She was learning patience, and care, and how to do a thing right.

Among some Plains peoples, the finest quillworkers belonged to societies of their own, much as the men belonged to societies of hunters and warriors. When a woman finished a great piece of work, a robe covered with quillwork, or a cradle for a new baby, or a tipi liner, she might be honored for it at a feast, and her work would be talked about all through the camp. The cradle that Little Star is lying in now was quilled by Plenty Robes the winter before the baby was born. It took her most of that winter.

Glass beads were changing all of this, in the very years we are visiting. Small seed beads, made in Venice and Bohemia and carried west by traders, were becoming more common in Lakota camps through the 1860s and 1870s. They came in more colors than quills, and were quicker to work. Within a generation, beadwork would cover many of the things that had once been quilled. But tonight, in this lodge, the old art is still alive in Pretty Shell's fingers.

You are sitting beside her, close enough to watch. Her head is bent over the hide, and her black hair falls forward over one shoulder, and the firelight catches the side of her face. She is holding three quills between her lips, the way her grandmother does, and her eyes are narrowed in concentration. Every few moments, she takes one quill from her lips, and flattens it, and folds it, and tucks it in. A red one. A yellow one. A red one. The band grows, very slowly, across the hide.

You can smell the hide, soft and smoky, and the faint sharp smell of the dyed quills. You can hear the fire ticking and settling, and the soft scrape of Plenty Robes' awl, and from outside, very far off, a dog barking once, and then stopping. The warmth of the fire is on your hands. The cold of the night is somewhere behind you, held off by the liner, close but not touching you.

In our own time, most of the things we wear were made by machines, in places we will never see, by people we will never meet. Here, every stitch in every garment in this lodge was made by someone sitting within a few feet of you, by firelight, with a bone or iron awl and a thread of sinew.

On the other side of the fire, Two Hawks is working too. He is making arrows. He has a bundle of straight shoots of chokecherry wood, cut last autumn and dried, and he is scraping them smooth with a knife, and straightening each one by warming it near the fire and bending it gently in a hole drilled through a piece of bone. Swift Cloud is watching him, and from time to time he leans forward and takes a shaft from Two Hawks's hand, and sights along it with one eye, and hands it back without a word, or with a single small nod.

There is another Lakota voice that can help us see this lodge, and it belongs to a man named Black Elk. He was an Oglala, born in December 1863, so that in the winter we are visiting he was a small boy of six, in a camp of his own somewhere to the west. Many years later, as an old man, he told the story of his life to a Nebraska poet named John Neihardt, and the book that came from it, Black Elk Speaks, was published in 1932.

In one famous passage, Black Elk spoke about the shape of things. He said that the power of the world always works in circles. The sky is round, he said, and the earth is round, and the wind whirls, and birds make their nests in circles. And then he spoke of the old homes. "Our tepees were round like the nests of birds, and these were always set in a circle, the nation's hoop, a nest of many nests, where the Great Spirit meant for us to hatch our children."

Listen to that last part again. A nest of many nests. That is what this camp is, under the moon tonight. Twenty round lodges, set in a ring among the trees, each one a nest, each one with its small fire, each one holding a family.

Historians and Lakota scholars have long discussed how much of Black Elk Speaks belongs to Black Elk himself, and how much to Neihardt. Black Elk spoke in Lakota. His son translated his words into English, and Neihardt's daughter wrote them down, and then Neihardt shaped them into the book, and some of its most beautiful passages were his own writing. Some scholars feel that the book tells us more about Neihardt than about Black Elk. Others, including many Lakota readers, have found that the heart of it rings true. The transcripts of the original conversations survive, and they show that the image of the circle, at least, came from the old man himself.

Luther Standing Bear, too, wrote about the women of his family with deep pride. In the first chapter of My People the Sioux, he told his readers that his mother, when she married his father, had been considered the most beautiful young woman among the Sioux. And when he came to describe the tipi itself, he wrote about how it was put up and taken down, and how its flaps were set and its fire was kept, as skilled and careful work, done with a precision that outsiders seldom noticed. That work belonged, above all, to the women. It is the work that Her Good Road and Plenty Robes do every day, and it is the work that Pretty Shell is learning tonight, one quill at a time.

Back in the lodge, Little Star has begun to fuss. Her Good Road lifts her from the cradle and holds her against her shoulder, and walks slowly around the fire, the long way, swaying a little, and hums. It is a low song, three or four notes, over and over. The baby quiets. Her eyes close. Her mother keeps walking, slowly, around and around the fire, the way the moon walks around the sky.

Pretty Shell finishes her band of quills, and ties off the last stitch, and holds it up to the firelight. It is not perfect. But it is straight, and every quill lies flat.

Plenty Robes looks at it for a long time. Then she says that it is good. It is not a word she uses easily, and Pretty Shell knows it. The girl bends her head over the hide again, to hide her face, and begins the next row.

And the fire burns on in the center of everything, round and bright, a small sun at the heart of a nest of hide.

## Part Six — Around Eight O'Clock: The Season for Stories

The moon has climbed higher now, and it no longer looks in at the smoke hole. It has moved off to the south, and its light lies across the outside of the lodge, so that the whole western side of the cover glows a faint, cold silver, while the inside glows gold. The arrows are put away. The quills are rolled up in their bags. And Young Badger has moved closer to his grandmother, and is looking at her in a particular way.

Why does he only ask for these stories in winter?

Among the Lakota, as among many peoples of the Plains, the long tales of the old days were told in the cold months, through the dark evenings of winter. In summer, people were busy, and the days were long, and the camp was always moving. But in winter, the people stayed in one place for weeks at a time, and the nights stretched out for fourteen or fifteen hours, and families sat close together around their fires. It was the season for stories.

Some families held to this very firmly, and said that certain stories, the old sacred ones, should only be told when the snow was on the ground. Different people gave different reasons for it. Some said that in summer the animals and spirits who appeared in the stories were awake and might be listening, and might not like what was said about them. Others simply said that it was the custom, and that the custom was old, and that was reason enough.

So Plenty Robes waits until the work is put away, and the fire is fed, and everyone is settled. Then she begins.

There were many kinds of stories. There were the great stories of how the world came to be the way it is, of the first people, and the gift of the buffalo, and the meaning of the stars. There were stories of the family itself, of grandparents and great-grandparents, of good hunts and hard winters, of who had married whom, and where. And there were the stories about Iktomi.

Iktomi is the spider. He is also, in the stories, a kind of man, a trickster, clever and greedy and vain and always hungry, who is forever trying to fool the other animals and the people, and who is usually fooled himself in the end. Children loved the Iktomi stories, because they were funny. But under the laughter, the stories taught them a great deal. They taught what happens to people who are greedy, or boastful, or who think they are cleverer than everyone else.

One of the best known of these tales was written down in English, in the year 1901, by a young Dakota woman named Zitkala-Ša, whose name means Red Bird. She had grown up on the Yankton reservation, hearing these stories from her mother and the old people around her, and she gathered them into a book called Old Indian Legends. Her version of the story of Iktomi and the ducks goes something like this.

Iktomi is walking along a lake, carrying a great bundle on his back. A flock of ducks calls out to him, and asks what he is carrying. Iktomi tells them it is a bundle of songs. The ducks beg him to sing for them. So Iktomi builds a little lodge of reeds, and invites them all inside, and tells them that they must dance, and that they must keep their eyes closed while they dance, or else their eyes will turn red forever. The ducks dance, and dance, with their eyes shut tight. And Iktomi, of course, is planning to catch them for his supper.

But one little duck opens his eyes, just a crack. He sees what Iktomi is doing, and he cries out a warning, and the ducks fly up and away out of the smoke hole of the lodge. And ever since that day, the story says, that kind of duck has had red eyes, because he opened them.

Iktomi does catch a few ducks, in the end. He roasts them in the ashes of his fire. But while they are cooking, he hears two tree branches creaking against each other in the wind, and he climbs up to stop the noise, and gets his hand caught between them. And there he hangs, stuck fast, while the hungry animals of the prairie come along and eat every one of his ducks, right below him.

When Plenty Robes reaches that part, Young Badger laughs so hard that he has to lie down on his robe. Even Swift Cloud is smiling.

Now, here is the surprising part, and it is not in the story at all. It is what happened after the story.

In many Dakota and Lakota families, a child who had been told a story was expected to tell it back. Not right away, but perhaps the next evening, or the one after, in front of the whole family. The child had to repeat it as closely as possible, with all its details in the right order, and the grown-ups listened, and corrected, and praised. In this way, a story passed from the grandmother into the grandchild's memory almost word for word. It was not only entertainment. It was how the history and the wisdom of a whole people were carried from one generation to the next, with no books at all.

We know this, in part, because a Dakota man who grew up this way described it himself. His name was Ohiyesa, and he was born in Minnesota in 1858, and after a childhood in his grandmother's lodge, he went on to become a doctor, and took the English name Charles Eastman. In 1902 he published a book about his early life called Indian Boyhood. One of its chapters is called Evening in the Lodge. In it, he remembered how, almost every evening, one of his parents or grandparents would tell a legend or a true story of something done in the past, while he listened. And he remembered how, on the following evening, he was usually asked to repeat it. As he described it, the grown-ups listened closely to how well he remembered, and in this way a boy's memory was trained, evening after evening, all through his childhood.

Historians who study oral tradition do not all agree on how exact this kind of memory could be. Some point out that stories told by different families, recorded by different visitors, often differ in their details, and that every teller added something of their own. Others say that this was never the point, and that the core of each story, the shape and the meaning of it, was kept remarkably steady across many generations. The Iktomi stories that Zitkala-Ša wrote down in 1901, for example, are still told today, in recognizably the same form.

In our own time, our stories mostly come to us through screens, and we can watch the same one over and over, exactly the same each time, and never need to remember a word of it. Here, if you want to keep a story, you have to carry it inside you.

You are lying on your side now, on a buffalo robe, with your head propped on your hand, listening. The fire is between you and Plenty Robes, and through the shimmer of heat above it you can see her face, lined and lively, her hands moving as she talks. When Iktomi climbs the tree, her hand climbs too. When the hungry animals come, her fingers creep along the robe in front of her, sly and quiet. When the ducks fly up out of the smoke hole, she flings up both her arms, and everyone in the lodge looks up at the smoke hole, at the real smoke hole above them, as if they might see the ducks going out of it into the moonlight.

The robe under you is thick and soft, and still faintly warm from the ground beneath it, which has been warmed by the fire all day. The hair of the robe is long and curly against your cheek. It smells of smoke and animal and something sweet, the sweetgrass that Plenty Robes keeps folded among the bedding. You can hear the creek ice, very faintly, ticking and groaning to itself as it settles in the cold. And the fire talks quietly, too, in small pops and whispers. Every so often, Her Good Road reaches over without a word and lays one more stick across it, so that the story never has to stop for the dark.

Then it is Young Badger's turn.

Swift Cloud has asked him to tell a story that he heard from his grandmother three nights ago, the one about Iktomi and the blanket he gave as a gift to a great rock, and then wanted back. And the boy sits up straight, and folds his hands, and clears his throat, and begins.

He tells it well. He gets lost only once, near the middle, and his grandmother leans forward and says three words, and he finds his way again. When he finishes, nobody says anything for a moment. Then Swift Cloud nods once, and says that he will make a storyteller one day. Young Badger glows like the fire.

You listen to him, and you find that you are listening the way the grown-ups are listening. Not to the story, exactly, because they have all heard it many times. You are listening for the places where he might slip. You hear the boy's voice go up a little when he comes to the funny part, where Iktomi runs from the rolling rock, and you hear it slow down, carefully, when he comes to the end, the way his grandmother's voice slows down. He is not only telling the story. He is telling it the way she tells it. In twenty years, perhaps, his own children will hear her voice in his.

Then Pretty Shell asks for a different kind of story. Not an old tale, she says, but a true one. She asks her grandmother to tell how she met their grandfather.

Plenty Robes pretends to be shocked. She says that this is not a story for children. Everyone laughs, because it is the answer she always gives, and then, as she always does, she tells it anyway.

She tells how she was a girl of sixteen, and her family was camped on the Platte River, in a summer of good grass and many buffalo. She tells how a young man of another band kept walking past her family's lodge, every evening, wrapped in a blanket up to his eyes, pretending that he was on his way somewhere else. She tells how he played a flute, at night, down by the river, a courting flute of red cedar, and how every girl in the camp knew that it was being played for someone, and how none of them knew for whom. She tells how her mother knew, long before she did.

And she tells how, in the end, he brought horses to her father's lodge, and tied them outside, and went away, and waited. And how her father, after letting him wait two full days, untied them, and led them to his own herd.

She has told this story a hundred times. Every time she tells it, she stops at that same place, and looks into the fire, and says nothing for a little while. Her husband has been gone for some years now. But in the story he is always young, walking past the lodge in his blanket, pretending not to look.

Her Good Road reaches over and lays her hand, for a moment, on her mother's arm.

You lie very still on your robe, and you think that of all the stories told in this lodge tonight, this may be the one that the children will remember longest.

Luther Standing Bear grew up with stories like these, too, and later in his life he wrote a book of them, called Stories of the Sioux, published in 1934. He wrote them down so that they would not be lost. He had heard them as a boy, by the fire, in the winter, in exactly this way.

Two Hawks has been lying back against his backrest through all of this, with his eyes half closed, listening. Now he sits up, and reaches for a stick, and lays it on the fire. And he says that since everyone is telling stories tonight, perhaps the old man would tell one, too.

Everyone looks at Swift Cloud.

The old man is quiet for a long while. He looks into the flames. Then he says that he will not tell a story tonight. Instead, he will do something else. He will bring out the winters.

Pretty Shell and Young Badger glance at each other. They know what that means. Plenty Robes sets down her work. Her Good Road lays Little Star, sleeping now, back in her cradle, and tucks the robe around her.

And Swift Cloud turns slowly, and reaches behind him, into the shadows at the back of the lodge, where a long bundle wrapped in soft hide is lying against the liner. He draws it into his lap, carefully, the way you might lift a sleeping child. And he begins to untie the cords.

The fire leans toward him, just a little, as if it wanted to see.

## Part Seven — Around Nine O'Clock: The Winter the Sun Went Dark

Outside, the moon is high over the valley, and the cold is very deep now. The snow has gone hard and glittering, and the creek is silent under its ice. Nothing moves in the camp except the smoke. Inside the lodge, everyone has drawn in close to the fire, and Swift Cloud is slowly unrolling the bundle across his knees.

What is inside it?

It is a hide. A single buffalo hide, scraped thin and tanned soft, almost white, and on its smooth inner side there are pictures. Small pictures, painted in black and red and brown, each one no bigger than the palm of your hand. There are dozens of them. And they are not scattered at random. They begin in the very center of the hide and turn outward in a spiral, around and around, like the coil of a sleeping snake, or the shell of a snail, until they almost reach the edges.

Each picture is a year. Each picture is a winter.

This is what the Lakota called a waniyetu wowapi, a winter count. In many bands, one man was trusted to keep it, and usually he had been taught by the keeper before him. Every year, as the winter came on, the keeper sat down with the elders of the band, and together they talked over the year that had passed, from the first snow of one winter to the first snow of the next. And they chose one event, just one, to give that whole year its name. It might be a great hunt. It might be a sickness, or a flood, or a visit from strangers, or a sign in the sky. Then the keeper painted a small picture of it onto the hide, next to the picture of the year before.

The picture was not the whole record. It was a key to unlock the memory. The keeper knew the story behind every picture, and could tell it to anyone who asked. He could point to any winter on the spiral and tell you what happened that year, who was born, who died, where the camp was, and what the people ate. The painted hide and the keeper's memory together were a history of the band, going back seventy years or more.

And that is the second of our three questions, coming into view. How did a family tell the years apart? We are very close now.

Swift Cloud does not need to look at the hide to know what is on it. He has kept this count for more than thirty years. It was given to him by his own uncle, who kept it before him, and that uncle had it from the man before him, and the oldest pictures at the center are so faded you can barely see them. But he looks at it anyway, with his finger moving slowly along the spiral, because looking is part of it.

Now here is a real winter count, one that survives, and that you could see for yourself.

In the autumn of 1876, an army officer at a post in Dakota Territory obtained a copy of a winter count that had been kept by a Yanktonai man named Lone Dog. The Yanktonai are close relatives of the Lakota, part of the same great family of peoples. Lone Dog's count covered about seventy winters, beginning with the winter of 1800. The copy that reached the officer had been painted onto a large sheet of cotton cloth, from a hide original. And in 1877, an army officer and scholar named Garrick Mallery published a study of it, with a picture of the whole spiral, and a translation of what each figure meant, as the people who knew it explained.

Lone Dog's count is beautiful. The pictures spiral out from the center, just like Swift Cloud's. And some of them describe events that you can still find in the records of the outside world.

One of them is a picture of a black moon with many small red stars around it. It is the winter of 1833 to 1834. Mallery's translation gives its name in three words: "The stars fell." And it is not a legend. In the early morning of the thirteenth of November, 1833, the whole sky over North America filled with shooting stars, a meteor storm so great that people in the eastern cities woke their neighbors, and some of them were sure that the world was ending. On the Plains, the people saw it, too. It appears in almost every winter count that survives from these years. For historians today, that single picture is a gift. It lets them pin a winter count to the calendar of the outside world, and count forward and backward from it, year by year.

Swift Cloud was about thirty years old, the winter the stars fell. He remembers it. He has told the children about it many times. He tells it again now, because Young Badger asks him to. He tells how he woke in the night, and went outside, and the whole sky was moving, white streaks falling everywhere, in silence, without end, until dawn. And he tells how the old people said that it meant something, and how nobody could agree on what.

And there is another picture in Lone Dog's count that matters especially tonight. It is the winter of 1869 to 1870. That is this winter. The winter we are visiting. Its picture is a black sun, with small stars beside it.

On the seventh of August, 1869, in the afternoon, the moon passed directly in front of the sun, and a great eclipse swept across North America, from the far northwest down to the Atlantic coast. In some places, the sun went completely black. Here, in this valley, if you work out the positions of the sun and moon for that afternoon, you find that around four o'clock almost the whole sun was covered. Nearly all of it. For a few minutes, the summer afternoon went dim and strange, the light turned silver, and the birds fell quiet, as if evening had come early.

The people of Lone Dog's band chose that day to name their year. The winter of the black sun.

Close your eyes for a moment, and you can be there, on that August afternoon, five months ago.

You are standing among the chokecherry bushes along this same creek, with a skin bag at your hip, half full of dark fruit. The air is hot and heavy and smells of dust and sage. Grasshoppers are clicking in the grass. The sky is a hard, pale blue, without a cloud in it, and the sun is high in the southwest, too bright to look at.

And then, very slowly, something begins to feel wrong.

At first it is only the light. It grows thinner, somehow, though there is no cloud. The colors of the grass and the hills go flat and grayish, as if you were looking at them through smoked glass. The heat on the back of your neck fades. The grasshoppers fall silent, one after another, until there is no sound at all along the creek. The shadows of the chokecherry leaves on the ground are no longer soft. They have gone sharp-edged and strange, and in the gaps between the leaves the little spots of light are not round any more. They are crescents, hundreds of tiny bright crescents, scattered over the ground at your feet.

Across the creek, the horses have stopped grazing. They stand with their heads up, ears pricked, looking at nothing. A dog somewhere in camp begins to howl.

You look up, squinting, through your fingers. The sun is no longer round. It is a thin, burning curve of light, like a new moon made of fire, and the sky around it has turned a deep, dusky blue, the blue of evening, in the middle of an August afternoon. For a moment, you think that you can see a star.

Then, slowly, the curve begins to widen again. The light comes back. The grasshoppers start up, hesitantly, one at a time. The horses lower their heads and go back to the grass. And all along the creek, people who had stopped where they stood, women with baskets, children, old men, begin to move again, and to call out to one another, and to talk, all at once, about what they have just seen.

That is the kind of day a winter count was made for.

Swift Cloud lets you hold the edge of the hide now, very carefully, so that you can look closer. It is lighter than you expected, and so soft that it drapes over your fingers like cloth. The paint has worn smooth into the skin. Near the center, where the oldest winters are, some of the pictures have faded almost to nothing, and the old man says that he repaints them, sometimes, very gently, when he can no longer see them well, so that the people who keep this count after him will still be able to read them.

Historians who study winter counts have noticed that counts from different bands do not always agree on which year an event belongs to. The eclipse of August 1869 appears in some counts in the year we would call 1868 to 1869, and in others in 1869 to 1870. Some historians think this is because the bands began their years at slightly different times, at the first snow, which could come early or late. Others think that some keepers, when they came to make new copies of their counts, years later, shifted a picture by a year by mistake. Both may be true. Either way, the counts are not wrong. They are simply counting in their own way.

Swift Cloud has reached the outer edge of the spiral now, the last picture he has painted. It is last winter, the winter that Little Star was born. And beside it, there is a space of empty hide.

He looks at it for a long time.

Then he asks his family what this winter should be called. He does not mean that they will decide it, here, tonight. That will be done properly, with the other old men of the band, when the camp has gathered. But he wants to hear what they think.

Young Badger says at once that it should be the winter of the great moon.

Swift Cloud smiles, and says that every winter has a great moon.

Two Hawks says, slowly, that it should be the sun going dark. That was the thing that everyone saw, every person in every camp, and that nobody will forget. Plenty Robes agrees with him. She says that she was picking chokecherries along the creek when it happened, and that she sat down where she was, in the grass, and did not get up again until the light came back.

Swift Cloud nods. He says that he thinks so too. He will speak for it when the old men meet.

You are sitting very close to him now, close enough to see the hide in the firelight, and the small faded pictures marching around and around it. Some are figures of people, drawn with a few lines. Some are horses. Some are lodges. One, near the center, is a lodge with spots on it, which means a sickness. Another is a buffalo with a little arrow beside it, which means a winter of good hunting. You can smell the hide, very old and very soft, and faintly of smoke, the smoke of seventy winters of lodge fires. The old man's finger moves along the spiral, and his voice moves with it, telling each year by its name. The winter of deep snow. The winter they camped at the forks. The winter of many horses. The winter the stars fell.

And now we can answer our second question. How did a family tell one year from another, with no paper calendar on any wall? They gave each year a name. Not a number, but a name, taken from the one thing that everyone in the band would remember about it. They painted that name onto a hide, in a spiral that grew outward winter by winter, and a keeper held the whole history in his memory and could tell it on any night. And so a person did not say that he was born in 1858. He said that he was born in the winter of deep snow, or the winter of many horses. And everyone knew exactly which winter he meant.

That is how Luther Standing Bear told it, too, at the start of his book. His people did not give him a date. They gave him a season and the name of a year.

Swift Cloud points to a picture near the outer edge, and looks at Young Badger, and says that this one is his. The winter he was born. Then he moves his finger along, and points to another, and looks at Pretty Shell. And then to the last one, and looks over at the cradle, where Little Star is sleeping.

Every person in this lodge has a place on the spiral.

Young Badger leans over the hide, and looks for a long time at the small faded picture of his own winter, as if he has never really seen it before. Then he looks at the empty space beyond the last picture. The space where this winter will go.

The old man begins to roll the hide back up again, carefully, from the outside in, so that the last winters are wrapped around the first ones, and ties the cords, and lays the bundle back in its place against the liner, behind him, in the shadows.

And the fire burns on in front of him, the same fire that burned in front of his uncle, and his uncle's uncle, in lodges like this one, under winters like this one, a small light carried forward, year after year, through all the names.

## Part Eight — Around Ten O'Clock: The Ponies Under the Moon

The winter count is wrapped and put away. The children have gone quiet, the way children do after a long evening, when the warmth of the fire begins to pull at their eyelids. And Two Hawks gets up, and reaches for his robe, and pulls on his winter moccasins, because before anyone in this lodge can sleep, there is one more thing to be done.

He has to see to the horses.

You go with him. You stoop through the low door and straighten up outside, and the cold strikes your face like a sheet of water. It is far colder now than it was at sunset. The air is perfectly still, and so clear that the moon seems close enough to touch, and every tree along the creek stands out against the snow in sharp black lines, each twig drawn as fine as a hair. Your breath goes up in a great white cloud, and hangs there, and you walk through it.

What does a horse eat, on a night like this, when every blade of grass for a hundred miles is buried under snow?

Part of the answer is that a Plains pony knows how to find grass for itself. Horses will paw down through the snow with their front hooves, scraping it aside, to reach the dry grass underneath. The ponies of the Lakota were small and tough and shaggy in winter, and they could live through cold that would have killed many of the larger, grain-fed horses of the soldiers. Through most of the winter, in good years, they fed themselves this way, ranging out from the camp by day under the watch of the boys and young men.

But when the snow was deep, or crusted over with ice so that a horse could not break through it, or when the herd had grazed the valley bare, there was another food. It was standing all around the camp. It was the cottonwood trees.

The bark of the cottonwood, especially the young, tender bark of the smaller branches, is sweet and soft, and horses love it. And so, all through the winter, the men and boys and often the women went out with axes, and cut down branches and young trees, and dragged them to where the horses could reach them. The horses stripped the bark with their teeth, and ate the twigs and buds, and left the branches peeled and white on the snow. That is what Two Hawks was doing this afternoon, when you heard his axe down among the trees.

Now here is a surprising thing, and it was written down by a very famous traveler indeed.

In the winter of 1804 to 1805, the explorers Meriwether Lewis and William Clark spent the cold months at a small fort they had built beside the earth-lodge towns of the Mandan and Hidatsa peoples, on the upper Missouri River, a few hundred miles north of this valley. And on the twelfth of February, 1805, Lewis wrote about the horses in his journal. His own horses had come back from a long, hard trip, exhausted and hungry, and he offered them meal, the ground corn that he would have fed a horse back east, soaked in water to make it soft. They would not eat it. Instead, he wrote, they chose the bark of the cottonwood. And he went on to explain that this was what the horses of the peoples around him lived on through the winter, the branches and bark of the cottonwood, which the women cut down and brought to them.

Lewis was surprised. He was a Virginian, and he seems never to have expected a horse to turn down good corn. But the horses of the upper Missouri knew what was good for them in winter. And so did the people who kept them.

Historians do not entirely agree on how much a horse could live on cottonwood alone. Some accounts from the Plains describe it as the usual winter food of the herds, eaten every day for months, and say that the horses came through the winter on it in fair condition. Others say that it was a food for hard times, which could keep a horse alive but not strong, and that a herd fed too long on bark grew thin and weak by spring. And at least one trapper of the 1830s complained that the bark of one kind of cottonwood, the narrow-leaved kind that grows further west, was so bitter that his horses would not touch it at all. Probably all of them were right, about their own horses, in their own valleys.

The pony herd is grazing a little way downstream, in an open place among the trees where the wind has kept the snow thin. You and Two Hawks walk toward them along the creek bank, and the snow creaks under your moccasins, and the ponies lift their heads as you come. There are perhaps twenty of them belonging to this lodge, which makes Two Hawks a man of some standing, though not a rich one. Their coats are thick and rough, with frost glittering on their backs and on the long hair of their manes. Their breath steams in the moonlight, and their eyes catch the light, and shine.

Two Hawks speaks to them as he walks among them, low and steady, the same few words over and over. He puts his hand on a neck here, a shoulder there. He is counting them, though he does not seem to be counting. He is looking at each one, at the way it stands, at its eyes and its ears.

The young mare who was favoring her foreleg this afternoon is standing a little apart from the others, near the heap of cottonwood branches. Two Hawks goes to her, and runs his hand slowly down her leg, from the shoulder to the hoof, and lifts the hoof, and looks at it in the moonlight, and presses gently with his thumb. The mare stands still for him and lets him look. Then he sets the hoof down, and straightens, and nods. It is a bruise, he tells you, nothing worse. She will be sound in a few days.

You stand beside him with your hands tucked inside your robe, and you look at the herd. The ponies are stripping the bark from the cottonwood branches he cut this afternoon, and you can hear the sound of it, a steady, gentle tearing and crunching, like someone eating an apple in the next room. The bark comes away in long pale strips. The peeled wood beneath gleams white in the moon. There is a sweet, green smell in the cold air, a smell almost like spring, coming from the torn bark. One of the ponies, a gray, comes over to you, curious, and pushes its soft nose against your sleeve, and blows a long warm breath across your hand, and goes back to its branch.

Luther Standing Bear loved horses all his life. In My People the Sioux, he wrote about watching his father prepare to go out and catch wild horses, when he was a small boy, and you can feel, in the way he tells it, how much he admired his father, and how much he wanted to be like him. Through the whole book, his father is the man he measured himself against. And a Lakota man's horses were, in a way, his wealth and his pride and his freedom all at once. With good horses, a family could follow the buffalo, and move camp, and carry its lodge and its belongings wherever it needed to go. Without them, it was stuck.

The Lakota had not always had horses. The horse came to the northern Plains in the 1700s, traded northward from people further south, who had them from the Spanish settlements in New Mexico. Before that, the people moved their camps with dogs, which pulled small loads on frames of poles called travois. The horse changed everything. Within a few generations, it had become so much a part of Lakota life that it was hard to imagine the world without it. One of the Lakota words for the horse meant something like sacred dog, or mysterious dog.

Two Hawks has finished his walk among the herd. He stands for a moment at the edge of the trees, looking out at the valley, at the moonlit snow, at the far white line of the bluffs. Then, without turning his head, he speaks to someone behind you.

Young Badger is there. He has followed you out of the lodge, quietly, with his robe wrapped around him up to the eyes, and his feet in his dry moccasins. He thought nobody had noticed. His father had noticed the moment he stepped through the door.

Two Hawks asks him which pony he would ride tomorrow, if he came.

The boy stares at him. Then he points, without a word, to a small spotted gelding grazing at the edge of the herd. Two Hawks looks at the pony for a long moment, as if he is thinking about it. Then he says that it is a good choice. That pony has sense.

And that is all he says. But the boy understands. Tomorrow, he is going.

He does not shout. He does not run. He walks back to the lodge beside his father, very straight, very slowly, the way a man would walk. Only once, when he thinks nobody is looking, he gives a small hop in the snow.

Two Hawks pretends not to see it.

You walk back through the camp together, the three of you, and you look around you as you go. Most of the lodges are still glowing from inside, a soft red now rather than gold, as fires everywhere burn down toward embers. From one of them comes the low murmur of voices, a man and a woman talking quietly. From another, a baby crying, and then a woman's voice humming, and then the crying stops. Somewhere at the far side of the ring, an old man is singing to himself, a slow song that rises and falls and rises again, and stops in the middle, and does not start again.

The dogs of the camp lie curled in hollows they have made in the snow, close against the lodge covers, where a little warmth leaks through. One of them lifts its head as you pass, and looks at you, and puts its head down again. They are not pets, exactly, these dogs, and they are not quite guards. But they are part of the camp, as they have been for longer than anyone can remember, and if anything strange came near in the night, they would know it first.

Above the camp, the smoke of twenty fires rises in twenty thin gray threads, straight up into the moonlight, and far above, so high that you can barely see them, the threads seem to bend and drift together, and join, and go on as one faint haze across the stars.

At the door, the boy stops, and turns, and looks back at the herd, at the small spotted pony stripping bark in the moonlight. Then he ducks inside. Two Hawks follows him, and you follow Two Hawks.

The warmth meets you at the door like an embrace. The fire is lower now, a bed of red embers with a few small flames dancing on top, and its light is deeper and redder than before, and it fills the lodge with a soft glow, like the inside of a shell held up to the sun.

Her Good Road looks up from the fire, and sees her son's face, and knows at once. She says nothing about it. But a little later, while the boy is pulling off his outer moccasins, she reaches into a parfleche and takes out a small pouch of soft hide, and begins to fill it with wasna, a little at a time, pressing it down with her thumb. Then she ties it closed, and lays it beside his robe, where he will find it in the morning.

She takes up his winter moccasins too, the ones of buffalo hide with the hair turned in, and turns them over in her hands, and looks at the soles, and at the seams. One seam at the heel has begun to open. She takes out her awl, and a thread of sinew, softens it in her mouth, and mends it, in a few quick stitches, without a word, while the boy watches her.

Two Hawks, settling back against his backrest, says that the boy will ride beside him, and will do exactly what he is told, and will come back to camp the moment he is told to. Young Badger says yes to all of it. He would say yes to anything tonight.

Swift Cloud, from the back of the lodge, says that when he was ten he was not allowed to go, and that he went anyway, and that his father did not speak to him for four days. Plenty Robes says that she remembers it, and that it was five days, not four. And everyone laughs, even Young Badger, and the old man laughs most of all.

The embers settle, and a little flame rises from among them, and lights all their faces at once, and sinks again, and glows.

## Part Nine — Around Eleven O'Clock: Robes Around the Fire

The camp is almost silent now. The old man who was singing has stopped. The voices from the other lodges have faded away, one by one. Only the smoke still rises, thinner than before, into a sky that is so bright with moonlight that the stars have nearly disappeared. And inside the lodge, the family is making its beds.

Where does everyone sleep, in a single round room, with a fire in the middle?

The answer is that they sleep around the edge, in a ring, like the spokes of a wheel. Each bed lies along the curve of the wall, against the liner, with the sleeper's head toward the wall and the feet toward the fire. That way, the feet, which feel the cold first, are nearest to the warmth, and the head is sheltered behind the liner, out of any draft. And each person has their own place, the same place every night, so that nobody ever has to wonder where to lie down.

The beds themselves are simple, and very warm. On the ground, under everything, is a thick layer of dry grass, or of sage, or of small branches, to lift the sleeper off the cold earth. On top of that lie buffalo robes, with the hair side up, soft and deep. And on top of the sleeper, more robes, with the hair side down, so that you lie between two layers of thick fur, as warm as a buffalo itself on the open prairie.

At the head of some of the beds there is a backrest. It is a kind of tall, sloping mat, made of many thin, straight willow rods laced together side by side with sinew, and hung from a tripod of poles. By day, people lean against them, the way you would lean against the back of a sofa. By night, they shelter the head of the bed. Swift Cloud's backrest is old and dark with smoke, and it is the finest in the lodge, with quilled bands at the top and long fringes of hide at the bottom.

Pretty Shell is spreading her robes now, on the left side of the lodge, next to her grandmother's. She smooths the grass beneath them with her hands, and pulls out a stalk that is poking up, and lays the top robe ready, folded back. Young Badger is doing the same on the other side, next to his father, though less carefully. He is still thinking about tomorrow. He keeps looking at the pouch of wasna beside his bed.

You have a place too. Plenty Robes has made it for you, near the door, on a thick bed of grass, with two robes, one beneath and one above. Guests often slept near the door, nearer the cold, but you do not mind. You sit down on your bed, and pull off your moccasins, and set them by the fire as Two Hawks did, propped on sticks, with the soles toward the heat.

There is a lovely passage in a later book by Luther Standing Bear, a book about the beliefs and the daily life of his people, which he called Land of the Spotted Eagle. It was published in 1933, six years before he died. In it, he wanted to explain to his readers how the Lakota had felt about the country they lived in, these plains and hills and creek bottoms, the same country that lies outside this lodge tonight.

He wrote that his people did not think of the great open plains, and the beautiful rolling hills, and the winding streams with their tangled growth, as wild. And then he wrote this. "Only to the white man was nature a 'wilderness' and only to him was the land 'infested' with 'wild' animals and 'savage' people. To us it was tame."

Listen to that last line again, very quietly. To us it was tame.

When outsiders looked at this valley in January, they saw a frozen wilderness. A cold, empty, dangerous place, a long way from anywhere. But to the family in this lodge, it is not empty at all. It is home. Every tree along the creek is known. Every hill has a name. The cold is not an enemy, exactly. It is simply winter, which comes every year, as it always has, and which you meet with a good lodge, and a good fire, and dry wood, and plenty of robes.

Historians have looked closely at how Luther Standing Bear's books were made. He wrote in English, which was his second language, and he worked on each of his books with the help of an editor, who shaped and polished the text for publication. Some scholars have asked how much of the final wording is his, and how much theirs. Others, including many Lakota readers today, point out that whatever an editor may have smoothed, the memories, the opinions and the voice are unmistakably his own. He was a man who had lived in both worlds, the world of the tipi and the world of the city, and who chose very deliberately to speak up for the first one.

Now the family is lying down.

Swift Cloud goes first, as the eldest. He lies down slowly, in his place at the back of the lodge, behind the fire, with his head toward the liner and his feet toward the embers. His sister's daughter spreads his top robe over him and tucks it in at his shoulders, and he says something to her, too quietly for you to hear, and she smiles. His wife died many years ago, and he chose then to live with his sister and her family, and they have been glad to have him.

Plenty Robes lies down next, on the left, and Pretty Shell beside her. The girl lies on her side, facing the fire, with her hands tucked under her cheek. Her eyes are open, and she is watching the embers. She is thinking, perhaps, about her quillwork, and about her grandmother's word, good.

On the right side of the lodge, Young Badger has pulled his robe right up over his head, so that only a tuft of black hair shows at the top. His father lies down beside him, on his back, with his hands folded on his chest, and looks up at the smoke hole.

Her Good Road is the last to lie down. Before she does, she goes around the lodge once more, the long way. She checks the door flap, and ties it closed. She looks at the kettle, and moves it a little further from the fire. She looks at the liner all the way round, and pushes in one more handful of grass at a place where she can feel a thread of cold coming through. Then she lifts Little Star from her cradle, and lies down with the baby in the crook of her arm, under the same robe, so that the child is held against her warmth all night.

The empty cradle stands propped against the backrest beside her bed, with its quilled bands of red and yellow catching the last of the firelight. Plenty Robes made it, the winter before the baby was born. In the daytime, it is the baby's whole world, a snug bag of soft hide laced up around her, lined with soft dry moss and cattail down, so that she can be carried on her mother's back, or hung from a saddle, or propped up against a lodge pole to watch the family at work. At night, she needs no cradle. She has her mother.

For a little while, there is whispering.

On the left side of the lodge, Pretty Shell turns toward her grandmother, under the robes, and asks her something in a very small voice. You cannot hear the question. But you hear Plenty Robes answer, just as softly, and you hear the girl give a little breath of a laugh, and then ask something else. The old woman answers that too. It is the kind of talk that happens only in the dark, when the faces cannot be seen and the voices are low, the kind of talk that a girl of thirteen saves up all day for her grandmother. Perhaps it is about quills. Perhaps it is about a young man who walked past the lodge this afternoon, rather slowly, wrapped in a blanket up to his eyes. Plenty Robes does not say. She only reaches out a hand from under her robe, and finds the girl's hair in the dark, and strokes it, once, twice, and draws her hand back in.

On the right side of the lodge, Two Hawks speaks quietly to his wife across the embers. He tells her that they will go west along the ridge in the morning, where he saw the tracks, and that they will be back before the sun is high, and that the boy will stay with him. She says that she knows. He says that he will keep the boy's feet dry. She says that she knows that, too. Then neither of them says anything for a while.

Young Badger, under his robe, is pretending to be asleep. He is listening to every word.

The quiet settles down over the lodge like snow. You notice how the sounds change as it does. There is no wind. There are no voices any more. The fire has stopped crackling, and only hisses, very softly, now and then, when a little sap boils out of a stick. The grass beneath the beds creaks once, as someone turns over, and then is silent. Outside the cover, the dogs are silent, and the horses are silent, and the creek is silent under its ice.

In a lodge like this one, nobody needed to be told to be quiet at night. Lakota children learned very early, as Luther Standing Bear himself remembered, that silence was a kind of courtesy, and that a person who could sit still and say nothing was a person to be respected. They learned to listen before they spoke, and to listen to the night, too, to the wind and the animals and the trees. So now, as the lodge goes still, the stillness is not empty. It is full of listening.

And then everything is still.

You lie down in your own bed, near the door, and pull the top robe up to your chin.

It is heavier than any blanket you have ever slept under. A whole buffalo robe weighs a great deal, and its weight presses down on you, gently, everywhere at once, like a hand on your shoulder. The fur is thick and soft against your neck and your cheek. It smells of smoke and grass and, very faintly, of animal, a warm, clean smell. Beneath you, the grass crackles softly when you move, and then settles, and is quiet.

Your feet are toward the fire. You can feel its warmth on the soles of them, through the robe, steady and kind. Your head is near the liner, and you can hear, if you listen closely, the faint whisper of cold air rising behind it, past your head, up toward the smoke hole. But none of it touches you. You are inside a pocket of still, warm air, like a seed inside a husk.

You look up. Above you, the poles rise into the dark, crossing and gathering, lit red from below by the embers. The painted figures on the liner are dim now, the horses and the people barely visible, as if they too were going to sleep. And at the very top, framed by the poles, the smoke hole is a small square of silver, where the moonlight is.

Somewhere outside, very far away, the ice on the creek gives a long, low groan, as it settles in the cold. Then silence again.

You breathe in, and out, and the robe rises and falls with you.

A little later, a tree cracks in the frost, down among the cottonwoods, sharp and sudden. Nobody in the lodge stirs. They have heard that sound every winter of their lives. It is only the trees, talking to themselves in the Moon of Hard Times.

You can hear the family breathing now. Swift Cloud's breath is slow and deep, with a small catch at the end of each one. Plenty Robes breathes softly, like a whisper. Young Badger is restless still, turning under his robe, and then turning back. Little Star makes small sounds against her mother's shoulder, the sounds a baby makes while she is falling asleep, and then she makes no sound at all.

The fire is low, a deep red bed of embers with only one or two small flames still dancing above it. Its light rises and falls, rises and falls, very slowly, as if it, too, were breathing.

Her Good Road lies with her eyes open for a while, watching it. She is the one who will wake if it needs her. She has been the keeper of this fire for many winters, and her body knows, even in sleep, when the embers are getting low, and when the cold is creeping in. Later tonight, she will rise, and tend it. But not yet.

For now, the fire glows quietly at the center of the ring of sleepers, red and warm and steady, and the whole lodge breathes around it.

## Part Ten — Around Midnight: The Keeper of the Fire

The moon stands at the top of the sky now, as high as it will go tonight, and its light falls steeply down through the smoke hole, a pale column of silver reaching toward the embers. Everyone in the lodge is asleep. Everyone except one.

Who keeps the fire alive, through all the long hours of a winter night?

Her Good Road opens her eyes. Nothing has woken her. No sound, no cold. But some part of her, the part that has kept this fire for many winters, has noticed that the embers are growing dim, and has called her quietly up out of sleep.

She lies still for a moment, listening. Little Star is breathing softly against her arm, warm and heavy. Then, very slowly, she slides her arm out from under the baby, and tucks the robe back around her, and sits up.

The cold meets her at once, on her shoulders and her neck, where the robe falls away. It is much colder in the lodge now than it was at bedtime, though still far warmer than the night outside. She pulls a smaller robe around her shoulders and leans toward the fire.

The embers are a dull, dark red, glowing under a soft gray skin of ash. There are no flames at all. She takes up a short stick and stirs them, very gently, and as the ash falls away, the red beneath it brightens, and a little warmth rises up into her face. Then she reaches for the wood she set ready at bedtime. Not the quick, bright cottonwood of the evening. She chooses two thick pieces of ash, dense and heavy, that will burn slowly. She lays them across the embers, close together, and blows on them, once, softly, the way you might blow on a cup of something hot.

A small flame rises. It licks along the underside of the wood, and catches, and holds. She watches it for a while, to be sure. Then she rakes a little ash back up around the sides of the new wood, to slow it down, so that it will smolder and glow for hours instead of blazing up and dying.

This was the secret of a winter fire. Not a big fire. Never a big fire. A tipi fire that was too large would throw out more smoke than the smoke hole could carry, and send sparks flying up toward the poles and the cover, and roast the people nearest it while doing nothing for the rest. A good fire was small and steady, fed often, with the right wood, and in the deep of the night it was banked under ash, so that the heat was kept in the embers like a seed in the ground, ready to wake again in the morning.

And the person who kept it was, very often, the woman of the lodge. It was her fire, in her home. She knew its moods, the way she knew the moods of her children.

You are awake, too. You do not quite know why. Perhaps the small sound of the stirring stick woke you, or the new light on the poles above. You lie in your bed near the door, under the heavy robe, and you turn your head, and watch her.

She is kneeling by the fire with the small robe around her shoulders, and her long hair loose down her back, and her face lit from below, red and gold. She is not hurrying. Every movement is slow and sure. She lays a stick, and waits, and watches, and lays another. The light rises gradually around her, warming the poles, the liner, the sleeping shapes of her family all around the ring. You can see Swift Cloud's white hair against his dark robe. You can see the tuft of Young Badger's hair, still poking out from under his. You can see Pretty Shell's hand, curled near her face. All of them are sleeping in the slow warm light of the fire that she has just woken.

The new wood smells sweet and clean as it begins to burn, a little like honey, a little like tea. The warmth comes to you slowly, across the floor, and settles over your bed like a second robe. You feel it on your forehead, and on your eyelids.

You listen to the small sounds she makes. The soft knock of a stick against a stone. The rustle of the robe around her shoulders as she leans forward. The faint breath she gives the embers, no louder than a sigh. And then the new sound of the fire itself, a quiet, steady whispering, like someone talking very softly in another room. It is the most comforting sound you have ever heard. It means that someone is awake, and that everything is being looked after, and that you do not have to be the one to do it.

Little Star makes a tiny sound in her sleep, behind her mother, a sound like a question. Her Good Road turns her head at once, and listens, and waits. The baby is quiet again. Her mother turns back to the fire.

Her Good Road looks across at you, and sees that you are awake, and gives a small nod, as if to say, it is all right, go back to sleep. Her eyes are kind, and a little tired, and very calm, the eyes of someone who has done this many times before. Then she turns back to the fire.

There is another line by Luther Standing Bear, in Land of the Spotted Eagle, that is worth hearing in the quiet of this hour. He was writing about the elders of his people, and about why they had always kept so close to the earth, and to the living things around them. "The old Lakota was wise," he wrote. "He knew that man's heart, away from nature, becomes hard."

He had seen both kinds of life. He had grown up in a lodge like this one, with a fire in the center and the trees and the creek and the horses close around him. And he had lived for many years in cities, in Philadelphia and, later, in Los Angeles, among a great many people who had never in their lives slept on the ground, or heard the ice on a creek settle in the night, or known the name of the moon. He did not think the cities had made those people happier. He thought the old way, the way of the lodge and the fire and the open land, had something to teach them. That was why he wrote his books.

Historians and Lakota speakers do not all agree on how the old Lakota year was counted. Some say there were twelve moons, named and remembered in order, the way we count twelve months. Others say there were thirteen, because thirteen moons come nearer to filling a whole year, and that winter had four of them, while spring and summer and autumn had three each. The names of the moons differed from band to band, too, and even from family to family. Perhaps the truest answer is that the people did not count the moons to fill a calendar. They named them for what was happening in the world around them, and watched for the signs, and the year took care of itself.

Her Good Road has finished with the fire. It is burning low and steady now, two thick sticks glowing under their ash, with one small flame. She sits back on her heels.

For a little while, she does not move. She looks up at the cover of her lodge, glowing faintly where the firelight touches it, and her eyes follow the long seams that run across it from the ground to the smoke hole.

She remembers making it. It was four summers ago, in the moon when the chokecherries are ripe, after a good hunt, when there were hides enough. She had tanned them through the spring, one after another, until her arms ached at night. Then she had cut them, carefully, to the shape she carried in her head, the shape her mother had taught her. And then she had invited the women. Her mother, and her aunts, and her friends, and an old woman of the camp who was known for her skill at cutting and fitting a cover. They had come one bright morning and sat in a long row on the grass, with the hides spread out before them, and sewn all day, with awls and sinew, talking and laughing, and by evening the cover was done.

Afterward, she had given them a feast. That was the custom. A woman who made a new lodge fed the women who had helped her, and fed them well, and the old woman who had led the cutting was given a gift of her own. Her Good Road remembers that day as one of the happiest of her life. She remembers the smell of the new hides, and the sound of all those voices, and the moment, the next morning, when she and her mother raised the cover onto the poles for the first time, and it fitted.

The first winter, the cover was still pale, almost white. It glowed like a lamp when the fire was lit inside. Now it is darker, smoked to a warm brown near the top, and it glows golden instead of white. The smoke has made it better. It sheds the rain, and it dries soft after the snow, and it holds the warmth. In a few more years, it will grow thin and worn, and she will make another, and this one will be cut down into smaller things, moccasin soles and bags and a little cover for a child's play lodge. Nothing will be wasted.

But not for a long time yet.

Then she does something you did not expect. She gets up, very quietly, and goes to the door, and unties the flap, and lifts it, just a little, and looks out.

You can see past her, through the gap, into the night.

The camp lies under the moon, white and still. The lodges stand in their ring, each one with its faint glow, each one with its thin thread of smoke. Beyond them, the bare cottonwoods stand along the frozen creek, their trunks pale, their shadows black on the snow. And beyond the trees, rising up on the north and west, the long, dark line of the bluffs, steep and high, with the moonlight on their edges, standing between the valley and the open plains like a great wall. Above them, the sky is pale and enormous, and very still.

The cold comes in through the gap, and touches your face, and the fire leans away from it. It is a different cold from the cold of the evening. It is deeper and stiller, and it smells of nothing at all, not smoke, not snow, not trees, as if the night itself had frozen clean. The stars that the moon has not hidden are small and hard and white. Somewhere down by the creek, a pony shifts its weight, and the snow creaks under its hoof, and then there is no sound again.

Her Good Road looks at the bluffs for a long moment. She has done this every winter night of her life, the way her mother did, and her mother's mother. She is looking at the weather, at the sky, at the trees and the snow and the shape of the land. She is checking, without thinking about it, that the world is where she left it.

It is. Nothing has changed. The valley is quiet. The bluffs stand between her family and the wind.

She lets the flap fall, and ties it again, and comes back to the fire, and holds her hands out over it for a moment, to warm them. Then she goes to her bed, and lies down, and gathers Little Star back into the crook of her arm, and draws the robe up over them both.

The baby stirs, and turns her face against her mother, and sighs, and is still. Her small fist opens on the robe, and stays open, soft as a leaf.

You hear Her Good Road's breathing slow, and slow, until it is the same as everyone else's in the lodge.

You lie and listen for a while to all of them breathing together. There is something very peaceful in it. Seven people asleep in a ring around a fire, under one roof of hide, in the middle of a frozen valley, in the Moon of Hard Times, with the whole huge cold night pressing in around them. And not one of them is cold.

Above you, in the smoke hole, the moon has moved a little further across, and its silver light now falls slantwise across the poles, and touches the top of the liner on the far side, and lights up one small painted horse, which seems, for a moment, to be galloping across the hide all by itself in the moonlight.

Your eyes are growing heavy again. The warmth of the robe is pulling at you. The smell of the woodsmoke and the grass and the fur wraps around you. The fire glows at the center of the lodge, low and red and faithful, tended and banked and safe, and it will keep you all warm until morning.

## Part Eleven — Around Three in the Morning: Why This Bend of the Creek

The moon has crossed to the western side of the sky now, and it hangs above the high bluffs, lower and more golden than before. Its light no longer falls into the lodge. The smoke hole is a square of deep, soft blue, with one or two stars showing in it at last. And at the back of the lodge, in the place of honor, an old man has opened his eyes.

Why is Swift Cloud awake?

Only because he is old. Old men sleep in pieces, a little at a time, and wake in the deep of the night, and lie still, and think, and then sleep again. There is nothing wrong. He is warm. His robe is heavy and soft around him, and the fire his niece banked at midnight is still glowing in front of him, a low red eye under its gray lid of ash.

He turns his head and sees that you are awake, too. And because it is the middle of the night, and because the old and the wakeful have always talked together in the middle of the night, in a low voice, so as not to wake the others, he speaks to you.

He asks if you have wondered why the camp is here.

You have. It was our third question, all the way back at the beginning of the evening. Of all the miles of river and creek in this country, why did the camp choose this one bend of this one small creek to spend the winter in?

Swift Cloud tells you, slowly, one thing at a time, the way he would tell a child.

First, he says, look at the bluffs. They rise high and steep on the north and on the west of the valley. And on the northern Plains in winter, the killing winds come from exactly there, out of the north and the northwest, across hundreds of miles of open land with nothing to stop them. A camp out on the high prairie would take the full force of every storm. But down here, in the bend, below the bluffs, the wind passes over the top. The camp sits in a pocket of still air, the way you lie in a pocket of still air behind the liner.

Second, he says, look at the trees. The cottonwoods grow where there is water, along the creeks and rivers, and in all this open country there are very few other places with so much wood in one spot. Wood for the fires, every day, all winter long. And bark for the horses, when the snow is too deep for them to reach the grass.

Third, the grass itself. Here in the bottom, sheltered from the wind, the snow lies thinner in places, and the grass grows tall along the creek in summer and stands dry and sweet under the snow in winter. The ponies can paw down to it. Out on the open plains, the wind packs the snow hard as stone, and there is nothing to eat.

Fourth, the water. Under the ice of the creek, the water is always running, all winter, and a hole cut in the morning will give water to the whole camp. And here, at this bend, where the creek runs deep against the bank, there is a place where a spring comes up under the ice and keeps it thin, so that the girls can open it easily even on the coldest morning.

And fifth, he says, the buffalo and the deer know all of this too. They come down into the sheltered breaks and bottoms in the hard months, out of the wind, just as the people do. A camp in a place like this is a camp with food not far away.

Historians who study the old winter camps of the Plains do not entirely agree on how often the same places were used. Some point to winter counts and to the memories of elders that mention a favorite wintering place again and again, year after year, over many generations. Others note that the buffalo moved, and the grass was grazed down, and the wood was used up, so that a band might have to choose a new valley every few winters. Probably both were true. Each band had its favorite places, and went back to them when it could, and chose new ones when it had to.

Far up the Missouri, the Mandan and Hidatsa peoples knew the same wisdom. When the explorers Lewis and Clark spent the winter of 1804 and 1805 beside their towns, their journals noted how much those peoples relied on the wooded river bottoms, and on the cottonwoods that gave the people their firewood and their shelter, and the bark that carried their horses through the cold.

So that is the answer to our third question. The camp chose this bend of the creek because the bluffs stand between it and the north wind, because the cottonwoods give wood for the fires and bark for the horses, because the grass lies sheltered in the bottom, because the water runs all winter under the ice, and because the animals come here too. It is not one reason. It is all of them together, in one place, and the old people knew how to read the land and find it.

Swift Cloud is quiet for a while after he finishes. Then he says one more thing. He says that his uncle brought the camp to this bend, in a winter long ago, before Swift Cloud was born. And it is marked on the count. The winter they first came to the bend under the bluffs.

Then he smiles, in the dark, and says that is enough talk for one night. And he settles back into his robe.

There is nothing more to learn tonight. Nothing more to do. Only to rest.

You watch the old man settle. He pulls the robe up a little around his shoulders. He turns his face toward the fire. He closes his eyes. His breathing slows, and slows, and grows deep, with that small catch at the end of each breath. After a little while, there is no catch at all. Just the slow, even sound of an old man sleeping.

He is asleep. Swift Cloud is asleep, warm in his place at the back of the lodge, with his winter count lying safe behind him against the liner.

You lie still and listen to the others.

Plenty Robes is sleeping on her side, with one hand stretched out from under her robe toward her granddaughter. She has not moved in hours. Her breathing is so soft that you can barely hear it.

Pretty Shell is sleeping beside her, curled small, with her knees drawn up. Her face is turned toward the fire, calm and smooth, and very young. She is dreaming, perhaps, about red quills and yellow ones, lying flat.

Across the lodge, Young Badger has finally stopped turning. He has pushed the robe down from his face in his sleep, and he lies on his back now, with one arm flung out, and his mouth a little open. Beside his bed, the pouch of wasna waits. In the morning he will ride the spotted pony. But for now he is far, far away, deep in the soft dark of a boy's sleep.

His father sleeps beside him, quiet and still, a big dark shape under a big dark robe.

And Her Good Road sleeps with Little Star in the crook of her arm, the two of them wrapped together under one robe, so that you can hardly tell where the mother ends and the baby begins. The baby's breath is quick and light. The mother's is slow and deep. Together they make a kind of soft music.

Seven people, in a ring, asleep.

The lodge is warm. Not hot, but warm all the way through, the warmth of a home that has had a fire at its center all day and all night. The liner holds the warmth in. The grass behind it holds the warmth in. The snow banked around the outside holds the warmth in. And the cover above, smoked and brown and strong, keeps the whole night out.

You can feel how safe it is here.

The fire under its ash gives a small sigh, and settles. A little puff of gray rises from it, and drifts up, slowly, toward the smoke hole, and goes out into the blue.

Outside, nothing moves. The ponies stand together under the cottonwoods, close against one another for warmth, their heads low, their eyes half closed. The dogs lie curled against the lodge covers, noses under their tails. The creek is quiet under its ice. The moon is sinking slowly toward the bluffs, and the bluffs stand dark and steady, as they have stood for longer than anyone can say, between the valley and the wind.

And in the whole camp, in twenty lodges set in a ring among the trees, twenty small fires are glowing under their ash, and families are sleeping around them, safe and warm.

Somewhere out there, too, beyond these bluffs, in another valley, in another camp, a small boy of thirteen months is sleeping. He is wrapped in a soft robe beside his mother, in a lodge much like this one, with a fire much like this one glowing at its center. His name, for now, is Plenty Kill. He does not know yet that he will one day be called Luther, or that he will travel very far from here, or that he will write all of this down. He knows only that he is warm, and that his mother is near, and that the fire is glowing.

He is asleep. Let him sleep.

Back in our own lodge, the shadows lie soft and deep around the edges, where the firelight barely reaches. The painted horses on the liner have gone dark. The poles rise up into the dimness, and cross, and meet, and you can barely see them now. Only the small blue square of the smoke hole shows where they gather, far above, with one star in it.

You watch the star for a while. It does not move. Or if it moves, it moves so slowly that you cannot see it go.

The warmth of the lodge is all around you. It is in the robe, and in the grass, and in the air you breathe. It is in the ground beneath you, which has been warmed by the fire for many days and many nights, and gives a little of that warmth back to you now, slow and steady, through the grass and the fur.

Somewhere in the lodge, someone shifts in their sleep, and the grass whispers, and is still again.

The fire gives one small tick, like a clock.

You think about the long evening behind you. About the water carried up from the creek in a girl's hands, and the smoke flaps turned to the breeze, and the soup in its wooden bowl. About the quill that split, and the quill that lay flat. About the ducks that flew out of the smoke hole, and the old woman's story of a young man in a blanket, walking slowly past her mother's lodge. About the winter count, unrolled across the old man's knees, with all its small faded pictures spiraling out from the center, and the one empty space at the edge, waiting for the black sun. About the ponies stripping bark in the moonlight, and a boy giving a small, secret hop in the snow.

All of it is behind you now. All of it is done. And all of it was good.

There is nothing left to do. Nobody needs you to be awake. The fire does not need you. It has its keeper, and she has tended it, and it will glow until morning. The horses do not need you. They have their bark, and each other. The children do not need you. They are deep in sleep, and their mother is beside them.

You can rest.

Let your hands lie loose. Let your shoulders sink into the soft fur of the robe. Let your feet, toward the fire, feel its warmth, gentle and steady. Let your breathing go slow and deep, like the old man's, like the mother's, like the whole lodge breathing together in the dark.

The night is very quiet now. So quiet that the quiet itself seems to have a sound, soft and deep and wide, like the sound of snow falling, though no snow is falling.

The moon is sinking toward the bluffs. Its light lies long and soft across the snow, and touches the tops of the lodges, and the frost on the cottonwoods, and the backs of the sleeping ponies, and turns them all to silver.

The camp sleeps.

You let your eyes close.

The robe is heavy and soft on your shoulders. The fur is warm against your cheek. The grass beneath you is quiet. You can smell the woodsmoke, faint and sweet, and the sage, and the hide. You can hear the breathing of seven people, slow and even, all around you in the dark.

You do not need to think about anything. There is nothing to carry now. The questions have all been answered. The fire is banked. The door is tied. The night is long, and still, and kind.

Let your breathing slow, the way theirs has. In, and out. In, and out.

And in the center of the lodge, under its soft gray blanket of ash, the fire glows on, red and patient and alive, keeping all of you warm.

## Part Twelve — Around Five in the Morning: The First Gray Light Over the Bluffs

The hours go on, slowly, softly, the way hours go in the deepest part of a winter night. Four o'clock. Five. Six. Nobody counts them. In the lodge, everyone sleeps, and the fire glows under its ash, and the world outside lies still and white and silent under the setting moon.

In these last dark hours, the lodge is at its quietest. Even the fire has stopped speaking. There are no more small pops and hisses, only a deep, soft glow under the ash, like the glow of a coal held in cupped hands. The air is still. The robes are still. Seven people breathe slowly in the dark, and their breathing has become so even and so gentle that it is hard to tell one from another. It is simply the sound of the lodge itself, breathing.

Far away, many years from this night, an old man in California sat at a table and remembered lodges like this one. He remembered how the smoke went up and out through the top, so that the air inside stayed clear. He remembered how the fire in the center kept the whole lodge warm, whatever the weather was doing outside. He had been born into this world in the Moon when the trees crack, and he carried it with him all his life, wherever he went, and he set it down on paper so that it would not be forgotten. His name was Luther Standing Bear. And on this night, in a lodge somewhere beyond the bluffs, he is still only a baby, asleep beside his mother, with the fire glowing in front of him.

He is warm. Everyone is warm.

Outside, the moon has nearly reached the line of the western bluffs. Its light lies long and low across the valley, and the shadows of the lodges stretch out toward the east, long and pointed and blue, across the snow. The frost has gathered on everything in the night, on the cottonwoods and the lodge poles and the tips of the smoke-flap poles, and on the frozen hide of each door, so that the whole camp is outlined in white, as delicately as if someone had drawn it with chalk.

Nothing is moving out there. Nothing needs to.

What is the first thing to change, when the night begins to end?

It is not a sound. It is not the light, not yet. It is something smaller than either. It is the color of the smoke hole.

All night, that small square at the top of the lodge has been blue, a deep, dark, soft blue, with a star or two in it. And now, if you were awake to watch it, you would see it begin to change. It is so slow that you cannot see it happening. You can only see that it has happened. The blue is a little paler than it was. A little grayer. The star is gone.

You are awake to watch it. You have been drifting in and out of sleep for a long time, warm and heavy, half dreaming. And now you open your eyes, and look up, and see the gray.

You do not move. You do not need to. You lie still under the heavy robe, and watch the square of sky grow lighter, very slowly, like water clearing in a pool.

The lodge around you is very quiet. The fire is low, a bed of dark red under a thick gray blanket of ash. It has kept its warmth all night, just as Her Good Road meant it to. If you hold out your hand toward it, you can feel the heat still rising from it, gentle and steady, like the warmth from a sleeping animal.

The sleepers lie around it in their ring, just as they lay at midnight. Nobody has moved very much. The robes rise and fall, rise and fall, slowly. Their shapes are softer now, as the first gray light begins to come down through the smoke hole and fill the lodge, so faintly that it is less a light than a lessening of the dark.

You can see the poles again now, rising up into the grayness, crossing, meeting. You can see the faint shapes of the painted horses on the liner, coming back out of the darkness one by one, as if they had been out all night and were only now returning home. You can see the dark bundle of the winter count, lying where the old man left it, against the liner behind his bed.

Outside, the moon has not yet set. It hangs low over the western bluffs, pale and large and very soft now, fading as the sky behind it fills with light. And in the east, behind the door of the lodge, behind the bare cottonwoods and the frozen creek, the sky is turning the color of a dove's wing, and then the faintest, faintest pink.

The cold is still deep. Outside, every twig of every cottonwood is furred with frost, white and delicate, and the snow lies smooth and blue, and the creek is silent under its ice. The ponies stand together under the trees, close and quiet, their backs white with frost. The dogs are still curled in their hollows. Not one of them has stirred.

In a little while, the camp will wake. It will begin, as it always begins, with a woman.

In a little while, Her Good Road will open her eyes, and know at once, without thinking, that it is almost morning. She will slide her arm out from under Little Star, and tuck the robe back around the baby, and sit up into the cold. She will lean toward the fire, and take up her stick, and rake back the gray ash from the embers. And there, underneath, red and alive, the fire will be waiting for her, exactly as she left it.

She will lay on a few sticks of dry cottonwood, quick and bright. She will blow on them, once, softly. And the flames will rise, and catch, and climb, and the lodge will fill with gold, and with warmth, and with the clean sweet smell of new woodsmoke. The smoke will rise up through the smoke hole in a fresh gray thread, and anyone looking at the camp from the bluffs would see it go up, and know that this lodge had come safely through the night.

Then Plenty Robes will wake, and sit up slowly, and complain about her knees, and smile. Pretty Shell will stir, and stretch, and remember her quillwork, and the word her grandmother said. Swift Cloud will wake in his own time, and lie still for a while, looking up at the poles, and then sit up and ask what the weather is doing.

And Young Badger will open his eyes, and remember, all at once, what day it is. He will sit straight up out of his robes, and look at the pouch of wasna beside his bed, and then at his father. And his father, who will already be awake and lacing on his winter moccasins, will look back at him, and nod.

Little Star will wake last of all, or perhaps first of all, with a small cry, and her mother will lift her and hold her close to the new fire. The baby will blink at the flames, with her round, serious eyes, the way she did yesterday afternoon. And then she will laugh.

The door flap will be untied and lifted, and the cold will come in, bright and clean, and someone will go down the trodden path to the creek with a hatchet and two brass kettles to open the ice. The dogs will stand up out of their hollows and shake the frost from their coats. The ponies will lift their heads. And the sun will come up at last over the eastern ridge, small and white and very bright, and lay its first long light across the camp, and the covers of twenty lodges will glow gold all at once.

But not yet. Not yet.

For now, everyone is still asleep.

You lie in your warm bed by the door, and you look at them, one by one, in the gray first light.

Swift Cloud is asleep at the back of the lodge, in the place of honor, his white hair spread on the dark fur of his robe, his winter count resting safe behind him, with its spiral of names, and its one empty space at the edge, waiting.

Plenty Robes is asleep on the left, on her side, with her hand still reaching out from under her robe toward her granddaughter, the hand that has made more robes than she can remember, and that showed you how to flatten a quill.

Pretty Shell is asleep beside her, curled small and warm, with her knees drawn up, and her face calm, and her band of red and yellow quills rolled safe in its scrap of hide beside her bed.

Young Badger is asleep on the right, on his back, with one arm flung out, and his mouth a little open, dreaming, perhaps, of a small spotted pony, and a long ride along the ridge, and the tracks of buffalo in the snow.

Two Hawks is asleep beside him, a big dark shape under a big dark robe, quiet and still, his hands folded on his chest.

Her Good Road is asleep with Little Star in the crook of her arm, the two of them wrapped together under one robe, the mother's breath slow and deep, the baby's quick and light. The keeper of the fire, resting beside it. Her hair is spread across the robe behind her, and her face, in the gray light, is peaceful, and her hand rests lightly on the baby's back, even in sleep, so that she will feel the smallest stir. And Little Star, eight months old, the youngest person on the spiral, sleeping warm against her mother's heart, in her first Moon of Hard Times.

Seven people, under one roof of hide, in the bend of a frozen creek, below the bluffs, among the cottonwoods.

All of them safe. All of them warm.

Around them, the lodge holds them all, the way a cupped hand holds a small bird. The poles lean in over them from every side. The liner stands close around them, keeping off the cold. The grass behind the liner, and the snow banked against the cover, and the cover itself, smoked brown and strong, all keep the long night out. Four summers of a woman's work, and many winters of her mother's teaching, and the knowing of more generations than anyone can count, are folded around these seven sleepers like a robe.

None of them is thinking about any of that. They are only sleeping.

The gray light deepens a little more in the smoke hole. It is the softest light there is, the light that comes before color, and it touches everything in the lodge gently and equally, the faces and the robes, the kettle by the fire, the bundle of the winter count, the quilled cradle, the small pouch of wasna beside a boy's bed. It does not wake anyone. It only rests on them.

A very small sound comes from the fire. Under the ash, one of the thick sticks of ash wood has burned through in the middle, and the two halves settle against each other, softly, with a sound like a sigh. A thin thread of smoke rises from the place where they touch, and climbs, very slowly, up past the poles, up toward the gray square of sky, and goes out.

Then the lodge is still again.

You let your eyes close once more, and you do not open them for a long, long time.

The lodge is quiet. The lodge is warm. The night has passed safely, every hour of it.

You can rest, too. There is nothing more to do, nothing more to carry. Let the robe be heavy on your shoulders. Let your hands lie loose. Let your breathing go slow and even, the way theirs has. In, and out. In, and out.

You are safe here. You are warm. You can stay as long as you like.

Outside, the gray light grows, very slowly, over the bluffs. The moon sinks lower in the west. The frost glitters on the cottonwoods. The ponies dream on their feet. The smoke rises, thin and gray and straight, from twenty lodges in a ring.

And here, in the center of this one lodge, in the gray first light, under its soft blanket of ash, the fire is still glowing, red and patient and alive, just as it was when we found it yesterday afternoon. It has kept this family warm all through the longest, coldest night of the Moon of Hard Times. It has kept its small red heart burning in the dark, inside its two walls of hide. And it is waiting, quietly, for the morning.

If you enjoyed spending this winter night in the lodge of Her Good Road and her family, a like or a subscription would mean a great deal, but only if you truly enjoyed it. And if there is a time or a place you would love to spend a night in, leave it in the comments, because your ideas truly help.

Next time, we will visit a very different kind of home, on a very different kind of night, somewhere far from these plains.

Sleep well, friend. Good night.

### Verification notes

- **Computed, not searched:** for a camp at about 43.75° N, 102.0° W (a creek valley above the White River, in present southwestern South Dakota), using PyEphem, in local mean solar time: full moon on 17 January 1870 (about 100 percent lit); sunset about 4:51 pm; moonrise about 5:10 pm; the moon still above the western horizon at dawn (moonset about 8:19 am on 18 January); civil dawn about 6:57 am and sunrise about 7:29 am on 18 January. For the solar eclipse of 7 August 1869, PyEphem gives maximum at this site at about 4:06 pm local mean time, with the sun about 33° high and about 98 percent of its diameter covered (a deep partial eclipse, not total, here). The narration says "almost the whole sun was covered."
- **Verbatim, checked by search this session:** Standing Bear, *Land of the Spotted Eagle* (1933): "Only to the white man was nature a 'wilderness' and only to him was the land 'infested' with 'wild' animals and 'savage' people. To us it was tame." (confirmed across several reproductions; one copy has "infected," the common reading "infested" is used). Standing Bear, *Land of the Spotted Eagle*: "The old Lakota was wise. He knew that man's heart, away from nature, becomes hard." (wording varies slightly among reproductions; the narration quotes only these two sentences, in the most common form). Black Elk Speaks (1932): "Our tepees were round like the nests of birds, and these were always set in a circle, the nation's hoop, a nest of many nests, where the Great Spirit meant for us to hatch our children." Mallery's gloss for Lone Dog 1833–34: "The stars fell." Painting titles by Catlin, as held by the Smithsonian American Art Museum.
- **Paraphrased, not quoted (exact wording could not be confirmed by search):** everything else attributed to Luther Standing Bear in *My People the Sioux* — his birth reckoned by season (the month when the bark of the trees cracks) and the name of the year, later worked out as December 1868; his mother considered the most beautiful young woman among the Sioux when she married; the tipi chapter's account of the wind flaps (north wind, north flap raised; both closed and tied to a stake in rain), of a correctly pitched tipi having no smoke inside, and of the central fire keeping the lodge warm in any weather; his chapter on his first buffalo; his memory of watching his father prepare to catch wild horses. The narration presents all of these as "he wrote that…" or "he told his readers that…", never in quotation marks. The narration's remarks that Standing Bear remembered silence as a courtesy are paraphrased from *Land of the Spotted Eagle* without a fresh search. Catlin's remark that the Crow made the most beautiful lodges, dressing the hides nearly white and quilling them, and that the great lodge stood about twenty-five feet high, is paraphrased (only an ellipsized quotation was found). Eastman's "Evening in the Lodge" (nightly stories, the boy asked to repeat them) is paraphrased; the exact sentence could not be confirmed. Lewis's 12 February 1805 entry (horses refusing moistened meal, preferring cottonwood bark; winter diet of cottonwood bark and branches cut by the women) is paraphrased. Zitkala-Ša's two Iktomi tales are retold in summary; the animals that eat Iktomi's ducks are left unnamed in the narration because the version's exact animal was not confirmed.
