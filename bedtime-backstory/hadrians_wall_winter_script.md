## Part One — Around Half Past Two in the Afternoon: The Last Gray Light on the Crags

The wind comes up the face of the crags from the north, and it does not stop. It is December in the north of Britain, in the year one hundred and thirty-six, and a long gray wall of stone runs along the top of the ridge as far as you can see in both directions. Below it, the ground falls away to frozen lakes and brown moor. Inside a fort on that ridge, in one small stone room, an iron brazier is already glowing, red at its heart, and eight men will spend the whole night beside it.

Not the legions of the picture books, all scarlet cloaks and gleaming helmets, marching in step toward some famous battle. Just one ordinary barrack room on one ordinary night.

Without a few old tricks, you would not have lasted until morning.

So while we sit with these men, I would like you to carry three small questions with you. The first is this. What did a Roman soldier actually wear under his tunic in a British winter? The second. Why were the soldiers on this Wall, for the most part, not Italians at all? And the third. When these men sat down to write a letter home, what did they write home for?

These are not three separate questions. They are three doors into the same small room, and by the time the brazier burns low in the small hours, we will have walked through all of them.

Now, let your head grow heavy on the pillow. Let your shoulders drop and loosen, the way a soldier's shoulders drop when he finally sets down a shield he has carried all day. Your eyes may close whenever they like. You do not need to remember anything. You only need to listen, and to be warm. So let us begin.

The afternoon light on the ridge is thin and pale, the color of old pewter. At this time of year, this far north, the sun barely climbs above the southern hills at noon, and by half past two it is already leaning down toward the west. The shadows of the towers stretch long across the frozen grass.

The fort sits on the slope just behind the Wall, a neat rectangle with rounded corners, like a playing card laid on the hillside. Its stone walls enclose a little over five acres. There are gates on all four sides, a headquarters building in the middle, granaries with raised floors to keep the grain dry, a fine house for the commanding officer, and long, low barrack blocks lined up in rows like loaves on a baker's shelf.

The Romans called this place Vercovicium. Today we call it Housesteads, and it is still there, on its ridge in Northumberland, with the wind still blowing through it.

Here is a small question to begin with, one we will answer before this part is done. Why would anyone build a fort in a place as exposed as this?

Let us look first at the Wall itself, because it is the reason all of these men are here.

In the year one hundred and twenty-two, the emperor Hadrian came to Britain. He was a restless emperor, a traveler, a man who liked to see his frontiers with his own eyes and to walk among the soldiers who guarded them. Soon after his visit, work began on a barrier that would run right across the narrow neck of northern Britain, from the river Tyne in the east to the shallow waters of the Solway in the west. It stretched about seventy-three of our miles, which the Romans counted as eighty of their own.

It was not built all at once, and it was not built by slaves. It was built by the soldiers of the legions, the professional heavy infantry, working in gangs that each took a stretch of the line. We know this because they left their names. Small stone slabs set into the Wall record which century of which legion built which length, like signatures at the bottom of a long letter.

Every Roman mile along the Wall there was a small fortlet with a gate through it, which we now call a milecastle. Between each pair of milecastles stood two small stone towers, the turrets. And then, while the work was still going on, the plan changed. The army decided to bring the big forts right up onto the line of the Wall itself, so that whole regiments would live along it. Housesteads is one of those forts, built in the years after the Wall began.

So this is the answer to our small question. The fort is here, on this cold and windy ridge, because the Wall is here. And the Wall is here because the ridge is a natural rampart. The rock beneath it is a long, hard band of dark stone that geologists call the Whin Sill. On its northern side, it drops away in sheer cliffs. Anyone coming from the north has to climb. Anyone standing on top can see for miles.

The soldiers paid for that view with the wind.

Now here is something that surprises most people when they first hear it. Hadrian's Wall, for all its fame, was not the most important thing on this frontier when the Roman army first arrived. Before the Wall, there was a road. It ran east and west a little to the south, and it was strung with forts and watch posts. Historians call it the Stanegate. One of the forts on that road, only a couple of miles from where we sit tonight, was called Vindolanda.

And Vindolanda matters to us more than any other place in Britain, because of what came out of its mud.

In the early nineteen seventies, archaeologists digging there found thin slivers of wood, about the size of a postcard and no thicker than a coin. When they were peeled apart and photographed under infrared light, faint writing appeared on them, in ink. They were letters. Lists. Accounts. Party invitations. Requests for beer. They had been written by the soldiers and officers and families of the Vindolanda garrison, and by people who wrote to them, and then thrown away, and the wet, airless ground had kept them safe for nearly two thousand years.

I need to be honest with you about something before we go on, because it matters on this channel. Those letters were written around the years ninety-seven to one hundred and five. That is about a generation before the Wall was begun. So the men who wrote them never saw Hadrian's Wall. They lived on this same frontier, in this same weather, in the same kind of army, and they are the closest voices we have to the men in our barrack room tonight. But they are older voices, and I will always tell you so when I use them.

We also need a voice from the Wall's own time, or close to it, to tell us why it was built. And there is really only one.

Long after Hadrian's death, a writer put together a collection of emperors' lives that we now call the Augustan History. It was set down a good two and a half centuries after the Wall was begun, and it is not always a careful book. But in its life of Hadrian, the writer tells us that the emperor went to Britain, set many things right there, and was the first to build a wall, eighty miles long, to divide the barbarians from the Romans.

That one sentence is almost all the ancient world tells us directly about the purpose of this enormous thing. Eighty miles. Barbarians on one side, Romans on the other. Everything else, we have had to work out from the stones.

Now imagine that you are standing at the north gate of this fort, a little after half past two on this December afternoon. You are wearing everything you own, and you are still cold. The wind finds the gap between your collar and your neck and slides in like cold water. Your fingers, inside the wool wrapped around them, have gone stiff and stupid. When you breathe, the air is so sharp that it stings the inside of your nose.

You look north through the gate. There is nothing there but land. Long brown slopes, black patches of bog, a gray lake with a skin of ice at its edges. A few threads of smoke rise from somewhere far off, where the people of the hills are lighting their own fires against the dark. It is very quiet. The only sounds are the wind and, somewhere behind you, the ring of a hammer in the fort workshops.

You turn and look back into the fort. The roofs of the barracks are dark and wet. Smoke drifts from small vents and chinks, gray against the gray sky. There is a smell on the air of charcoal and wet wool and something cooking. A man hurries past you, his cloak pulled tight, a bundle of firewood under one arm. He nods at you and does not stop.

You follow him down the narrow lane between two long barrack blocks. The doors along the wall are low and close together, each one opening into a pair of rooms. He ducks into one of them. You duck in after him.

And there it is. In the back room, on the beaten floor, an iron brazier stands on its short legs, a shallow bowl of glowing coals, with a faint shimmer of heat rising above it. The warmth reaches your face first. Then your hands. You hold them out toward it without thinking, the way cold people everywhere hold out their hands to a fire.

Think, for a moment, of how you came home today. A door, perhaps a key, a switch on the wall, and the room was already warm, because a machine somewhere in the house had been quietly keeping it so all day. Here, every hour of warmth has to be carried in by hand, piece by piece.

Historians do not entirely agree about what the Wall was for. For a long time, it was pictured as a great military rampart, a line where Roman soldiers stood shoulder to shoulder against invading hordes. Many scholars now think it was something more like a controlled border, a way of watching and managing who crossed, of collecting tolls and keeping an eye on traffic and trade, with gates at every mile for exactly that purpose. Others answer that a wall fifteen feet high, with a deep ditch before it, was surely built with fighting in mind as well. The truth may simply be that it was both, at different times and in different places.

Now, let us meet the men of this room.

This barrack room never quite existed under one name, but every detail of their night is real.

The man who came in with the firewood is Vatto. He is thirty-three, broad and red-cheeked, with forearms like a blacksmith's. He comes from the country of the Tungrians, on the river Meuse, in what is now Belgium. In this room, he is the keeper of the brazier and the cook, not because anyone ordered him to be, but because he is better at both than anyone else, and everyone knows it.

Sitting on the end of one of the low beds, mending a strap, is Ammius. He is thirty-eight, gray at the temples, with a quiet, careful voice. He is the decanus, the leader of these eight. He has served for nineteen years. The others listen when he speaks, partly because he is in charge and partly because he rarely says anything that is not worth hearing.

The oldest man in the room is Agilis. He is forty-four and has served for twenty-four years, which means that in a little over a year, if all goes well, he will be discharged with honor. He was born near the mouth of the Rhine, among the Batavians, and he still says a few words in his own old language when he is tired or annoyed.

By the brazier, with a writing tablet on his knee, is Felicio, twenty-seven, the clerk of the group. He can read and write well, and so he spends much of his life writing things for other people, including, on many evenings, their letters.

Crescens is twenty-four and works in the fort's leather workshop as a shoemaker. Senecio is thirty and serves as a medical orderly, a man who can bind a wound and knows which herbs settle a stomach. Butto is twenty-nine, a big, slow-speaking man from the hills along the Meuse, who works at the kilns and sings, very badly, when he thinks he is alone.

And the youngest is Vindex. He is eighteen. He has been in the army for less than a year. And he is the only man in this room who was born in Britain, in the little settlement outside the fort at Vindolanda, the son of a soldier and a British mother.

We should be honest about one more thing. We do not know for certain which unit held this fort in the years around one hundred and thirty-six. Later, in the century that followed, a cohort of Tungrians was stationed here for a very long time. Tonight, our eight men belong to an infantry cohort of the same kind, raised among the peoples of the lower Rhine and the Meuse, and that is as close to the record as we can stand.

Eight men. Two small rooms. One brazier.

Vatto kneels down beside it and feeds in a few pieces of fuel, and the coals take them with a soft, pleased crackle.

## Part Two — Around Half Past Three in the Afternoon: The Bath-House Before Dark

The sun has slipped lower now, and the gray in the sky has a faint yellow edge to it, like the rim of a lamp. In a little while it will be gone.

Ammius stands up, stretches his back, and says the words that every man in the room has been waiting for. Their century has the baths this afternoon, until the light goes. Five of them reach for their cloaks before he has finished speaking.

Here is a small question for this part of the evening. Why would a soldier, on a freezing December afternoon, walk out of a warm room into the wind, only to go and get wet?

The answer is that the Roman bath was not really about washing. Or not only. It was about heat.

At most forts on this frontier, the bath-house stood outside the main walls, down the slope a little, usually close to running water. There was a good reason for keeping it outside. A bath-house had a furnace, and a furnace, burning hour after hour, was one of the most dangerous things in a Roman fort. If it set fire to a roof, better that it was a roof standing on its own.

Inside, the bath was a little journey from cold to hot. You undressed in a changing room and left your clothes on a shelf or in a niche in the wall. You might go first into a cold room, with a cold plunge for the brave. Then a warm room. Then a hot room, where the air was thick and wet and the floor was so warm that the bathers wore wooden-soled sandals to keep from scorching their feet.

The trick that made all of this possible was the hypocaust. The floor of each heated room was not laid on the ground. It was raised on short pillars, little stacks of square clay tiles, so that there was a hollow space beneath it, perhaps two feet deep. At one end, outside the building, a furnace burned. Its hot air and smoke were drawn through the hollow space under the floor, warming the tiles from below, and then up through hollow box-shaped tiles built into the walls, warming them too, before escaping through vents near the roof.

So the bathers sat inside a building that was, in effect, a giant warm oven. The heat came from the floor and from the walls all around them at once. On a day like this one, that was worth the walk through the wind.

Somebody, of course, had to feed that furnace. The stoking was hot, dirty, tiring work, done in a low chamber outside the building, where a man crouched in front of a fire mouth and kept shoveling in fuel for hours on end. On a winter day, he was perhaps the warmest man in the whole fort, and also the sootiest. His face would be black by the end of his shift, and his arms would ache, and the bathers on the other side of the wall would never see him at all.

A bath-house also had to be kept clean, and that was no small task either. The floors were scrubbed. The drains were cleared. The water had to be carried or channeled in, and the dirty water let out again, down the slope and away. The whole building was, in its way, a small machine, and like any machine it needed tending every day it ran.

And because it was warm, it was also the place where men talked. A Roman bath was something like a club, or a tavern without the wine. You sat for a long time in the warm rooms, with nothing much to do but sweat, and so you gossiped. Who had been given a better job. Who had had a letter from home. Which officer was in a bad mood, and why. Which merchant in the settlement outside the walls was charging too much for his sausages. More news probably passed through the bath-house in a week than through the headquarters in a month.

There was no soap in the way we would think of it. Instead, a bather rubbed olive oil into his skin, sat and sweated in the hot room until the dirt loosened, and then scraped the oil and sweat and dirt away with a curved metal tool called a strigil. Then he went back to the warm room, and perhaps, if he was hardy, a quick plunge in the cold water to close the pores and wake himself up.

Now, the surprising part. Somebody had to build all of this, and somebody had to keep the furnace fed, and the soldiers did much of it themselves.

Among the Vindolanda tablets there is a short report, a list really, written by a clerk on a day in late April around the year one hundred. It records what the men in the workshops were doing that day. There were three hundred and forty-three of them. The list then breaks them down. A dozen were working as shoemakers. Some were at the kilns. Some were working with lead, or with clay, or plastering, or with the wagons, or at the hospital. And among them, in the clerk's tidy hand, is the line "builders to the bath-house, eighteen."

That is all. Just a working note, written by a man counting heads. But it tells us that, a generation before our night, eighteen soldiers on this same frontier spent an April day building the warm rooms they would later sit in. They were soldiers, but most of their days were the days of builders and craftsmen.

And there is one more surprise, to do with what they burned. Firewood was precious on these bare hills. The Wall country did not have endless forest. But it did have something else lying in the ground. In several places along this frontier, the black stone we call coal comes right up to the surface, in seams you can dig with a spade. Archaeologists have found coal at a number of the forts along the Wall. So some of the warmth tonight, in the bath furnace and perhaps in the brazier too, may be coming from coal, dug from the hillsides more than a thousand years before the great coal mines of Northumberland.

Now imagine that you are walking down the slope with Crescens and Vindex, the wind pushing at your back, the grass crunching with frost under your boots. Ahead of you, below the fort walls, there is a low stone building with a thin column of smoke rising from one end. As you come closer, you can smell it, a rich, dry, smoky smell, and you can hear the furnace before you see it, a low roar like a distant wind in a chimney.

You duck inside the changing room. It is dim, lit by a small window high up in the wall, and it smells of damp stone and oil and other men. You pull off your cloak and your tunic and everything else and leave them in a niche in the wall, and for a moment you are colder than you have been all day, standing barefoot on stone.

Then you step through the next doorway.

The warm room feels like a hand laid gently on your skin. The floor is warm under your feet. You breathe out slowly. And then you go on, through one more door, into the hot room, and the heat closes around you completely. The air is so thick you can almost lean on it. Your skin prickles, then flushes, then begins to sweat. The walls are warm when you touch them. Somewhere beneath your feet, the furnace roars on, and a little shiver of heat comes up through the floor with it.

You sit on a stone bench beside the others. Nobody says very much. Somebody passes a little flask of oil. You rub it into your arms and your legs, and you sit, and you sweat, and for the first time since the summer, you are entirely, completely warm. All the way through. Even your fingers. Even your toes.

Some evenings, perhaps, you would come out of a long, hot shower in a warm bathroom, wrap yourself in a towel, and step straight into a heated bedroom. Here, the men must walk back up the hill through the frost, with wet hair under their hoods, and keep that warmth inside them for as long as they can.

Historians have argued a little about how often ordinary soldiers really used these baths. Some think the bath was close to a daily habit, organized in turns, century by century, so that each group of men had its hours. Others suggest that with a winter fuel supply this hard to gather, the furnace may not always have been lit, and that a full hot bath was sometimes more of a regular treat than an everyday routine. Both views agree on one thing. When the bath was hot, the men went.

In the hot room, Crescens is telling a story. He tells it to Vindex, because Vindex is new and has not heard it before, and because the older men have heard it many times and would rather not hear it again.

The story is about a centurion, many years ago, who was so proud of his fine new boots that he wore them into the hot room to show them off, and the floor was so hot that the nails in the soles grew warm, and he had to dance out of the room in front of everybody, hopping from foot to foot. Crescens tells it with his hands and his whole body. Vindex laughs so hard that he has to wipe his eyes.

Senecio, sitting a little apart, says nothing, but he is smiling. He has seen too many burned feet in his time to find the story entirely funny, and he has also told it himself, more than once.

Then Crescens leans close to Vindex and says, more quietly, that it is not really a story about a centurion at all. It is a story about a shoemaker who made the centurion's boots and hammered in far too many nails, because the centurion had been rude to him. The shoemaker, he says, was his own teacher. Vindex looks at him, uncertain whether this second story is true. Crescens only shrugs and scrapes the oil from his arm with the curved strigil, very slowly and carefully, as if it were the most serious work in the world.

Agilis is lying on the warm bench at the other end of the room with his eyes closed. He has bathed in this way, in this kind of room, for twenty-four years, in forts up and down the frontier. He knows it will not be much longer now. He tries, sometimes, to count how many baths that must be, and he always falls asleep before he finishes.

Ammius stays only a short while. He is the decanus, and he does not like to leave the room empty of its leader for too long. He scrapes himself clean, takes one plunge in the cold water, which makes the others wince just to watch, and goes back up the hill in the last of the light, his hair still wet beneath his hood.

Vindex, meanwhile, has gone back to the warm room, and is sitting with his back against the heated wall, letting the heat soak slowly into him. He has never quite got used to this. As a boy, in the little settlement outside the fort at Vindolanda, he would sometimes stand near the stoke-hole of the bath-house there, just to feel the heat on his face, until the stoker shooed him away. Now he is a soldier, and the warm rooms are his by right. He sits very still, as if afraid that someone will notice and send him out.

His mother used to say that the soldiers were strange people, who took off all their clothes in the middle of winter to go and sit in a hot stone box. His father used to answer that she had never been warm until she married into the army. Vindex smiles at the memory, there in the steam.

Senecio comes and sits beside him. As the medical orderly, Senecio has a professional interest in the baths. He tells Vindex, in his quiet way, to drink some water before he goes back out, because a man who sweats this much in the hot room and then walks straight into the frost can easily come over faint. Then he looks at the boy's hands, which are cracked and red across the knuckles from the cold, and tells him to rub a little of the oil into them, and to keep doing it every night. Vindex does as he is told. The cracks sting, and then they stop stinging.

The men come out of the bath-house just as the sun touches the hills in the southwest. At this time of year, the sun sets here at around ten minutes before four in the afternoon, and the day has been only a little over seven hours long. The whole western sky is a long, thin band of orange and pink beneath the gray. The Wall on the ridge above them is black against it, every stone of its top edge sharp and clear.

They walk back up the slope quickly, steam rising faintly from their shoulders into the cold air, their hair stiffening in the wind. Vindex looks back once, at the smoke rising from the bath-house furnace into the last colored light, and thinks that it is one of the most beautiful things he has ever seen. He does not say so. He is eighteen, and the others would laugh.

Up in the barrack room, Vatto, who stayed behind, has kept the brazier going, and the room is warm and smells of charcoal and onions when they duck in through the door.

## Part Three — Around Half Past Four in the Afternoon: Dusk and the First Watch

The orange band in the west has faded to a smudge of dull red, and the sky above it is turning the deep, cold blue that comes just before full dark. Somewhere near the headquarters building, a trumpet sounds, a long, low note that rolls out over the roofs and away across the moor.

Vindex hears it and stands up from the warm bench by the brazier with a sigh. Tonight he has the first watch on the tower beside the north gate. He pulls on his cloak, takes up his spear and his shield, and goes out into the dusk.

Here is a small question for the coming of the dark. How does a soldier on this frontier tell the time on a winter night, with no clock on the wall and no watch on his wrist?

The Romans did not divide the day the way we do. They split the daylight into twelve hours, from sunrise to sunset, and the night into twelve more, from sunset to sunrise. That works neatly enough near the equator. But here, this far north, in the middle of December, the daylight lasts only a little over seven of our hours. So each Roman daytime hour, on a day like today, was only about thirty-seven of our minutes long. And each hour of the night stretched out to well over an hour and a quarter.

A Roman hour, in other words, was not a fixed length of time. It was a share of the light, or a share of the dark. In midwinter, the day hours shrank and the night hours swelled, like the tides.

For the army, the night was divided more simply still, into four watches. Each watch was a quarter of the darkness, and at this time of year, on this ridge, a single watch could last over four of our hours. The changes were signaled by trumpet calls, so that men in every corner of the fort could hear them. A headquarters building might also keep a water clock, a vessel that let water drip out at a steady rate, to help the officers judge when the next call should come.

So Vindex will stand on the tower until the trumpet calls the end of the first watch, which tonight will be some time after eight o'clock by our reckoning. Then another man will come up the steps to take his place, and Vindex will come home to the brazier.

Before he can climb the tower, though, he needs the watchword.

Every evening, a word or a phrase was chosen and written on a small tablet, which was passed down from the officers to each unit in turn, and then each soldier on guard had to know it. Anyone approaching the gate in the dark would be challenged and asked for it. The custom was very old. The Greek historian Polybius described it in the Roman army three centuries earlier. A man who did not know the word would not be let in, whatever he said about himself, and a sentry who let him in anyway would be in very deep trouble indeed.

From the top of the tower, if there were still enough light, you would see how the whole frontier fits together. In front of the Wall, to the north, there was usually a wide, deep ditch, though here on the crags the cliffs do that work instead. Behind the Wall, to the south, runs another great earthwork, a broad flat-bottomed ditch with banks of earth on either side, which we now call the Vallum. It marked off a military zone, a strip of land that belonged to the army. Civilians could cross it only at certain places, under the eyes of soldiers.

So a traveler coming down from the north would meet the Wall, then a gate in a milecastle, then the strip of military ground, and then the Vallum, and only then the open country of the province. It was less like a single wall and more like a long, careful corridor, with doors at either end, and soldiers standing at every door.

Along the stretches between the forts, small groups of men lived in the milecastles and the turrets, sometimes for days at a time. A turret was a little stone tower, perhaps twenty feet square, with a room on the ground floor where the men could keep a fire, and a ladder or stair to the top. A milecastle was larger, a walled yard with a gate through the Wall at one end and a small barrack inside for a handful of soldiers. On a night like tonight, the men in those lonely towers were the most exposed of all. They were also, in a way, the most independent, far from the eyes of their officers, with nothing to watch but the dark.

Now, the surprising detail of this part, and it is one of the most famous little scraps of writing ever found on this frontier.

Among the Vindolanda tablets, from around the years ninety-seven to one hundred and five, there is a short note about the local people. It is written in a plain, practical way, as if by an officer making a report, and much of it is damaged. But what survives says, more or less, that there are very many cavalry, that the cavalry do not use swords, and then, in a phrase that has become famous, that the "wretched little Britons" do not mount up to throw their javelins.

In the Latin, the word is Brittunculi. It is a diminutive, a word made smaller, and it is not kind. It is something like calling a grown man a little fellow, with a curl of the lip. Scholars had never met the word in any Latin text before this little tablet was read. And here it is, scrawled on a sliver of wood by someone on this frontier, the closest thing we have to hearing a Roman soldier talk about the people who lived around him, when he thought nobody important would ever read it.

That one word is a reminder that the men in the forts did not always think well of the people beyond them. And yet the frontier was never only Romans on one side and Britons on the other. People traded across it. People married across it. Young men from the region joined the army. Vindex, climbing the tower steps in the dusk, is himself half British.

Historians disagree about what that note was really describing. Some read it as a report on an enemy, an officer explaining to his successor how the local warriors fought. Others think it may describe British recruits being trained at the fort, men who had not yet learned to fight the Roman way on horseback, which would make the sneer more like that of a sergeant grumbling about new soldiers. The words do not settle the question either way.

Now imagine that you are standing with Vindex on top of the tower beside the north gate. Up here, there is nothing between you and the wind. It comes over the edge of the crags and hits you full in the face, and you turn your head and pull your hood forward, and still it finds its way in.

You look out to the north. The light is almost gone. The land below the ridge has become a dark, uneven sea of shadow, and the frozen lakes are dull patches of pewter in it. Far away, there is a single pinprick of orange light, a fire burning in some farmstead on the moor. You watch it for a long time. It does not move.

Your hands are cold on the shaft of your spear. Your feet, inside your boots, are going numb. You stamp them on the stone floor of the tower, one and then the other, softly, to keep the blood moving. Your breath comes out in a pale cloud and is snatched away by the wind before you can see it.

Below you, if you turn and look back into the fort, you can see the dim shapes of the barrack roofs, and here and there a faint red glow leaking from a chink in a shutter or a gap under a door. One of those glows, you know, is your own room, where the brazier is burning and the others are taking off their wet cloaks and the smell of supper is beginning.

You turn back to the north and the dark, and you wait.

You wait, and the dark settles. One by one, the stars come out, first a few bright ones low in the south, then more and more, until the sky above the moor is crowded with them. They are far brighter and closer than any stars you have ever seen from a town. There is no other light anywhere to dim them. High in the southeastern sky, the moon is already up, about two-thirds full, pale and silver, and it lays a faint shine across the frozen lakes below the cliffs.

You can tell the hour, if you know how, by watching certain stars climb the sky. The older soldiers know. They have stood on towers like this one for years, and they can look up at the sky and tell you, near enough, how much of the watch is left. Vindex does not know yet. He looks up anyway.


Think of how simply we tell the time. A glance at a phone, a clock on the oven, a small glowing number in the corner of a screen. Vindex has the trumpet, the turning of the stars, and the ache in his feet.

Down on the walkway below the tower, a figure comes along the inside of the Wall, cloak flapping, and stops at the foot of the steps. It is Ammius. Vindex stiffens, and calls out the challenge, as he was taught. Ammius gives the watchword. Then he climbs up and stands beside the boy for a moment, looking north.

He does not need to check on Vindex. The boy knows what he is doing. But Ammius remembers his own first winter on this frontier, nineteen years ago, and how long the first watch felt, and how small and alone he felt standing up here in the wind with the whole black country of the north in front of him. So he climbs up and stands there for a while and says nothing, and that is enough.

After a time, Vindex says, quietly, that his mother told him once that the people out there tell stories about the Wall. That they say it was built by giants. Ammius thinks about this. Then he says that in a way they are right. It was built by legionaries, and anyone who has met a legionary knows that they think they are giants.

Vindex laughs, and the wind takes the sound away.

Ammius asks him whether he has heard the word that some of the older soldiers use for the people out there. The boy nods. He has heard it. He has heard it used about himself, too, once or twice, by men who thought it was a joke. Ammius is quiet for a moment. Then he tells Vindex that his own father was called worse things, by Romans, in his time, and that his father's people were called barbarians by the army that they later served for three generations. Words like that, he says, are said by people who have not yet been cold enough, or long enough, beside the men they are talking about.

Vindex thinks about this for a long time after Ammius has gone. He thinks about his father, who was a Tungrian and a soldier, and his mother, who was a Briton and a soldier's wife, in all but law. He thinks that if he is anything, he is both. Up here, on the tower, between the dark country to the north and the lit fort to the south, it seems a very natural thing to be.
Then he claps the boy on the shoulder, tells him to keep his feet moving, and goes back down the steps.

Alone again, Vindex thinks about his father. His father served at Vindolanda, in the years when the old wooden fort stood there, long before this Wall was begun. He used to tell stories of those days. Of the commander's house, where the prefect's wife had held parties. Of the endless lists and reports the clerks wrote on their thin wooden tablets, and how they would burn piles of them on bonfires when the unit moved on, and how the rain would put the fires out before they were finished. Vindex used to think these were the dullest stories in the world. Tonight, on the tower, they do not seem so dull. They seem like the only stories that belong to him.

His father died three winters ago, of a fever, in the little house outside the fort. His mother still lives there, with his two younger sisters. They are only a couple of miles away. On a clear night, from a tower on this ridge, he thinks he could almost see the smoke from their roof, if he knew exactly where to look.

Vindex turns back to the north. The farmstead fire is still there, far out on the moor. He wonders who is sitting beside it. A family, perhaps, like his mother's family. A grandmother with her hands held out to the flames. Children being told to be quiet. He watches it, and keeps his feet moving, and waits for the trumpet.

Down in the lane, Ammius pauses at the door of the barrack room, sees the red glow of the brazier through the gap at its foot, and goes in to the warmth.

## Part Four — Around Five o'Clock in the Evening: Wet Wool and Dry Socks

Full dark has come down over the ridge now, and the barrack room has become the whole world. The door is shut against the wind, and the shutters are closed, and the only light comes from the brazier and from one small clay lamp on a shelf, its little flame bowing every time somebody moves.

The men who went to the baths are back, and their cloaks are wet with the mist that has begun to drift over the crags. The front room, where they keep their shields and spears and kit, smells of damp wool and leather and the oil they rub into their armor to keep the rust away.

Here is a small question for this part of the evening. Why does every man in this room hang his wet cloak high up on a peg near the roof, instead of right beside the warm brazier?

The answer is that wool forgives a great deal, but it does not forgive a fire. Hold a wet woolen cloak too close to hot coals and it will scorch, and shrink, and stiffen, and smell for weeks. Hang it high in the warm air near the ceiling, where the heat gathers, and it will dry slowly through the night, soft and whole. Most soldiers learned this, usually by ruining a cloak in their first winter.

And that cloak was perhaps the most important thing a soldier owned.

Let us look, then, at what a soldier on this frontier actually wore. On top, there was the cloak. The common military cloak was a heavy rectangle of thick wool, wrapped around the shoulders and pinned with a brooch at the right shoulder, so that the sword arm stayed free. It could be wrapped tight against the wind, or thrown back in the work of the day, and at night, in a pinch, it became a blanket. On this frontier, a hooded cape was also common, a garment the Britons themselves were famous for. Much later, in the year three hundred and one, when the emperor Diocletian issued a great list of maximum prices across the empire, the British hooded cloak was there in the list, by name, among the woolen goods of the provinces.

Under the cloak came the tunic. This was a long woolen shirt, reaching to about the knees, with short sleeves or sometimes longer ones, and it was belted at the waist. A soldier might wear two tunics, one over the other, in the depths of winter. Around his neck he might wrap a scarf, to stop his armor rubbing his skin raw and to keep the wind off his throat.

On his legs, here in the north, he very likely wore trousers. To an Italian of an older generation, trousers had been something only barbarians wore, the badge of the Gauls and Germans. But the army was practical. On the great carved column that the emperor Trajan put up in Rome in the year one hundred and thirteen, many of the auxiliary soldiers are shown wearing close-fitting breeches that reach to just below the knee. In a place like this, nobody was going to argue with a pair of warm trousers.

And on his feet, he wore heavy leather boots, or by this time more often a sturdy closed shoe, its sole studded with iron nails for grip, so that a whole century marching on a stone road would ring like a smithy.

Over the tunic, on duty, went the armor, and armor in winter is a cold friend. A shirt of iron mail weighs something like twenty pounds or more, and on a frosty night it draws the heat out of a man's body like a stone draws heat out of a hand. So under the mail, soldiers wore a padded garment of linen or wool, partly to soften the blows and partly, in a place like this, to keep the cold iron off their skin. Out on the tower tonight, Vindex is wearing every layer he owns beneath his mail, and he is still cold.

His hands are the hardest part to keep warm. The Romans did have something like gloves, though they were not common. Around the year one hundred and four, the writer Pliny the Younger described the habits of his famous uncle, the naturalist Pliny the Elder, and he mentioned that in winter the secretary who sat beside the old man, taking down his notes, wore coverings on his hands, so that not even the cold weather could steal any time from his studies. Most soldiers made do with long sleeves pulled down over their knuckles, a fold of the cloak, and the warmth of their own breath.

But that is only what you could see. What about underneath?

So here, at last, is the answer to our first question. What did a Roman soldier wear under his tunic in a British winter? He wore underpants. And he wore socks. Sometimes, if he was lucky, they were sent to him by someone who cared about him.

We know this because of one small, battered letter found at Vindolanda, written around the years ninety-seven to one hundred and five, a generation before the Wall was begun. Much of it is lost. But in the part that survives, the writer tells the man he is writing to that he has sent him pairs of socks from someone named Sattua, two pairs of sandals, and two pairs of underpants. Then, at the end, he sends greetings to several friends by name and to all of the man's messmates, the men he shared his room with.

That is the whole of it, more or less. Socks, sandals, underpants, and love to the lads. It is one of the most human things ever found on this frontier.

The Latin words tell us a little more. The underpants were subligaria, a kind of loincloth or short drawers, tied or wrapped around the hips, worn by many men, and by some women too, across the Roman world. The socks were udones. They were not knitted. Knitting, as we know it, would not reach Europe for something like a thousand years. Roman socks were cut and sewn from woolen cloth, or made of felt, and they could be quite thick, almost like a soft inner boot.

And yes, the Romans wore socks with their sandals. A small woolen sock sized for a child has come out of the ground at Vindolanda too. In this climate, nobody minded how it looked.

Think of the warm layers you might pull on for a winter walk today, the thin thermal shirt, the fleece, the fabric that wicks sweat away from the skin. Every one of those layers, for these men, was wool or linen, cut and sewn by hand, and every one had to last.

Historians do not agree about who Sattua was. For a long time it was read as the name of a place, as if the socks came from some town or district with that name. The editors of the tablets now think it is more likely a person's name, perhaps a maker or a supplier of socks, because a similar name has been found stamped on a piece of leather from the site. So Sattua may have been a sock-maker, a small trader in warm things, whose name has outlasted almost everyone else's from that frontier. Neither reading can yet be proved.

Now imagine that you are standing in the back room, just in from the cold, and you are changing for the evening. You unpin your cloak, and it is heavy and wet, and you reach up and hang it on the peg near the roof beam, where the others are already hanging, steaming gently in the warm air. You unlace your boots. Your fingers are slow and clumsy, and the leather laces are stiff with cold.

You pull off the boots, and then the socks beneath them, which are damp and cold and clinging to your feet. Your feet are pale and wrinkled and numb. You sit down on the edge of the low bed and rub them, hard, with both hands, and slowly, painfully, the feeling begins to come back, first as a prickling, then as a deep ache, then as warmth.

From the bag at the foot of your bed, you take out a dry pair of socks. They are thick, soft wool, a little rough, sewn with neat, careful seams by someone who knew what they were doing. You pull them on, one and then the other. And the feeling of dry, thick wool around cold feet is one of the plainest, deepest comforts there is. You sit there for a moment, simply enjoying it.

You shift on the bed, and turn your feet toward the brazier, not too close. The coals glow and pulse. A little warmth begins to soak into the wool, and into you.

Crescens, the shoemaker, is sitting cross-legged on the floor near the lamp with a boot in his lap. It is Agilis's boot, and one of the iron nails has worked its way loose from the sole. Crescens has a small iron awl and a handful of new nails in a cloth, and he is working the old nail out and fitting a new one, tapping it home with a little hammer, very softly, so as not to annoy everybody.

He works in the fort workshop all day, making and mending shoes for the whole unit. A soldier's feet are his life, Crescens likes to say, and a soldier's shoes are his feet. He has heard that in the old days, there were workshops on this frontier with many shoemakers working side by side, and he believes it. There are never enough shoes.

Agilis is sitting on his bed in his socks, watching Crescens work on his boot. He has had that boot for three years, and it has been mended so many times that very little of the original leather is left. He says this is like the army itself. Every man in the cohort has been replaced, he says, and every piece of every fort has been rebuilt, and yet it is still the same old cohort, marching on the same old nails.

Ammius is sorting through the bag of clothing he keeps under his bed, holding things up to the lamp to look for holes. He finds one, in the heel of a sock, and passes the sock to Senecio, who is better with a needle than anyone else in the room. Senecio threads a bone needle with woolen yarn and begins to darn it, his head bent over the work.

Vatto is lifting the lid on a pot set on an iron stand above the brazier, letting out a cloud of steam that smells of onions and barley and salt pork. He stirs it, tastes it, adds a pinch of something from a small pot, and puts the lid back on. Supper, he announces, will be ready when it is ready, and not before.

Felicio has come in from the headquarters, where he spends his days in the clerks' office copying lists and reports, and he is stamping his feet and rubbing his hands, and he has brought news. A courier has come in from the south, from the big depot at Corbridge, and there is a sack of letters for the cohort. Felicio has seen the sack. He does not know yet who the letters are for. They will be handed out tomorrow, by the centurions, in the morning.

Everyone in the room goes very slightly quiet at this. Each of them is wondering, without saying so, whether there might be something in that sack with his name on it. A letter. A parcel. A pair of socks.

Butto, who has come in behind Felicio, says loudly that he hopes there is a parcel for him, because his underpants are a disgrace. Everyone laughs. Agilis says that the whole fort already knows this. Butto, without the slightest embarrassment, agrees.

Senecio finishes the darn, bites off the thread, and holds the sock up to the lamp to inspect it. The heel is whole again, the new wool a slightly different color from the old. He tosses it back to Ammius, who catches it without looking up. Then Senecio takes out his own small bundle of things, a little roll of linen bandages, a pot of salve, a few dried leaves wrapped in cloth, and lays them out on his blanket to check them, as he does every evening. It is a habit. Nobody in this room is hurt tonight. But in a cohort of several hundred men, somebody, somewhere, always has a blister, or a cough, or a cut hand from the workshops, and sooner or later they come to Senecio.

He looks over at Butto, who is sitting with his big feet stretched out toward the brazier, and tells him that if he keeps them that close to the coals, his new socks will be scorched before he ever gets a parcel to replace them. Butto pulls his feet back, just a little.

Vindex is still out on the tower, in the wind. Ammius glances at the boy's empty bed, and at the dry pair of socks folded at its foot, where Vindex left them that afternoon, ready for when he comes in. Then Ammius takes those socks and lays them on the floor near the brazier, at a careful distance, so that they will be warm when the boy gets back.

## Part Five — Around Six o'Clock in the Evening: Barley, Bacon and the Question of Beer

The mist has thickened outside, and now and then a gust of wind throws a fine wet spray against the shutters, like a handful of sand. Inside, the pot on the brazier has begun to bubble in earnest, and Vatto says the words that every man in the room has been waiting for. It is ready.

Here is a small question for supper. Where did a soldier on this frontier get his bread?

The answer, for most of the year, is that he made it himself. Or at least, he and his messmates did.

The army issued each man a ration of grain, mostly wheat, measured out from the great granaries of the fort. Those granaries are some of the most carefully built buildings on the whole Wall. Their floors were raised on low stone pillars, so that air could flow beneath them, keeping the grain cool and dry and safe from damp and rats. Thick buttresses held up the walls, because grain, piled high, presses outward with enormous force.

But the army did not hand out flour. It handed out grain. So in each barrack room, there was a hand mill, a rotary quern. It was made of two round, flat stones, one on top of the other. The lower stone stayed still. The upper stone had a hole in the middle for the grain to be poured in, and a wooden handle at the edge, and a man turned it round and round, and round again, and the grain was crushed between the stones and came out at the edge as coarse flour.

It was slow work, and hard on the arms and shoulders. A contubernium would take it in turns, a man grinding for an hour while the others did other things. Then the dough was mixed, and taken to an oven to be baked. At many forts, the ovens were built into the back of the rampart, the earth bank that ran around the inside of the fort walls, where their heat and their sparks were kept well away from the wooden roofs of the barracks.

None of this food was free. A soldier's pay was docked for it. Pay records written on papyrus, which survive from the dry sands of Egypt, show soldiers having money taken out of their wages for their food, for hay, for their boots and straps, for their clothing, and even for the midwinter festival of the camp, the Saturnalia. What a soldier had left over went into savings, kept for him at the headquarters, in a strongroom under the floor, and a careful man could leave the army with a useful sum.

So when Vatto stirs the pot tonight, he is cooking with food the men have, in a sense, already paid for. Nobody in this room wastes a crumb.

The bread itself was dense and dark and chewy, and a little gritty from the stones. A man who had eaten army bread for twenty years would often have teeth worn flat by it.

Alongside the bread came everything else. Barley, which could be cooked into a thick porridge or used for brewing. Beans and lentils. Cheese. Salt, which mattered enormously. Bacon and pork fat, which mattered even more in winter. And meat. Old ideas about Roman soldiers living mostly on grain have been overturned by the bones dug up from the rubbish pits of forts like this one. Cattle, sheep, pigs, and deer and hare from the hunt, all turn up in quantity.

To drink, there was sour wine, a thin, sharp drink that the Romans valued for being cheap and thirst-quenching, often mixed with water. And there was beer.

Which brings us to one of the most famous letters on the whole frontier.

Around the years ninety-seven to one hundred and five, a cavalry officer named Masclus wrote a short letter to the commander at Vindolanda, a prefect named Flavius Cerialis. Much of it is worn away. It seems to have asked about the orders for the next day. Masclus addressed his commander, rather grandly, as his king. But it is the end of the letter that has made him famous. He wrote, as the editors translate it, "My fellow-soldiers have no beer. Please order some to be sent."

That is all. A plain request from a junior officer, sent up the line on a sliver of wood. Somewhere on this frontier, a generation before the Wall, a group of cavalrymen had run out of beer, and somebody had to do something about it.

And somebody did make it. In another account from Vindolanda, from a few years later, there is a man listed as Atrectus the brewer. His title is written in the Latin of the army, as cervesarius, from cervesa, the Celtic word for beer that the Romans borrowed. Other accounts from the fort record supplies of Celtic beer being issued, measured by the modius, a dry measure of about two gallons. The men on this frontier, many of them from the beer-drinking lands of the Rhine and the Meuse, plainly had no intention of giving it up.

Historians have argued for a long time about how much meat a Roman soldier really ate. An older view, drawn partly from writers like Julius Caesar, who describes his troops enduring a siege on meat alone as if it were a real hardship, held that soldiers ate mainly bread and porridge and saw meat as a poor substitute. More recent work on the animal bones from forts in Britain and along the Rhine suggests that meat, especially beef, was a regular part of the diet. Perhaps the answer is that it depended on where you were, and when, and how far the nearest cattle market was.

Now imagine that you are sitting on the edge of a low bed in the back room, a wooden bowl in your hands. Vatto has ladled it full from the pot, and it is so hot that you can feel it through the wood. You hold it close to your face, and the steam rises up into your nose, thick with the smell of barley and onions and salt pork, and something green that might once have been a cabbage.

You take a spoonful. It burns your tongue a little, and you do not care. It is thick and salty and filling, and the pork fat coats your mouth, and the warmth of it goes all the way down. You can feel it arriving in your stomach and spreading outward from there, into your chest and your arms.

Someone passes you a heel of bread, torn from a round loaf. It is dark and dense and has a faint taste of smoke from the oven. You dip it in the bowl, and it soaks up the broth, and you eat it slowly. You can feel, now and then, a tiny gritty crunch from the millstones between your teeth.

Earlier this afternoon, it was your turn at the quern. You knelt on the floor of the front room with the heavy stones before you, and poured a handful of grain into the hole in the top, and took the wooden handle, and turned. Round and round. The stone grinding against stone made a low, steady rumble, like a cart far off on a road. Your shoulders ached after the first quarter of an hour, and your hands were white with dust. The flour gathered in a soft pale ring around the base of the stones, and you swept it into a bowl with the side of your hand. It did not seem like very much, for so much turning.

And now here it is, in your hand, as bread. That is the thing about a night like this. Almost everything you touch, you can trace back to someone's tired arms.

Around you, the room is quiet except for the sound of spoons on wooden bowls and the soft pop and hiss of the brazier. Nobody talks much during the first bowl. The talk comes with the second.

Think of how an evening meal usually begins for us. A refrigerator, a shop down the road, flour already ground in a paper bag. Every mouthful in this room tonight was ground by hand, carried up a hill, and counted out by a clerk.

When the first bowls are empty, Vatto fills them again, and Ammius reaches under his bed and brings out a small clay jug, and there is a murmur of approval. It is beer. Not very much of it. Ammius has been saving it. He pours a little into each man's cup, and then pauses, and pours a little into one more cup, which he sets aside on the shelf beside the lamp.

That one is for Vindex, for when he comes in from the tower.

Butto lifts his cup and says that he would like to propose a toast to Atrectus, whoever he was, and to every brewer who has ever lived. Everyone drinks to that.

Then the talk begins, as it does every night, and tonight it is about food. Agilis, who has been in the army longest, says that in his first fort, on the Rhine, there was a cook who could make the salt pork taste like roast boar. Crescens says that is impossible. Agilis says that Crescens has never eaten roast boar in his life and would not know. Crescens admits this.

Felicio, who sees the prefect's household accounts pass through the clerks' office, says that if he were the commander, he would send out for a hundred chickens and a basket of eggs and all the apples in Britain. Everyone in the room agrees that this is what they would do too.

Officers' households really did shop on that scale. Among the Vindolanda tablets, about a generation before the Wall, there is a list written for an officer's household, asking for twenty chickens, "a hundred apples, if you can find nice ones," and a hundred or two hundred eggs, "if they are for sale there at a fair price." Nobody in a barrack room like this one would ever see a hundred apples at once. But they could dream, and Felicio, without knowing it, has dreamed exactly the same dream as a servant on this frontier thirty years before him.

Senecio says, quietly, that he would settle for one nice apple. Vatto says that there will be no apples until the summer, and that if Senecio wants something sweet, there is a little honey in the jar on the shelf, and he may have a spoonful, but only one. Senecio takes his spoonful, slowly, as if it were a great feast.

Butto finishes his second bowl and looks hopefully at the pot. Vatto puts the lid on it firmly. That is for Vindex too.

Agilis has gone quiet, looking into his bowl. When Crescens asks him what he is thinking about, he says that he is thinking about his mother's cooking, in the house where he grew up, on the flat wet land near the mouth of the Rhine. She used to cook fish, he says, caught in the river channels that morning, with a sauce of something sharp and green. He has not eaten fish cooked like that in twenty-four years. He is not even sure he could find the house again, or whether anyone he knew is still living in it.

Then he looks up and says that when he is discharged, he might go back and look. Or he might not. He might stay here, in the settlement outside the fort, where at least he knows the brewer. Everyone laughs at that, and Agilis laughs too, but the question stays with him, in a quiet corner of his mind, for the rest of the evening.

Vatto, who knows his men, ladles a last spoonful of pork from the bottom of the pot into Agilis's bowl, without a word, and Agilis eats it, without a word.

Felicio has his writing tablet out again on his knee, and he is making a list of his own, a short one, of things the room needs. Fuel for the brazier. Oil for the lamp. Salt. A new length of leather thong for Butto's boot, which Crescens says is beyond hope. Felicio writes neatly and quickly, with a stylus on the wax, and every few words he looks up and asks if anyone has anything else to add. Butto says apples. Felicio writes it down, and then rubs it out again.

Ammius sits back against the wall, his cup in his hands, and watches his men. They are fed. They are warm. They have a little beer. For a decanus on this frontier in winter, there are few better feelings than that.

Outside, far off, a dog barks in the settlement beyond the fort walls, and another answers it, and then it is quiet again. The mist presses against the shutters. The wind has dropped a little.

Vatto rakes the brazier gently with an iron rod, settling the coals, and adds a few more pieces of fuel, and a soft orange light spreads across the faces of the men.

## Part Six — Around Seven o'Clock in the Evening: Ink, Wax and the Clerk's Lamp

The bowls have been wiped clean with the last of the bread and stacked by the door, and the pot has been set aside on the floor, its lid on, keeping Vindex's supper warm. The men have spread out a little around the room, each to his own small business. This is the hour of mending, and polishing, and talk, and, for one man, of writing.

Felicio has moved the clay lamp from the shelf to the floor beside his bed, and he is sitting cross-legged in its small circle of light with a bundle of thin wooden leaves in his lap.

Here is a small question for this part of the evening. On the far northern edge of the empire, where papyrus from Egypt was rare and costly, what did a soldier write his letters on?

The answer is wood. Two kinds of wood, in fact, used in two quite different ways.

The first kind was the stylus tablet. It was a small rectangle of wood, a little like a slate, with a shallow recess hollowed out of one face and filled with dark wax. You wrote on it by scratching into the wax with a stylus, a pointed rod of iron or bone, and when you were done, you could smooth the wax flat with the blunt end and use it again. Two or three of these tablets could be tied together at the edge, like the pages of a tiny book, with the wax faces turned inward to protect the writing. They were used for notes, for drafts, for accounts, and for documents that had to last.

The second kind is the kind that has made Vindolanda famous. These were leaf tablets, thin slices of wood from local trees such as birch and alder, shaved down to about the thickness of a thin card, a couple of millimeters at most, and roughly the size of a postcard. You did not scratch these. You wrote on them in ink, with a pen.

The pen had a split metal nib, or sometimes a sharpened reed, and the ink was made of carbon, the fine black soot of a fire or a lamp, mixed with gum and water. Ink like that sits on the surface of the wood rather than sinking in, and it can last a very long time if it stays out of the light.

When a letter was finished, the leaf was scored down the middle and folded, with the writing on the inside. The address was written on the outside. Then it could be tied, sealed, and handed to whoever was going in the right direction, a courier, a soldier traveling on duty, a merchant with a cart.

Letters had their own good manners, and they were followed carefully, even on the edge of the world. A letter began with the name of the sender and the name of the person written to, and the word for greetings, often with "very many greetings" if the writer was being warm. Friends who were not related called each other brother, or sister, as a kind of affection. And a letter ended with a farewell, and very often with greetings sent to a whole list of other people, friends and messmates and families, so that one small leaf of wood might carry good wishes to half a dozen people at once.

Often, the closing lines are in a different hand from the rest. A clerk or a friend wrote the body of the letter, and then the sender took the pen and added the last line or two himself, a farewell in his own writing, as a kind of signature, so that the person at the other end would know it was really from him.

That is how the Vindolanda letters came to be. Thousands of thin leaves of wood, written, folded, sent, read, and thrown away.

Now the surprising detail, and it comes from one of the oldest of those tablets, written a little before the year one hundred, before even the famous letters of the prefect's household.

It is a strength report, an official return of the kind that clerks like Felicio wrote every day, recording the state of a unit. This one was for the First Cohort of Tungrians, a regiment raised from the Tungrian lands on the river Meuse, the very country Vatto comes from. On paper, the cohort numbered seven hundred and fifty-two men, with six centurions. But a large part of it was not at Vindolanda at all. A big detachment was away at the base at Corbridge. Dozens of men were away serving as guards to the governor of the province. Others were scattered in ones and twos on errands all over the north. Fewer than three hundred men, in the end, were actually at the fort that day.

And then the clerk recorded how many of those men were unfit for duty, and why. He divided them into three neat groups. The editors translate them as "sick, fifteen; wounded, six; suffering from inflammation of the eyes, ten."

Ten men, out of a few hundred, laid up with sore eyes. It sounds like a small thing. But in a world of smoky rooms and smoky fires and dust and grit and long winters indoors, it was not small at all. Eye trouble was one of the great nagging complaints of the Roman army, and here it is, written down in its own column, on a winter frontier, nearly two thousand years ago.

That little return also tells us something else, quietly. A Roman fort was rarely as full as its walls suggest. Men were constantly being sent somewhere else, on guard duty, on supply runs, on detachment to other forts. The barracks we walk through today were built for whole cohorts. On any given night, many of their rooms may have been half empty, and the men in them may have been from several different units.

And the army wrote down everything. Among the tablets are dozens of small daily reports, each one handed in by a junior officer at the end of his duty. They follow the same pattern again and again, almost word for word. The date. The unit. And then a short, comforting formula, which says, in effect, that all who ought to be at their posts are at their posts, and the baggage too. Day after day, a man on this frontier wrote down that everything was where it should be.

There is something oddly peaceful about those reports. They are the sound of a frontier on an ordinary day, when nothing at all has happened, and somebody has written it down to prove it.

We owe all of this to the mud. The tablets survived at Vindolanda because they were thrown into damp ground, some of them perhaps onto a bonfire that the rain put out, and then sealed over by layers of later building, packed down so tightly that the air could not get in. Without air, the tiny creatures that eat wood could not reach them. When they were first lifted out, they looked like nothing more than dark brown shavings of wood, and it took patient work under the camera to bring the ink back into view, line by faded line.

Historians have argued about who actually wrote all these tablets. One view is that most of them were written by a small number of trained scribes and clerks, men like Felicio, who wrote for everyone else, and that most ordinary soldiers could not read or write at all. Another view points to the large number of different handwritings found at Vindolanda, and to the many letters that seem to have been written by the sender himself, at least in their closing lines, and argues that a fair number of soldiers and officers could write, at least a little. The truth probably lies somewhere in between, and it probably varied a great deal from man to man.

Now imagine that you are sitting beside Felicio in the little circle of lamplight. You can feel the warmth of the brazier on one side of your face and the cold of the stone wall at your back. The lamp burns with a small, steady, yellow flame, and it smells faintly of hot oil. Its light is just enough to see by, if you lean close.

Felicio hands you a stylus tablet. It is lighter than you expected, smooth and cool in your hands, and the dark wax in its hollow shines faintly in the lamplight. You take the stylus and press its point into the wax, and it slides through the surface, leaving a pale, clean line behind it, with a tiny curl of wax at the end. It makes a soft, scratching whisper as it moves.

You try to write a letter, a single letter of the alphabet, and it comes out crooked. Felicio smiles, and takes the tablet back, and turns the stylus over, and with the flat end he smooths the wax back down, as if your letter had never been.

Then he takes up a pen and a thin leaf of wood. He dips the pen into a small pot of ink, and touches the nib to the rim to shake off the extra, and begins to write, in quick, small, flowing strokes, leaning close to the lamp. The ink goes down black and wet and glistening, and dries almost at once on the pale wood. You can smell it, faintly. A smell of soot and something sweet.

When you watch a man write by hand like this, by a single flame, every word seems to cost something. Each one is chosen. Nothing is wasted.

Felicio pauses, and blows very gently on the wet ink to help it dry, and the lamp flame bends in his breath. You can see the grain of the wood through the thin pale leaf, fine lines running the length of it, like the lines on the palm of a hand. When he holds it up to the lamp, the light glows faintly through it, honey-colored. It is a beautiful thing, in its plain way, this sliver of a northern tree turned into a page.

He sets it down beside him to dry, and takes up the next leaf, and dips his pen again.

Think of how easily our own words travel. We press a button, and a message crosses an ocean before we have set the phone down. Felicio's letter tonight, if all goes well, will take weeks to reach the person it is meant for, and it may never arrive at all.

Felicio is not writing for himself tonight. He is writing for Butto.

Felicio learned his own letters as a boy, in a town on the river Moselle, in a little school held in the corner of a market hall, where a bored schoolmaster made the boys copy lines of poetry onto wax tablets over and over until their hands ached. He hated it at the time. Now it is the reason he sits by a lamp each evening, instead of hauling stone in the quarries.

Butto cannot write. He can read a few words, his own name, the numbers on a ration tally, the name of his cohort painted on a shield. But he cannot make the letters himself. So, once in a while, he sits down beside Felicio, and talks, and Felicio writes.

Tonight Butto is writing to his brother, who is a soldier too, in another cohort, at another fort further west along the Wall. They have not seen each other for nearly two years. Butto sits with his big hands folded in his lap, frowning at the brazier, trying to think of what to say. Felicio waits, pen ready.

Butto starts by asking how his brother is. Then he stops. Then he says that he is well, and that the winter is cold, and that the food is all right, which Felicio writes down in a slightly better form than Butto said it. Then Butto stops again, for a long time.

The others in the room pretend not to listen. Crescens bends over a strap he is mending. Senecio is grinding something in a small mortar, a powder for one of his salves. Agilis lies on his back on his bed with his eyes closed. Ammius polishes the bronze fittings of his belt with a scrap of cloth, slowly, round and round.

Senecio looks up, then, and sees that Felicio is sitting directly in the drift of smoke from the brazier, which tonight is curling a little toward his corner of the room because of the draft from the door. He tells him, gently, to move the lamp and himself a little to the left, because a clerk with sore eyes is no use to anybody. Felicio does as he is told. He has heard Senecio say this before. He knows it is true.

Butto, at last, has thought of what he wants to say. He wants to ask his brother something. He leans in close to Felicio, and says it quietly, and Felicio nods, and writes it down.

We will come back to what he asked, a little later in the night.

Ammius finishes with his belt and holds it up to the light from the brazier. The bronze shines a soft, warm red in the glow of the coals, and he nods, satisfied, and lays it carefully at the foot of his bed.

## Part Seven — A Little After Eight o'Clock in the Evening: Dice, Candles and a Long Way from Home

Out across the fort, the trumpet sounds again, the long, low call that ends the first watch. Somewhere above the north gate, a new sentry is climbing the tower steps, and Vindex is coming down them. A few minutes later, the door of the front room opens, and a gust of cold air swirls in around him, and the lamp flame ducks and flickers and then stands up again.

The boy's face is white with cold, and his eyebrows are beaded with mist. He sets down his shield and spear with stiff hands and comes through into the back room, and the warmth hits him so hard that he simply stands there for a moment with his eyes closed.

Nobody says anything. Ammius points at the socks warming beside the brazier. Vatto lifts the lid off the pot. Senecio hands him the cup of beer from the shelf.

On the floor near the brazier, Crescens has laid out a small wooden board and a leather cup, and three small bone dice are rattling inside it.

Here is a small question for this part of the night. Why is it that tonight, in a Roman army barrack, eight men are allowed to gamble with dice in the open, when on most nights of the year they could be punished for it?

The answer is that it is the season of the Saturnalia.

The Saturnalia was the great midwinter festival of the Roman world, in honor of the old god Saturn. It began on the seventeenth of December, and by this time it had spread out to last several days, a whole week of feasting and freedom in the darkest part of the year. For those days, many of the usual rules were turned upside down. People wore bright, informal clothes instead of their formal dress. They greeted each other with a cheerful cry, Io Saturnalia. In many households, the masters served dinner to their slaves. And gambling with dice, which was disapproved of the rest of the year, was openly allowed.

People gave each other presents, too. Small, simple ones, as a rule. Wax candles were a favorite, a little gift of light in the darkest days of the year. So were tiny figurines of baked clay, and small jokes, and cheap little treasures. The poet Martial, writing in Rome in the eighties of the first century, filled a whole book with short verses meant to go with Saturnalia gifts, rich and poor, from writing tablets and toothpicks to warm cloaks and lamps.

On this frontier, too, people kept the festival. One of the letters found at Vindolanda, from around the years ninety-seven to one hundred and five, was written by a slave in the commander's household to a friend, asking him to see to some things for the Saturnalia, at a few small coins each, and radishes too, if the editors read the word rightly. Even here, at the end of the world, someone was doing the festival shopping.

In a great many households, someone was chosen by lot to be the king of the Saturnalia, a mock ruler for the festival, who could give silly orders that everyone had to obey. Sing a song. Carry the cook around the room. Call the master by a rude name. It was a time when the ordinary order of the world was set aside, just for a few days, and everyone was allowed to breathe.

Not everyone loved it. The philosopher Seneca, writing to a friend in Rome in the sixties of the first century, complained that it was December, and yet the whole city was in a sweat, given over to its feasting and its noise. He thought a wise man should keep a little apart from it all. Most people, it seems, did not take his advice.

The Romans played with two kinds of dice. There were cubes, much like ours, often of bone, with spots on each face. And there were knucklebones, the small ankle bones of sheep or goats, which have four flat sides that they can land on, each with its own value. A player threw four of them at once from a cup. If all four came up showing different sides, that was the best throw of all, and it was called the Venus, after the goddess of love and luck. The worst throw, four ones, was called the dog.

So tonight, in this room, the dice are out. And it is also, the men have agreed, a night for small gifts.

Now imagine that you are Vindex, just in from the tower. You sit down on the edge of your bed, and you pull off your cold, wet boots, and you take the warm socks that Ammius left by the brazier and pull them on. They are warm all the way through. You close your eyes for a moment, because it is almost too much to bear.

Someone puts a bowl in your hands. It is hot. It is the last of the pot, thick and salty, with the best of the pork that Vatto saved for you from the bottom. You eat it very fast, hunched over it, and then more slowly, and with every spoonful the cold of the tower goes out of you a little more.

You drink the beer. It is thin and a little sour and the best thing you have ever tasted.

Around you, the room is warm and close and full of the low sound of men talking. The brazier glows red. The lamp flickers. Somebody laughs. The dice rattle in the leather cup and spill out across the board, and Crescens groans, and Butto cheers, and Agilis says that the gods of the dice have always hated shoemakers. You look around at the faces in the firelight, and for the first time since you joined the army, you feel that you are home.

Then Ammius reaches into the bag under his bed and holds out something to you. It is a candle. A real wax candle, pale yellow, about as long as your hand, the kind they sell in the settlement outside the fort for the festival. It is not much. But it is a gift, and it is for you, and you hold it in both hands and do not know what to say.

Ammius only says, Io Saturnalia, and goes back to his belt.

Little by little, you feel your feet coming back to life inside the warm socks. It is not pleasant at first. It starts as a dull ache, deep in the bones, and then becomes a fierce, hot prickling, as if a thousand tiny needles were being pressed into your toes. You rub them through the wool. You stretch them toward the brazier. Senecio, watching you, tells you to keep them moving, and not to put them too close to the coals, because frozen feet do not feel a burn until it is too late. You do as he says. The prickling slowly fades into warmth.

Crescens holds out the leather cup of dice to you, and you shake your head, and then change your mind and take it. You shake it and throw. The four little knucklebones skitter across the board and come to rest, and all four show a different face. A Venus. On your very first throw of the night. The whole room groans and cheers at once, and Butto says that it is not fair, that the gods always love the young ones, and you cannot stop grinning.

The talk around the dice board, as the evening goes on, turns to home, as it often does. And that is a good moment for us to look at our second question.

Why were the soldiers on Hadrian's Wall, for the most part, not Italians at all?

To answer it, we need to know that there were two great kinds of soldier in the Roman army. The first were the legionaries. They were Roman citizens, heavy infantry, organized in great legions of about five thousand men each. It was the legions that built the Wall. But they did not live on it. Their bases were far to the south, at York, at Chester, and at Caerleon in Wales. By this time, even most legionaries were no longer Italians. They came from the citizen towns of the provinces, from Gaul and Spain and the Rhine.

The second kind were the auxiliaries. The word means helpers. They were soldiers recruited from the peoples of the provinces who were not Roman citizens. And it was mainly auxiliaries who manned the forts along the Wall and lived there, year after year, in rooms just like this one. They were organized in smaller regiments, cohorts of infantry and wings of cavalry, of about five hundred or a thousand men, and each regiment was named, at first, for the people it was raised from.

That is why the names on this frontier read like a map of the empire. Tungrians from the Meuse, in what is now Belgium. Batavians from the Rhine delta, in what is now the Netherlands. Gauls. Spaniards. Dalmatians from across the Adriatic. And in Hadrian's reign, only a few miles west of here, at the fort of Carvoran, a cohort of archers from Syria, from the far eastern edge of the Mediterranean, stood guard on the moors of northern Britain.

The Roman historian Tacitus tells us that these northern peoples were here from early on. In his life of his father-in-law, Agricola, the governor of Britain, written in the year ninety-eight, he describes a great battle somewhere in the north of Scotland. At the key moment, he says, Agricola sent forward his Batavian and Tungrian cohorts, because, he explained, they were long trained in fighting at close quarters. He clearly thought of them as some of the toughest soldiers in the army.

The Batavians, in particular, had a famous skill. They were renowned as swimmers, men who could cross a wide river in full kit, beside their horses, holding on to them in the current. Tacitus tells of auxiliaries who swam across the strait to the island of Anglesey and took its defenders entirely by surprise, because nobody there had imagined that soldiers could cross water like that without boats. Agilis, the Batavian in our room, can still swim like a seal, he says, though there is nowhere on this frozen ridge to prove it.

And then there is the reward for all those years of service.

An auxiliary soldier served for twenty-five years. When he was discharged, he was granted Roman citizenship, for himself, and for his children, and the right to a lawful marriage. Many veterans had a copy of that grant made in bronze, a pair of small, hinged metal plates, about the size of a postcard, engraved on both sides. Historians call them military diplomas.

One of them came out of the soil at Malpas, in Cheshire, a little over two hundred years ago. It was issued in the year one hundred and three, under the emperor Trajan. It names a man called Reburrus, son of Severus, from Spain, a junior officer in a cavalry regiment raised in Pannonia, on the Danube. It grants citizenship to those who had served twenty-five years or more, and to their children and descendants. Reburrus carried it with him, perhaps all the way to the end of his life, as proof of who he had become.

So here is the answer to our second question. The men on this Wall were not Italians because they were not legionaries. They were the empire's helpers, gathered in from its edges, from the Rhine and the Meuse and Spain and Syria, and set down here to guard yet another edge. And what they were promised, at the end of a quarter of a century, was not land in Italy or a return home. It was something less visible and, to many of them, more precious. It was the right to call themselves Roman.

Historians disagree about how long these regiments kept their original character. Some argue that units like the Batavians went on recruiting from their homelands for generations, keeping their languages and their customs alive on foreign frontiers. Others point out that once a unit had been in Britain for twenty or thirty years, most of its new recruits seem to have come from wherever it was stationed, so that a cohort of Tungrians might, before long, be full of young men born in Britain, like Vindex, with only the name to remind them where it began.

Agilis, who has been listening to all of this from his bed, sits up when the talk turns to discharge. In a little over a year, if the gods are kind, he will have his twenty-five years. He has already been to see Felicio about it, more than once, to make sure the dates in the records are right. He has worked out exactly how much he has saved at the headquarters strongroom, coin by coin. He knows exactly which bench he will sit on in the settlement tavern, on his first evening as a free citizen of Rome.

Crescens asks him what he will do with his bronze certificate when he gets it. Agilis says he will hang it on the wall of whatever house he lives in, and look at it every morning, and then go back to sleep. Everyone laughs.

But then he says, more seriously, that he will have it made in bronze for his son. Agilis has a son, in the settlement outside the walls, a boy of seven, by a British woman he has lived with for ten years, whom the army does not allow him to marry while he serves. When the grant comes through, she will be his lawful wife, and his son will be born a Roman citizen in the eyes of the law. That is the part that matters to him. Not for himself. For the boy.

The room is quiet for a moment.

Then Butto rattles the dice and throws, and wins, and shouts Io Saturnalia so loudly that the men in the next room bang on the wall, and the whole room dissolves into laughter.

Vindex sits by the brazier with his empty bowl in his lap and the candle in his hand, and he looks at it for a long while, turning it in the red glow of the coals.

## Part Eight — Around Nine o'Clock in the Evening: A Birthday Letter and the Lights of the Commander's House

The dice have been put away in their leather cup, and the board is leaning against the wall. The noise from the next room has faded. Across the fort, the sounds of the evening are thinning out, one by one, a door closing, a man calling goodnight, a cart wheel creaking somewhere near the granaries and then falling silent.

Felicio has finished Butto's letter, and he reads it back to him now, quietly, by the light of the lamp. Butto listens with his head on one side, nodding at each line, as if checking that the words have come out the way he meant them.

Here is a small question for this part of the night. Were there women and children living on this frontier, among all these soldiers?

The answer is yes, and more of them than people once imagined.

The commander of a fort like this one, the prefect, was usually a man of some standing, from a wealthy family in one of the provinces, and he did not come to the frontier alone. He brought his wife, his children, his slaves and servants, and his household. They lived in the commander's house, the largest and finest building in the fort, built around a courtyard, with its own kitchen, its own dining room, and, very often, its own heated rooms with a hypocaust beneath the floor.

The ordinary soldiers were not allowed to marry lawfully while they served. But that did not stop them having families. Outside the walls of almost every fort on the frontier grew a settlement, a village of traders and craftsmen, tavern keepers and blacksmiths, and also the women and children of the soldiers, living in small houses strung along the road. These families were real in every way except in law. Many of the men in this barrack room have someone, out there in the dark beyond the fort walls, whom they think of as home.

And we know there were children inside the forts, too, because of their shoes. Thousands of shoes have been found in the waterlogged ground at Vindolanda, preserved in the same airless mud that kept the tablets. Some are the heavy nailed boots of soldiers. But some are the delicate shoes of women, and some are small enough for children, even for toddlers taking their first steps. They tell us, in leather, that families walked these lanes.

The settlement beyond the walls was a busy little place in its own right. At Housesteads, archaeologists have uncovered the remains of such a settlement on the slope below the south gate, though most of what survives there was built a century or more after our night. Its buildings were long, narrow houses and shops set end-on to the road, so that each one could have a counter at the front, open to passers-by. In our year, the little town by the fort is smaller and plainer, but the same kind of life is already gathering around it. Here a soldier could buy a hot pie, a cup of something stronger than the army's beer, a new brooch for his cloak, a charm against bad luck. Here, too, lived the people who made his life bearable in ways the army never planned, the women who washed and mended, the cooks and the bakers, the children who ran errands for a coin.

On a festival night like this, the settlement is full of light and noise. Every house has its lamp burning. Somewhere, there is music. Somewhere, there is singing, and someone is almost certainly singing badly, and someone else is telling him to stop.

Now, the surprising detail of this part, and it is one of the most beloved objects ever found in Britain.

Around the years ninety-seven to one hundred and five, a generation before the Wall, the wife of the commander at Vindolanda was a woman named Sulpicia Lepidina. Her husband was the prefect Flavius Cerialis. And she had a friend, Claudia Severa, the wife of the commander of another fort not far away.

One day, Claudia sent Lepidina an invitation. It was written on a thin leaf of wood, like all the others, in a neat, practiced hand, almost certainly by a clerk or a secretary in her household. In it, she invited Lepidina to come to her for her birthday, on the eleventh of September, so that by her coming she would make the day more enjoyable. She sent greetings to Lepidina's husband from her own husband and her little son.

And then, at the bottom of the letter, the handwriting changes.

In a different hand, a little less polished, a little more personal, someone has added the last lines. The editors translate the close as "I shall expect you, sister. Farewell, sister, my dearest soul." The Latin for my dearest soul is anima mea, my soul. And that hand is almost certainly Claudia's own. Which would make those few lines among the earliest known examples of Latin written in a woman's own hand, and they were found on this cold frontier in the north of Britain.

A woman, in a fort, on the edge of the empire, wanted her friend to come to her birthday. She dictated the letter. And then, because the dictated words were not quite enough, she took the pen herself and wrote, I shall expect you, sister. Farewell, my soul.

Historians do not entirely agree about how many women lived inside the walls of forts like this one, rather than outside them. Some argue that apart from the commander's own household, the forts were essentially men's places, and that wives and children of ordinary soldiers lived in the settlement beyond. Others point to the women's and children's shoes found inside barrack areas, at Vindolanda and elsewhere, and suggest that some families, at least sometimes, lived right in among the soldiers, perhaps more often than the official rules would have liked.

Now imagine that you step outside the barrack room for a moment, into the lane. The mist has thinned. The air is still and very cold, and it bites at your cheeks and the end of your nose. Above you, between the dark roofs, the sky is full of stars, and the moon is high and bright in the south, laying a silver sheen on the wet stones of the lane.

You walk to the end of the barrack block and look across toward the center of the fort. There, behind the headquarters building, stands the commander's house. Its walls are taller than the barracks and finer, with real plaster on them, and from one of its windows comes a warm, yellow light, softened by the thin panes of greenish glass set into the frame.

You can hear something from inside. Music, very faint, a few notes on a pipe, and voices, and someone laughing, a woman's laugh, clear and bright in the cold air. It is the Saturnalia, and the commander's household is keeping the feast. Perhaps, in that warm room with its heated floor, slaves are reclining at dinner while their masters serve them, as the custom says. Perhaps the commander's children have been allowed to stay up late. Perhaps someone is playing a game of dice there too.

You stand in the cold for a while, listening, watching the yellow window. You think of the people in that room, and of the people in your own room behind you, and of the families out in the settlement beyond the walls, all of them keeping the same festival on the same cold night in their own small ways. And you think of Claudia Severa, long ago, in another fort on this same frontier, waiting for her friend.

As you stand there, a figure crosses the open space in front of the headquarters, a slave from the commander's house, by his clothes, carrying something covered with a cloth in both arms. He walks quickly, his breath smoking, and you catch the smell as he passes, warm bread and roast meat and something spiced. He nods to you, and you nod back. Tonight, for once, he is not hurrying for his master. He is carrying his own share of the feast home to the little room where he sleeps.

High on the walls, you can see the dark shapes of the sentries on the towers, standing very still. One of them shifts his weight from foot to foot, and you know exactly how his feet are feeling.

Then the cold gets into your bones, and you turn and go back down the lane, toward the faint red glow under your own door.

Inside, Butto's letter is finished. Felicio has folded it along its scored line and written the address on the outside, the name of Butto's brother and his cohort and his fort, in small, neat letters. Then he hands Butto the pen, and points at the bottom of the inside face of the leaf, where there is a little space left.

Butto looks at him, puzzled.

Felicio tells him that he can make his own mark there. Anything. A line. A shape. His brother will know it was really him. Butto takes the pen in his big fingers, very carefully, as if it might break, and dips it in the ink, and bends over the leaf. He draws, slowly, something that might be a boar, or might be a dog, or might be a small cloud. He looks at it for a long time. Then he hands the pen back. His ears have gone red.

Felicio says nothing at all about the drawing. He only nods, and folds the leaf again, and ties it with a thin thong, and puts it in his bag, to go with the courier in the morning.

Vindex has been watching all this from his bed, the candle Ammius gave him still in his hand. He is thinking of his mother, and his two little sisters, in their small house outside the fort at Vindolanda, only a couple of miles away. Tonight, he knows, his mother will have lit a lamp and set a little bowl of something sweet beside it for the festival, and the girls will be pestering her to let them stay up late, and she will be saying no, and then saying yes.

He has not written to his mother since he joined the army. He has not needed to. She is close enough to visit, on the rare days when he is given leave. But tonight, watching Butto bend over the leaf of wood, he wonders, for the first time, what he would say to her if he did write.

Felicio sees him looking, and seems to guess his thought, as clerks often do. He says that if Vindex likes, he will teach him his letters over the winter. An hour or so in the evenings, with a stylus and a wax tablet, until the boy can write his own name, and then his mother's name, and then a whole short letter. It is not hard, Felicio says. The army is full of men who learned to write by a brazier.

Vindex says yes before he has even thought about it. Then he looks embarrassed, and says that he does not want to be a trouble. Felicio says that it is the Saturnalia, and that teaching a boy his letters is a better gift than any candle. Ammius, from his bed, says that it is a better gift than any candle he could afford, anyway. Everyone laughs, and Vindex laughs too, holding his candle a little more tightly.

Senecio, sitting in the corner, says that when Vindex has learned to write, he can help with the medical lists, which Senecio himself writes so badly that the doctor at the hospital has complained about them twice. Crescens says that it is a good thing that Senecio is better at bandages than he is at letters, or half the cohort would have died of bad handwriting. Senecio throws a sock at him. It is a clean sock, luckily.

Agilis is thinking, too, of the settlement beyond these walls, where his own son is surely asleep by now, and where the woman he has lived with for ten years will be sitting up by a small fire, mending something, the way she always does when he is on duty in the fort and cannot come home.

Ammius, the decanus, does not say who he is thinking of. He rarely does. But he is turning a small bronze ring around and around on his finger, the way he does every night at about this time, before he lies down.

Vatto puts another few pieces of fuel on the brazier, and pokes it gently with the iron rod, and the coals glow up bright and then settle again, red and steady, warming the eight of them in the small stone room.

## Part Nine — Around Ten o'Clock at Night: Straw, Blankets and a Souvenir of the Wall

The music from the commander's house has stopped. The settlement beyond the walls has gone quieter too, with only a voice now and then, carried a long way on the still, cold air. In the barrack room, the lamp is burning low, and Ammius looks around at his men and says, in the quiet voice that means he is not really asking, that it is time to make up the beds.

Here is a small question for bedtime. How do eight grown men fit into a single back room, perhaps fifteen feet across, and all of them get a night's sleep?

The answer is, close together, and with a great deal of practice.

The back room of a contubernium was where the men lived and slept. Around its walls, there were low beds, or pallets, or perhaps in some forts wooden bunks, one above another, to save space. On each bed lay a mattress, a long sack of coarse cloth stuffed with straw, or with bracken cut from the hillsides in the autumn, which is springy and dry and keeps away fleas better than straw does. Over the mattress went woolen blankets, as many as a man could get, and over the blankets, on cold nights, his cloak.

There was not much room for anything else. Each man's few belongings were kept in a bag or a small chest at the foot of his bed, or hung from pegs on the walls. His weapons and armor and shield stayed in the front room, ready to be snatched up in a hurry. The brazier stood in the middle of the back room, on the floor, where its warmth could reach every bed, and where nobody could knock it over in the dark if they were careful.

Soldiers also kept their gods close at bedtime. Many of them carried a small figure of a favorite god or goddess, in bronze or clay, small enough to sit on a shelf or in a niche in the wall. In the bath-houses of the frontier, altars were often set up to Fortuna, the goddess of luck, who watched over men when they were at their most unguarded, naked in the warm rooms. Others prayed to the old gods of their own homelands, under names that no Italian would recognize, and set them alongside the Roman gods as if they were simply old friends from home who had come to visit. A quiet word before sleep, to whichever god a man trusted, was as ordinary as pulling up a blanket.

Before anyone lies down, though, there is one last walk to make.

The latrine at Housesteads is one of the best-known Roman toilets in Britain, and you can still see it today, in the southeast corner of the fort, at the lowest point of the hill. That was not an accident. The fort had no river running through it. Instead, it caught the rain. Stone-lined tanks around the fort gathered water from the roofs, and their overflow ran downhill, by the slope of the ground, to the latrine, where it flushed a deep channel beneath a row of wooden seats.

Men sat side by side on those seats, with no walls between them, and talked, as men did in the baths. In front of their feet ran a smaller channel of clean water. Many scholars think it was used for rinsing the sponge on a stick that the Romans used instead of paper. Others think the sponge story has been exaggerated, and that people used whatever came to hand, moss, or cloth, or leaves.

At Vindolanda, a couple of miles away, archaeologists found something in the mud in the year two thousand and fourteen that delighted everybody who heard about it. It was a wooden toilet seat, carved from a single board, with a hole in the middle, worn smooth by use. It had been thrown away before the Wall was ever built. The excavators thought it might be the only Roman wooden toilet seat to survive. Stone seats are common enough. But wood, they pointed out, was warmer to sit on, and on a night like this, that matters.

Now the surprising detail of this part, and it is about how these men may have thought of the Wall.

In the year two thousand and three, metal detectorists in the Staffordshire Moorlands, far to the south of here, in the middle of England, found a small, beautiful bronze pan. It is small enough to hold in two cupped hands, and it was once decorated with bright enamel, in reds and blues and other colors, in swirling Celtic patterns. Around its rim runs a line of lettering.

The lettering lists four forts on the western end of Hadrian's Wall, by their Roman names. And then come three more words, rigore vali Aeli. The editors translate the first two as "on the line of the Wall." And the third, Aeli, may well be Aelius, which was Hadrian's own family name. Put together, they may mean along the line of the Aelian Wall, the Wall of Hadrian. After that comes a name, Draco, perhaps the man who owned the pan.

It was a souvenir. It seems that people in the second century had little enameled vessels made to remember the Wall by, much as visitors today buy a mug or a tea towel. Perhaps Draco was a soldier who had served along the Wall and taken it home with him when he retired. Perhaps he was simply a traveler. Either way, somebody, long ago, was proud enough of having been here to have the Wall's forts written around the rim of his best bronze pan.

Historians do not agree about exactly how to read those last words. Some think that Aeli belongs with the Wall, so that the pan gives us the very name the Romans used for it, the Aelian Wall, named for Hadrian's family. Others think that Aeli belongs with the name that follows, so that the owner was a man called Aelius Draco, and the pan says only on the line of the Wall, belonging to Aelius Draco. Both readings are possible. The bronze keeps its secret.

Now imagine that you are walking down the lane toward the latrine, in the dark, with your cloak pulled tight around you. The mist has gone entirely now, and the air is perfectly still and very cold, colder than it has been all day. It is the kind of cold that seems to have a sound, a high, thin silence. Frost has formed on the stones of the lane, and it sparkles faintly in the moonlight, and it crunches very softly under your boots.

The moon has swung round into the west and hangs above the roofs, bright and silver, and you can see your own shadow walking beside you on the frosty stones, long and blue and sharp at the edges. Your breath hangs in front of your face in a pale cloud.

The latrine is a long, low building, and inside it is very dark, and it smells of what it is. You can hear water trickling somewhere beneath the floor. You sit on the cold wooden bench, and you are grateful, at least, that it is wooden. You do not linger.

On the way back, you stop for a moment and look up. The sky is crowded with stars, more than you could ever count, and the long, pale smudge of the Milky Way runs right across it from one side of the world to the other. The towers of the fort stand black against it. Up on the north gate, a sentry is standing very still, a dark shape against the stars.

Then you hurry back down the lane to your own door, and slip inside, and the warmth of the room folds around you like a blanket.

Inside, the men are making up their beds. Butto is shaking out his straw mattress with great energy, which fills the air with dust and makes everybody cough. Crescens tells him to stop. Butto stops. Then he shakes it once more, gently, because he says it needs it.

Senecio is laying out his blankets with great care, smoothing each one flat and tucking in the edges, as precisely as if he were binding a wound. Felicio has put away his tablets and his pens and his little pot of ink in the bag at the foot of his bed, and tied the bag shut, and he is lying on his back with his hands behind his head, looking up at the roof beams and the cloaks hanging there, still steaming faintly as they dry.

Agilis is already under his blankets, with his cloak spread over them and his hood pulled up over his head. Only his nose is showing. He says that after twenty-four winters, a man learns to waste no time between deciding to go to bed and being in it.

Vindex is sitting on the edge of his bed with his candle, unlit, in his hand. He does not want to light it, because he does not want it to burn away. In the end, he wraps it carefully in a scrap of cloth and puts it in his bag at the foot of the bed, next to his spare socks, where it will be safe.

Vatto, who is not yet in his own bed, because he never is until the brazier is settled, has begun to tell a story. He tells one most nights, in a low, slow voice, and the others pretend not to listen and listen anyway. Tonight it is a story his grandmother used to tell, in the Tungrian country, about a winter so long that the river froze from bank to bank, and the people walked across it to visit their neighbors on the other side, and the children slid on it all day, and a fox crossed it at night, leaving a line of small footprints in the frost, all the way from one country to another. That is the whole story. Nothing much happens in it. That is why he tells it at bedtime.

Butto says that he likes the fox. Vatto says that everyone likes the fox. That is the point of the fox.

Ammius walks once around the room, the way he does every night, checking. He looks into the front room at the shields and spears, leaning in their places against the wall. He checks that the door is shut, but not too tightly, and that the little vent in the shutter is open a crack, to let the smoke out and the fresh air in. He glances at each man in his bed, the way a shepherd looks over his sheep, counting them without seeming to count. Eight. All here. All warm. All fed.

Then he turns to Vatto, and Vatto nods, and kneels down beside the brazier. This is the last job of the evening, and the most important one. With his iron rod, Vatto rakes the glowing coals together into a neat, tight heap in the middle of the bowl. He adds a few last pieces of fuel, carefully, and then he takes a small shovel of gray ash from the pail beside the brazier and sprinkles it gently over the top, not too much, just enough to slow the burning down, so that the coals will glow softly all night long instead of blazing up and burning out.

You lie down on your own bed, at last. The straw mattress crackles softly under you and shifts to fit the shape of your body. It smells of dry summer grass and, faintly, of the man who slept on it before you. You pull the blankets up. They are heavy and rough and scratch a little at your chin, and you do not mind at all. Over them, you spread your cloak, still faintly warm from where it hung near the roof. You pull your feet up, in their thick dry socks, and tuck them into the warmest part of the bed.

From where you lie, you can see the low red glow of the brazier, and the shapes of the other men in their beds around you, dark humps under their blankets, and the cloaks hanging from the beams above, and the faint gray line of the vent in the shutter.

When he is finished, the brazier is a low, dull, steady red, like a sleepy eye, and the warmth coming from it is gentle and even, and it will last until morning.

## Part Ten — Around Midnight: The Second Watch and the Full Granaries

The lamp has been put out. The only light in the room now is the low red glow of the brazier under its blanket of ash, and the faint silver gray of moonlight at the crack in the shutter. Somewhere across the fort, the trumpet sounds for the end of the second watch, muffled by the walls, a long, soft note that seems to go on for a long time before it fades.

It is a little after midnight. Half the night is gone.

Most of the men are asleep, or nearly. Butto is snoring, gently for once. Agilis has not moved since he lay down. But Vindex is awake, lying on his back, listening, and across the room, Ammius is awake too, as the decanus so often is.

Here is a small, quiet question for the middle of the night. If the roads are deep in snow and mud all winter, how does a fort this far from anywhere keep eight hundred men fed until the spring?

The answer is that it plans for it, months ahead, and fills its granaries to the roof.

All through the late summer and autumn, while the roads were dry and firm, carts and pack animals came up from the south and the east, carrying grain from the farms of the province, and from the great supply bases nearer the coast. The grain was poured into the granaries, sack by sack, until the long stone buildings were full. Salted meat was packed into barrels. Fuel was gathered and stacked. Leather and cloth were laid in for the workshops.

The historian Tacitus, writing about the forts that his father-in-law Agricola built in the north, said that each one was supplied with enough to last for a whole year, so that the garrison could hold out through a long winter without help. That was the ideal. In a good year, it was very nearly true.

Not everything came from nearby. Some of it had traveled a very long way. Archaeologists digging at the forts of the Wall find the broken pieces of large clay jars called amphorae, and many of them came from the south of Spain, from the olive groves along the river Guadalquivir, packed with olive oil. Others carried wine from Gaul, or the strong, salty fish sauce that Romans loved to pour over almost everything. All of it had been shipped across the sea, and up the rivers, and carried on carts over the hills, to end up here, in a storeroom on a windswept ridge, to be poured out a little at a time over the long winter.

So in the lamp that Felicio wrote by this evening, there may well have been oil pressed from olives that grew under a hot Spanish sun, well over a thousand miles away. A little of the south, burning quietly in the north.

And so, on a night like this, the men in this room do not need to worry where their bread will come from in January, or in February. It is already here, lying in the dark granaries a little way up the lane, cool and dry on its raised floor, waiting.

But the roads still mattered, and a letter from this frontier shows us just how much.

It was written a few years after the famous letters of the prefect's household, sometime in the early years of the second century, still before the Wall. A man named Octavius wrote it to a man named Candidus, whom he called his brother. Octavius was a businessman of some kind, trading on a large scale with the army. And he was worried. He had bought a great quantity of grain, still in the ear, and he needed cash to pay for it, and he was asking Candidus to send money quickly, so that he would not lose the deposit he had already paid.

He also had hides to collect, a large stock of them, at Catterick, many miles to the south. And he had not yet gone to fetch them, he explained, because he did not want to injure the animals while the roads were bad.

That one small line says everything about winter on this frontier. The roads were bad. The animals might be hurt. So the hides waited, and the money waited, and a man sat down and wrote a letter instead.

Historians disagree about who Octavius really was. Some see him as a civilian merchant, a private trader who made his living buying and selling with the army, the kind of man who might have kept a shop in the settlement outside a fort. Others think he may have been a soldier himself, perhaps a junior officer in charge of supplies, doing the army's buying for it in the countryside. The letter does not say, and it could fit either.

Now imagine that you are lying in your bed in the dark, awake, listening. The room is very quiet. You can hear the soft breathing of the men around you, slow and even, and now and then the creak of a bed as someone turns over. Butto's snore rises and falls like the sea.

The brazier glows in the middle of the room, a low, dark red, like the last light of a sunset. You can see it through your half-closed eyes. You can feel its warmth on your face, very faint and very gentle, and the cold of the stone wall at your back, and the weight of the blankets on top of you, heavy and comfortable.

Outside, the wind has dropped away entirely. There is no sound out there at all. The whole frozen country of the north, from the ridge to the faraway hills, is still. Somewhere in that great stillness, a fox may be crossing a frozen stream, leaving a line of small prints in the frost. Somewhere, a farmstead fire is burning low. Somewhere, a sentry is stamping his feet on a tower.

You think about the granaries up the lane, full of grain. You think about the hides waiting at Catterick, and the carts waiting for the spring. Nothing needs to happen tonight. Everything is where it should be. All who ought to be at their posts are at their posts, and the baggage too.

You pull the blanket up a little higher, and you breathe out slowly, and your shoulders sink a little further into the straw.

Around you, the room has its own small night sounds. The soft creak of the roof timbers settling in the cold. The faint rustle of straw as someone shifts in his sleep. Crescens murmurs a few words in his dream, something about a boot, and then is quiet. Senecio breathes slowly and evenly, his hands folded on his chest, as neat in sleep as he is awake. From the next room, through the wall, comes the muffled sound of another man snoring, in a different rhythm from Butto, so that for a while the two snores seem to be having a slow, friendly conversation.

The cloaks hanging from the beams have stopped steaming. They hang still and dark above the beds, drying slowly in the warm air near the roof, the way they have hung above sleeping men in rooms like this one for years. By morning, they will be dry and soft and ready.

Across the room, Ammius is lying on his side, facing the brazier. He is turning the small bronze ring on his finger, round and round, in the dark. It belonged to his wife. She died, long ago, in the settlement outside a fort on the Rhine, in his sixth year of service. He has never spoken of her to any of these men, except, once, to Agilis, on a night much like this one, many winters ago. He wears the ring every day. Every night, at about this time, he turns it, and thinks of her, and then lets the thought go, gently, the way you let go of a bird.

Vindex, across from him, sees the faint movement of his hand in the red light, and does not understand it, and does not ask. He is thinking of his own things. Of the candle in his bag. Of the letters he will learn this winter. Of his mother's lamp in the little house a couple of miles away, burning for the festival.

He thinks that he would like, one day, to write her a letter, even though she lives close enough to walk to. He thinks he would like her to have something with his name on it, written in his own hand.

He thinks about what he might ask for in it, and he cannot think of anything he needs. He has socks. He has a candle. He has a bed, and seven men around him, and a warm brazier, and full granaries up the lane.

Felicio, who is not quite asleep either, murmurs something from his bed. Vindex whispers, asking what he said. Felicio says, sleepily, that he was only thinking that Butto had better get a reply to his letter, or none of them will ever hear the end of it. Then he turns over, and pulls his blanket up over his ear, and is asleep almost at once.

Vindex smiles in the dark.

He listens to the stillness outside for a long time. Then, very quietly, he hears a sound he has not heard all night. It is the softest of sounds, a faint, feathery whisper against the shutter, and it comes again, and again, and then it does not stop.

It is snow. It has begun, very gently, to snow.

Vindex lies and listens to it, the soft, endless whisper of it on the shutter and the roof, and he thinks that there is nothing in the world more peaceful than lying warm in bed and listening to snow falling outside in the dark. He is right about that.

In the morning, the whole ridge will be white. The Wall will be white, and the towers, and the lanes of the fort, and the roofs of the barracks, and the moor beyond, all the way to the far hills. Nobody will be going anywhere in a hurry. The roads will be bad, and the animals will stay in their stalls, and the carts will wait.

Vindex thinks of the snow settling on the shoulders of the sentry up on the north gate, and of the man stamping his feet and pulling his hood down lower, and he is very glad, for once, that it is not him. He thinks of the snow falling on his mother's roof, and on the little house where Agilis's son is sleeping, and on the commander's house, with its lamps all out now, and on the granaries, and on the frozen lakes below the cliffs, and on the wide dark moor where the farmstead fire was burning. All of it, everywhere, is being covered over, softly and slowly, in the same white quiet.

But that is the morning. For now, there is only the soft sound of the snow, and the breathing of the sleeping men, and the warmth.

Agilis, who has not moved since he lay down, stirs a little at the sound of the snow, and opens his eyes in the dark. He has heard that sound on a great many winter nights, in a great many forts, on the Rhine and on this island. He has heard it in tents on the march, and in turrets on the Wall, and once, long ago, lying under a cart by the side of a road, wrapped in his cloak, when the cohort was moving between forts and could not reach shelter before dark.

He thinks that next winter, if the gods are kind, he will hear it in a house of his own, with a door that is his own, and a woman beside him who is his lawful wife, and a son sleeping in the next room who is a citizen of Rome. He will lie in his own bed and listen to the snow, and he will not have to get up for any trumpet.

The thought is so pleasant that he lies there holding it for a while, the way you might hold a warm stone in your hand on a cold day. Then he lets it go, and pulls his hood a little further down over his eyes, and listens to the snow until he cannot tell any longer whether he is listening or dreaming.

Butto mutters something in his sleep and rolls over, and his snore stops for a moment, and then begins again, a little softer, as if he had turned down the sound.

The cold outside has eased a little, now that the snow is falling. Snow often brings a softer cold with it than a clear, starry frost, and the air coming in at the crack in the shutter is gentler than it was. The room is warm. The blankets are heavy. There is nowhere that anyone needs to be.

Vatto, who sleeps lightly, as keepers of fires do, half wakes at the sound, and raises his head, and looks at the brazier. It is still glowing, low and steady, under its ash. He nods to himself, satisfied, and lays his head down again.

In the middle of the room, the brazier breathes its quiet red warmth into the dark, and the snow falls, and falls, and falls.

## Part Eleven — Around Two o'Clock in the Morning: What They Wrote Home For

The moon has gone down behind the western hills, and the snow is still falling, softly and steadily, over the whole sleeping ridge. Inside the barrack room it is very dark now, darker than at any time tonight. The only light is the brazier, a small, dim, patient red in the middle of the floor, like a coal held in a cupped hand.

This is the deepest part of the night, the long middle of the third watch, when everything is still and the morning seems very far away.

Here is a small question for these quiet hours. When a whole fort is asleep, who is still awake?

A few people, always. Up on the towers, the sentries of the third watch are standing in the falling snow, wrapped in their cloaks, stamping their feet softly on the stone. At the gates, the guards are sitting in their small guardrooms, beside their own little fires, listening. And in the headquarters building, in the very center of the fort, there is a room that is never left alone, by day or by night.

It is the shrine of the standards. In it stand the standards of the cohort, the tall poles with their bronze emblems that the men follow on the march, and an image of the emperor. Beneath its floor, in many forts, there was a strongroom, where the men's savings were kept, every coin that Agilis has put away for his discharge. It was guarded through the night, and tonight, somebody is standing there now, in the dim light of a single lamp, keeping the cohort's heart safe while the cohort sleeps.

So the answer is that the fort is never quite asleep. A handful of men stay awake so that all the others can rest. Tonight, the eight men in our room are among the ones who rest.

Out on the towers, the sentries of this watch have the hardest hours of all. The snow gathers on their shoulders and on the rims of their helmets, and they brush it off, and it gathers again. They cannot see far tonight. The moor has vanished into a soft gray blur, and the farmstead fire that Vindex watched at dusk is lost behind the falling snow. So they listen instead. They hear the faint hiss of the snow, and the creak of their own leather, and now and then the far-off call of an owl from the woods along the valley. And they wait, as Vindex waited, for the trumpet that will send them back to their own warm rooms.

Down below the fort, at the bath-house, the furnace has been allowed to die down to a deep, slow heat for the night. Its stone walls will still be warm to the touch in the morning, and steam will rise from its roof into the cold air when the first light comes, as if the building itself were breathing.

The letters, too, are sleeping tonight. Butto's letter lies folded in Felicio's bag. The sack that came up from Corbridge this afternoon lies in a locked room in the headquarters, waiting to be opened. Letters on this frontier traveled however they could. Official dispatches went by the army's own couriers, riding from post to post along the roads. Private letters often went with whoever happened to be going the right way, a soldier traveling on duty, a merchant with a cart, a friend of a friend. A letter might take days, or weeks. It might be carried in a saddlebag through rain and snow and river crossings. It might, sometimes, never arrive at all.

So every letter that did arrive was a small piece of luck. A thin leaf of wood that had made it, somehow, across all those miles, into the right hands.

Now it is time for our third question. Earlier tonight, we watched Felicio write a letter for Butto, by lamplight. And Butto leaned close and asked his brother something, and we did not hear what it was.

So. When these men sat down to write a letter home, what did they write home for?

We have already met some of the answers. They wrote for socks, and sandals, and underpants. They wrote for beer, when the beer ran out. They wrote for money, and for chickens, and for apples, if you could find nice ones. They wrote for things for the Saturnalia. They wrote, sometimes, to invite a friend to a birthday.

But there is one more letter from Vindolanda that I have saved for this hour, because it holds the rest of the answer.

It was written around the years ninety-seven to one hundred and five, a generation before the Wall, by a man named Chrauttius. His name is Germanic, from the lands along the Rhine. He was writing to a man named Veldeius, and he began with the words, "Chrauttius to Veldeius his brother and old messmate, very many greetings." Old messmate. Contubernalis. A man who had once shared a room like this one with him.

Then Chrauttius did a very human thing. He told Veldeius that he was surprised he had written nothing back to him for such a long time. He asked whether he had heard anything from the old folks at home. He asked about a pair of shears that a friend, a veterinary doctor named Virilis, had promised to sell him, and whether they might be sent along by someone trustworthy. He sent greetings to a woman he called his sister, Thuttena. And he asked Veldeius to write back and tell him how another friend was.

That is the letter. A pair of shears. A few names. A gentle grumble about not hearing back.

And that, really, is the answer to our third question. Yes, these men wrote home for socks and beer and shears. But more than anything, they wrote home to be written back to. They wrote to find out whether the people they loved were still well. They wrote to remind someone, a long way off, that they were still here, still thinking of them, still cold on a hill at the edge of the world. The socks and the shears were, in a way, only an excuse. What they were really asking for was news, and kindness, and a reply.

Historians who study these letters remind us that brother and sister, in letters like this one, did not always mean what they mean to us. Very often, they were simply warm words for a close friend. So Thuttena may have been Chrauttius's real sister, or she may have been a dear friend he called sister. Veldeius may have been a brother by blood, or a brother only by years of sharing a room. In the end, it hardly matters. Either way, he was missed.

And now we can hear what Butto asked his brother, at the end of his letter, after the news about the weather and the food.

He asked him to write back.

That was all. Write back, and tell me how you are. And tell me if you ever think of the old days, when we were boys by the river. Felicio wrote it down just as Butto said it, and did not tidy up a single word.

Now, the night grows very soft.

Imagine that you are lying warm in your bed, in the deep dark, and the snow is whispering on the shutter. You are not cold anymore. You have not been cold for hours. The blankets are heavy on you, and the straw beneath you has shaped itself to fit you, and your feet are warm in their thick wool socks.

You can see, through your eyelashes, the soft red glow of the brazier. It does not flicker now. It simply glows, low and steady, like something breathing very slowly in its sleep.

You can hear the men around you breathing too. Slow. Even. Deep.

Felicio is asleep. He lies on his side, facing the wall, his blanket pulled up over his ear, his bag of pens and tablets tied shut at the foot of his bed. In it, folded and tied with its thin thong, is Butto's letter, with its small drawing at the bottom, waiting for the morning courier. Felicio's hands, which have written so many words today, lie still and open on the blanket.

Crescens is asleep. He lies flat on his back, one arm flung out over the edge of the bed, his fingers almost touching the floor. His little hammer and his cloth of nails are put away. Somewhere in his dreams, he is perhaps still mending a boot, or telling a story about a centurion who danced. He smiles a little in his sleep, and does not know it.

Senecio is asleep, his blankets smooth and neat, his hands folded on his chest, his little roll of bandages and his pot of salve tucked safe in his bag. Tonight, nobody needed them. He breathes so quietly that you can hardly hear him at all.

Butto is asleep. His snore has softened to a low, slow, comfortable rumble, like a cart far away on a road. His big feet stick out a little from the end of his blankets, in their thick socks, toward the warmth of the brazier, but not too close. His letter is written. His question has been asked. That is enough for tonight.

You breathe in, slowly, and out.

The room smells of wool, and straw, and charcoal, and the faint sweet smoke of the brazier.

Outside, the snow goes on falling. It falls on the Wall and on the towers. It falls on the frozen lakes below the cliffs, and on the moor, and on the roofs of the settlement where the families are sleeping. It falls softly on the roof just above your head. You can hear it there, the faintest whisper, and then it is so faint that you are not sure you hear it at all.

There is nothing you need to do. There is nothing you need to remember. The bread is in the granary. The cloaks are drying on the beams. The letter is folded in the bag. The watch is being kept by someone else tonight.

You let your hands lie loose on the blanket. You let your jaw soften. You let the weight of the blankets hold you, the way the snow is holding the whole quiet country outside.

Somewhere far off, so faint that it might almost be a dream, a dog barks once in the settlement, and then is still.

The red of the brazier swims a little, the way light does when your eyes are closing. It is the only color left in the world. Warm. Low. Steady.

Vindex is very nearly asleep. He is lying on his side, facing the brazier, his hand resting on the bag at the foot of his bed, where his candle is wrapped in its scrap of cloth. He is thinking, slowly, the way you think on the very edge of sleep, about the letter he will write to his mother, one day, when Felicio has taught him his letters. He will not ask for anything in it. He has decided that. He will only tell her that he is warm, and that the men are kind, and that he thinks of her. And then he will ask her to write back.

He thinks about what he will write first, when he has learned how. His own name, Felicio said. Then his mother's. Then perhaps the names of all the men in this room, one after another, in a row, so that he will never forget them. Ammius. Agilis. Vatto. Felicio. Crescens. Senecio. Butto. And his own, at the end.

He tries to picture the letters, as Felicio drew them in the wax, but they blur and soften and drift apart, like the snowflakes outside, and he cannot hold on to them. It does not matter. There is a whole winter to learn them in.

His eyes close.

His breathing slows.

Outside, the snow is falling more slowly now, in large soft flakes that drift down through the dark without a sound.

Inside, the room is warm, and quiet, and very still.

Across the room, the brazier glows on, low and red and steady, a small warm heart in the middle of the sleeping room.

## Part Twelve — From the Small Hours to First Light: Snow on the Wall

Far away across the fort, muffled by the snow, the trumpet sounds once more, for the last watch of the night. It is so soft now, through the walls and the falling snow, that it hardly seems like a sound at all. More like a memory of one.

In the barrack room, nobody stirs.

Here is a last small question, for the last hours of the night. What is the very last thing a decanus does, before he lets himself sleep?

He looks at the brazier.

Ammius is still awake, just. He has been lying on his side, facing the middle of the room, for a long time now, his hand resting on the blanket, the small bronze ring quiet on his finger. He has listened to the snow begin. He has listened to each of his men, one by one, slip down into sleep. He has counted their breathing without meaning to, the way he counted them earlier in their beds. Eight. All here.

Now he lifts his head a little from the straw and looks at the brazier, one more time. It is glowing low and steady under its ash, exactly as Vatto left it. It will last until morning. There is nothing more to do.

So he lays his head down again. And that is the answer to our last small question. He looks at the fire, and sees that it is well, and lets himself rest.

We have spent a whole long night in this room, you and I. We have walked to the bath-house in the last of the light, and stood on the tower at dusk, and watched the stars come out over the moor. We have pulled on dry socks, and eaten barley and bacon, and drunk a little beer saved in a jug. We have watched a clerk write by lamplight, and a big man draw a picture at the bottom of a letter. We have thrown a Venus with the dice, and held a candle that was a gift.

And all night long, the old voices of this frontier have been keeping us company. A man sending socks and underpants to a friend. An officer asking for beer. A servant hoping to find nice apples. A man grumbling, gently, that his old messmate had not written back.

And a woman, long ago, in a fort not far from here, inviting her friend to her birthday, and then taking the pen herself, at the bottom of the letter, to write, "Farewell, sister, my dearest soul."

Those letters lay in the dark, wet ground for nearly two thousand years, quietly keeping their words, the way the brazier is keeping its warmth tonight. Waiting for someone to come and read them.

Historians will go on talking, for a long time yet, about what this great Wall was really for. A barrier. A border. A way of watching who came and went. They will go on, gently, disagreeing. But tonight, for the eight men in this small room, the Wall is something much simpler. It is the roof over their heads. It is the reason they are here, together, warm, on a snowy night in the middle of winter, a long way from the places where they were born.

Now imagine that you are lying in your bed in this room, in the last soft hours of the night. You are warm all the way through. Your feet are warm. Your hands are warm. The blankets are heavy and comfortable on top of you, and the straw is soft beneath you, and your head is resting easy.

You can hear the snow, very faintly, on the roof.

You can hear the slow breathing of the men around you.

You can see, if you open your eyes just a little, the low red glow of the brazier in the middle of the room. You do not need to open them. You know it is there. You can feel it, a gentle warmth on your face, steady and kind.

Your breathing slows. Your thoughts grow soft and loose and far apart, like snowflakes drifting down past a window. You let them go, one by one. You do not need to hold on to anything. The night is being kept by others. The fire is being kept by Vatto. You are safe.

Let your shoulders sink a little further. Let your hands grow heavy. Let your breathing find its own slow pace, the way the breathing of the men in this room has found theirs.

If a thought comes, let it come, and let it go again, like a snowflake landing on a warm sleeve and melting away.

There is no trumpet that you need to listen for. There is no watch that you need to stand. There is no letter that you need to write tonight. Someone else is on the tower. Someone else is at the gate. Someone else is keeping the shrine in the headquarters, beside the single lamp. All you need to do is rest, here, in the warm, in the dark, beside the low red glow.

The straw rustles softly as someone turns over in his sleep, and then is quiet.

The snow goes on whispering on the roof, softer and softer.

Somewhere, a long way off, so far that you cannot be sure it is real, a cock crows once in the settlement, much too early, and then thinks better of it, and is silent.

Warm. Quiet. Safe.

Vatto, the keeper of the brazier, is asleep. He lies on his side, with one hand stretched out a little toward the brazier, as if even in his sleep he were keeping watch over it. His breathing is slow and deep. Every so often, his fingers move a little, as if he were raking the coals in his dreams.

Agilis is asleep, his hood pulled down over his eyes, only his nose showing. He is dreaming, perhaps, of a house of his own, with a door that is his own, and a son asleep in the next room. He is smiling a little under his hood.

And Ammius, at last, is asleep. His hand lies loose on the blanket. The ring is still on his finger. His face, in the faint red light, looks younger than it does by day.

The room is very still now. Even the brazier seems to be resting. Its glow is so low and so even that it hardly seems to change at all, from one long breath to the next. A thin thread of warm air rises from it, too faint to see, and drifts up among the cloaks hanging from the beams, and slips out at last through the little crack in the shutter, into the snowy dark.

Outside that shutter, the snowflakes are coming down slowly, large and soft, turning a little as they fall. Some of them settle on the sill, and stay there, and are joined by others, until there is a small, smooth drift resting against the wood. You cannot see it. But you can imagine it, if you like. A little white pillow of snow, on the other side of the shutter, keeping the room company.

Think of how far we have come tonight, and how little we have had to carry. A pair of dry socks. A bowl of something warm. A cup of beer. A candle. A letter. A fire. That is all the eight men in this room have needed, to come safely through one of the longest nights of the year. That, and each other.

You breathe in. The air smells of wool, and charcoal, and straw.

You breathe out. Slowly. All the way.

The hours pass. Slowly. Softly. The snow falls and falls, and then, somewhere in the gray before the dawn, it thins, and slows, and stops.

Outside, the whole ridge lies white and still under the last of the dark. The Wall runs away along the crags in both directions, a long line of white on white, until it vanishes into the soft gray distance. The towers wear caps of snow. The lanes of the fort are smooth and untouched. The roofs of the barracks are white, and the roof of the commander's house, and the roofs of the granaries, and far below, the roofs of the little houses in the settlement, where the families are sleeping too.

In the east, very slowly, the sky begins to pale. First a faint gray. Then a gray with a little blue in it. Then, low over the hills, the thinnest line of pearl.

Up on the north gate, the last sentry of the night stands quietly in the new snow, his cloak white across the shoulders, watching the first gray light come up over the land. The moor below him is soft and white and silent. The frozen lakes have disappeared under their own blankets of snow. Far away, on the white hillside, a thread of smoke is rising straight up into the still air, from the farmstead fire that has burned there all night, as faithfully as the fire in our room. The sentry watches it rise, and breathes out slowly, and is content.

Down in the settlement, the snow lies smooth on every roof. In one small house, a woman who sat up late mending is asleep at last, and a boy of seven is curled beside her, warm under a heap of blankets. In another, a little farther along the road toward Vindolanda, two small girls are sleeping, and their mother's festival lamp has burned down to nothing, its little bowl of something sweet still sitting beside it on the shelf.

It is nearly morning.

The light comes so slowly that you could not say when it began. One moment the crack in the shutter is a line of black. Then it is a line of deep gray. Then a paler gray, soft as wool. Nobody in the room sees it change. They are all asleep.

The cold outside is gentle now, and the air is clean and still, and it smells, if you could breathe it, of snow and of woodsmoke from the settlement, where the first fires of the morning will soon be lit. But not yet. Not quite yet. There is still a little while left, a last soft stretch of the night, and all of it belongs to sleep.

In the small stone room, eight men are sleeping.

Ammius, the decanus, the careful leader of the eight, is sleeping, the bronze ring on his finger.

Agilis, the oldest, the Batavian from the mouth of the Rhine, is sleeping, his hood over his eyes, dreaming of his son.

Vatto, the cook and keeper of the fire, from the Tungrian country on the Meuse, is sleeping, his hand stretched out toward the warmth.

Felicio, the clerk, is sleeping, his pens and tablets tied safe in their bag, and Butto's letter folded among them.

Crescens, the shoemaker and teller of stories, is sleeping, one arm flung out over the edge of his bed.

Senecio, the gentle orderly, is sleeping, his hands folded on his chest.

Butto, the big man from the hills along the Meuse, is sleeping, his snore as soft now as the snow.

And Vindex, the youngest, born here on this frontier, is sleeping, his hand resting on the bag where his candle is wrapped, dreaming, perhaps, of a letter he will write one day, and of the reply.

All eight of them are warm. All eight of them are safe.

And in the middle of the room, under its soft gray blanket of ash, the brazier is still glowing. Low, and red, and steady, as it has glowed all night long. Keeping the cold out. Keeping the eight of them warm, until the morning comes.

### Verification notes
- **Composite household.** Ammius, Agilis, Vatto, Felicio, Crescens, Senecio, Butto and Vindex are invented, as the narration says once. Housesteads (Vercovicium) is real. Its garrison in the AD 130s is not known for certain. Historic England and English Heritage give the First Cohort of Tungrians from the late 2nd century. The script says so and makes the men an unnamed cohort "of the same kind."
- **Vindolanda dating.** The narration says each time that the Vindolanda letters (c. AD 92–120) predate the Wall (begun AD 122), and that they come from the same frontier, not from the Wall.
- **Verbatim quotations,** checked in this session against RIB Online/Vindolanda Tablets Online summaries:
  - Tab. Vindol. 628: "My fellow-soldiers have no beer. Please order some to be sent."
  - Tab. Vindol. 155: "builders to the bath-house, eighteen" (narrated with the number spoken).
  - Tab. Vindol. 154: "sick, fifteen; wounded, six; suffering from inflammation of the eyes, ten" (category labels and figures confirmed: aegri, volnerati, lippientes).
  - Tab. Vindol. 310: "Chrauttius to Veldeius his brother and old messmate, very many greetings."
  - Tab. Vindol. 291 closing: "Farewell, sister, my dearest soul" (confirmed). "I shall expect you, sister" is from the standard Vindolanda Tablets Online rendering as remembered, and was not freshly confirmed word for word.
  - Tab. Vindol. 302: "a hundred apples, if you can find nice ones" and "if they are for sale there at a fair price" (confirmed via Romans Revealed and Romans in Britain summaries of the Bowman–Thomas text).
  - Tab. Vindol. 164: only the phrase "wretched little Britons" is quoted. The rest is paraphrased, because the popular rendering "unprotected by armour" is not supported by the scholarly text.
  - Staffordshire Moorlands Pan: "on the line of the Wall" (RIB rendering of *rigore vali*).
