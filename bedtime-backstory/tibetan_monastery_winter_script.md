## Part One — Around Half Past Two in the Afternoon: The Last Sun on the White Walls

It is December in southern Tibet, in the year 1924, and the sun is leaning toward the western ridge. High on a mountainside, nearly fifteen thousand feet above the sea, a small monastery clings to the rock. Inside its shrine room it is dim and still, and on a long shelf before the altar, rows of little brass bowls hold melted butter, each with a single quiet flame. The air outside is so dry and thin that it stings the inside of your nose. And the butter lamps burn on, small and steady, as they have burned all winter.

Not the Tibet of the picture books, all floating hermits and hidden kingdoms behind the clouds. Just one small monastery, on one ordinary winter night, and a handful of men and boys who live there.

And without a few old tricks, you would not have lasted until morning, not at this height, in this cold, with almost nothing to burn.

So tonight I would like you to carry three small questions with you. The first is this. How do you stay warm, at fifteen thousand feet, in the middle of winter, with almost no wood at all? The second. Why is it that in this monastery, the youngest monks, the little boys, are the ones who get up earliest of all, long before dawn? And the third. What is the practice the monks call inner heat, and what did scientists find when they came, many years later, to measure it?

These are not three separate questions. They are three doors into the same small house of prayer, on the same long night, and we will open each of them in its own time.

These monks never quite lived under one roof by these names, but every detail of their night is real.

So let your head grow heavy now. Let your shoulders loosen and drop, the way a heavy robe settles when you sit down at last. Your eyes may close whenever they like, and the lamps will keep burning without you. Let us go back, gently, to a bright, cold afternoon, on the side of a mountain, under a very large sky. So let us begin.

The monastery sits on a ledge of the mountain, about a third of the way up from the valley floor. From below, it looks like a scatter of white boxes stuck onto the brown rock, with one larger building in the middle, its walls painted a deep red along the top, under a flat roof. That red building is the shrine hall, where the lamps are. Around it, stepped up the slope, stand a kitchen, a storeroom, a small courtyard, and a row of tiny rooms, one for each monk, with low doors and windows no bigger than a book.

That deep red band along the top of the shrine hall is not paint on stone, as it looks from a distance. It is made of twigs. Thousands of short, thin branches of a tough mountain shrub, a kind of tamarisk, have been cut, tied into tight bundles, and packed side by side into the top of the wall, with their cut ends facing outward, and then colored a dark reddish brown. The bundles are far lighter than stone, so they spare the walls below a great deal of weight. And in Tibet, that dark band marked a building as something special, a temple or a lama's house, rather than an ordinary barn or home. Seen up close, the band looks soft, almost like fur, a wall wearing a collar.

The rest of the monastery is whitewashed with a wash of pale earth and water, poured over the walls from the roof, so that the buildings shine white against the brown mountain.

Its name, in Tibetan, means something like the White Rock. It is not a famous place. There were hundreds of small monasteries like it across the high country of Tibet in the 1920s, some with hundreds of monks, and some, like this one, with only a few. Through the winter, seven people live here.

The eldest is the head of the house, Lama Ngawang. He is seventy-six years old, small and bent, with a face as brown and creased as a walnut, and very bright eyes. He has lived at the White Rock for more than half a century.

Then there is Jampa, who is fifty-four, a broad, slow, quiet man with the deepest voice in the valley. He is the chant master, the one who leads the prayers in the hall and sets their pace.

There is Kunga, who is forty-three, thin and careful, with long fingers. He is the shrine keeper, and the lamps in the hall are his charge.

There is Lobsang, who is forty-eight, the kitchen monk and cook, with arms like a blacksmith's, and a laugh you can hear through two walls.

There is Sonam, who is twenty, the old lama's attendant and student, tall and earnest, still a little clumsy with his robes.

And there are two novices, two small boys with shaved heads and dark red robes too long for them. Tashi is eleven, and has been here three years. Dawa is nine, and came only this autumn, walking up the valley path beside his father with a bundle on his back.

Seven people under one roof, at the top of the world.

Why did anyone build a monastery up here, on the bare side of a mountain, rather than down in the shelter of the valley?

Part of the answer is the sun. A ledge on a south-facing slope catches the sun from the moment it clears the eastern ridge until the moment it drops behind the western one, and in winter in Tibet, the sun is the greatest single source of warmth there is. Part of the answer is that cold air is heavy. On a clear night it slides downhill, like water, and pools on the valley floor. A house a few hundred feet up the slope can be noticeably warmer at dawn than a house beside the frozen stream below. And part of the answer is quiet. A monastery was a place of study and meditation, and a ledge on a mountain is about as far from noise as a person can get.

So the White Rock faces south, like almost every monastery and village house in this part of Tibet. Its back is to the mountain. Its face is to the sun.

Here is something that surprises many people. Winter in central and southern Tibet is not usually a season of deep snow. Most of the year's rain and snow falls in summer, when the great monsoon clouds push up from India over the Himalaya. By December, the sky over this valley is most often clear, a dark and astonishing blue, with not a cloud in it for days on end. The snow lies thick on the high peaks to the south, but the valley floor below the monastery is brown and bare, dusted white only here and there in the shadows. The cold here is dry and bright. It is the cold of thin air and clear skies.

And the air is very thin indeed. At fifteen thousand feet, the weight of the air pressing down on you is only a little more than half of what it is at the seashore. Each breath you take holds only a little more than half the oxygen your lungs are used to. A newcomer climbing the path to the White Rock stops every few steps, panting, with a dull ache behind the eyes. The monks, who have lived up here all their lives, climb it without a pause.

Just a year before our night, in the winter of 1923, a French writer named Alexandra David-Néel was walking across these same high lands of Tibet, far to the east of the White Rock. She was fifty-five years old. She had studied Buddhism for most of her life, and she had learned to speak Tibetan well. With her adopted son, a young Tibetan lama named Yongden, she was making her way toward the city of Lhasa, disguised as a poor pilgrim, a beggar woman with a stick and a bundle, because foreign travelers were not then permitted to go there. In her book, *My Journey to Lhasa*, published in 1927, she describes that winter walk, months of it, over high passes deep in snow. She describes sleeping out under the stars, and living for days on almost nothing but roasted barley flour and tea. She describes the bitter cold of the passes, and the kindness of the people who took the two of them in. She reached Lhasa in February 1924, about ten months before our evening. Her book is one of the few accounts we have, from the 1920s, of what a Tibetan winter felt like to a person from the outside, walking through it.

You are standing now in the small courtyard of the White Rock, with your back against the warm south wall of the kitchen. The wall is built of stone and packed earth, more than two feet thick, and it has been soaking up the sun since morning. You can feel the warmth of it through your coat, on your shoulder blades. Your face is warm too, in the direct sun, almost hot. But step one pace to the left, into the shadow of the storeroom, and the cold closes around you at once, sharp and clean, like stepping into a river. The ground in the shadow is frozen hard. A little drift of old snow lies against the foot of the wall, gray and crisp at the edges. The sky overhead is so dark a blue that it seems close to purple. Far across the valley, the snow peaks stand white and enormous against it, every ridge sharp, and so clear in this air that you feel you could reach out and touch them, although they are a long day's walk away. Somewhere up on the roof, a string of prayer flags snaps in the wind, red and blue and white and green and yellow, faded by years of sun. The only other sound is a small, rhythmic tapping from the kitchen door.

In our own time, if we are cold in the afternoon, we turn a dial on a wall. Here, the people of this house plan their whole day around the sun. They work outside while it shines, and they come in when it goes.

Historians do not entirely agree about how much to trust Alexandra David-Néel. Some describe her books as careful, first-hand testimony from a woman who knew the language, lived for years among Tibetan teachers, and wrote down what she saw. Others point out that she also loved a good story, and that some of the stranger tales in her books are told at second or third hand and cannot now be checked. A fair reading is probably both. Her descriptions of ordinary things, of tea and cold and roads and people, are vivid and widely accepted. Her more marvelous stories are best read as what she was told, by the people she met.

The tapping from the kitchen door is Tashi. He is sitting on the step in the sun, with a flat stone on his knees, cracking a block of tea. The tea came to the monastery in the autumn, pressed hard into a brick the size of a small book, wrapped in paper and leaves, and it has to be broken into pieces before Lobsang can boil it. Tashi taps the corner of the brick with a smaller stone, carefully, the way Lobsang has shown him, and a dark flake breaks off and falls into the wooden bowl at his feet. He frowns at it and taps again.

Beside him, Dawa is sitting with his robe pulled right up to his ears, watching. He is supposed to be helping. Mostly he is watching the prayer flags.

"Not so hard," Tashi tells him, though Dawa has not touched anything. "If you hit it hard, it breaks into dust, and Lobsang shouts." Dawa nods solemnly. He has heard Lobsang shout. It is mostly laughing.

Inside the kitchen, Lobsang is crouched by the clay stove, feeding it. Upstairs, in his small room, Lama Ngawang is sitting by his window in the last of the sun, with a long, narrow book open on his knees, its loose pages wrapped in a yellow cloth. Jampa is out on the slope above the buildings, walking slowly round a heap of carved stones, turning his prayer beads between his fingers. Sonam is fetching water.

The heap of stones that Jampa is walking around is a mani wall, a long, low mound of flat stones, each one carved with the six syllables of a prayer, Om mani padme hum, and some of them painted, in faded red and blue and yellow. Over the years, pilgrims and herders and monks have added stones to it, one by one, each carved by hand as an offering, until the heap is longer than the kitchen and higher than a man's waist. You walk around it, as you walk around anything holy in Tibet, with it always on your right, and as you walk you pray. Jampa has walked around this one every afternoon for most of his life.

And in the shrine hall, alone in the dim and the quiet, Kunga is standing before the long shelf of lamps with a wooden spoon in his hand and a bowl of soft butter beside him, looking down the row of flames, one by one, the way a shepherd counts his sheep.

They are all still burning, every one of them.

## Part Two — Around Half Past Three: Dung Cakes on the Roof

The sun has slipped a little lower now, and the shadow of the western ridge has begun its slow climb across the valley floor below. Up at the White Rock, the light is still full and gold. But everyone in the house knows that it will not last, and the afternoon work has a quiet hurry to it.

Where does the fire in the kitchen come from, when the nearest real trees are two days' walk down the valley?

The answer is lying on the roof, in neat brown stacks.

The common fact is this. Across most of the high plateau of Tibet, there is no forest to speak of. Above about thirteen thousand feet, in this part of the country, the land is grass and stone and low, tough shrubs, and the only trees are a few stunted willows that people have planted and watered by hand near a village or a spring. Wood, real wood, for beams and doors and the shelves in the shrine hall, had to be carried up from the deep river gorges far to the south or east, on the backs of men and animals, and it was precious. Nobody burned it in a stove if they could help it.

What people burned instead, all across the high country, was dung. Above all, the dung of the yak, the great shaggy cattle of the plateau, which graze on the high pastures all summer long. A yak eats grass, and what it leaves behind on the meadow, once it has dried in the sun and wind, is light and fibrous and burns well, with a steady, low, glowing heat, a little like peat. In summer, when the herds are up on the high pastures, the herding families gather it in baskets. They gather far more than they need, and a share of it comes to the monastery as a gift, carried up the path in sacks on the backs of the yaks themselves.

Some of it arrives still soft. Then it is shaped by hand into round flat cakes, a little bigger than a dinner plate, and slapped onto a sunny wall to dry. All over Tibet, you could see houses and walls covered in these round brown cakes in late summer, and each one carries the print of the hand that made it, five fingers and a palm pressed into the dung. By winter they are dry and hard, and they come off the wall easily, and are stacked away.

And here is the surprising detail. At the White Rock, as in many Tibetan houses and monasteries, the winter fuel is stored on the flat roof, piled along its edges in a low brown wall of stacked dung cakes and bundled brushwood. It keeps the fuel dry, because the rain and snow are rare in winter and the roof is the driest place there is. It keeps it out of the way. And the stacks along the roof edge make a kind of second wall, a parapet, that breaks the wind and shelters the people who work up there, and adds a thick layer of something between the cold sky and the room below.

There are other fuels, too. The brushwood comes from low thorny shrubs that grow on the slopes, cut in autumn and tied into bundles. The dung of sheep and goats, which comes in small dry pellets, burns hotter than yak dung, and in some places it was kept for when a really fierce fire was needed. And there is juniper, the fragrant mountain shrub that grows in sheltered places. Juniper is almost never burned for warmth. Its sweet, sharp smoke is an offering, burned each morning in a small clay hearth on the roof, rising up to the sky.

One of the first Europeans to describe this life in detail was a French Catholic priest named Évariste Huc. In the 1840s, with a fellow missionary named Joseph Gabet, he traveled across Mongolia and northeastern Tibet, and spent months at the great monastery of Kumbum, and then went on across the high plateau to Lhasa. His account of the journey, published in French in 1850 and soon translated into English, is full of fuel. Again and again he describes the travelers, at the end of a day's march, gathering dried dung for the night's fire, which he called by its Mongol name, argol. In his account, a good supply of dry argol could be the difference between a warm evening with hot tea and a miserable night in the cold. Every traveler on the plateau, he made clear, learned very quickly to value it.

You are up on the roof of the White Rock now, standing with Lobsang beside the fuel stacks. The roof is made of packed earth, smooth and hard and slightly springy underfoot, laid over a bed of twigs and stones on top of wooden beams. At the edges it rises into the low brown wall of fuel. Lobsang is lifting dung cakes from the top of the stack, one at a time, and dropping them into a wide basket of woven willow. They are lighter than you would think, about as heavy as a stack of dry bread, and they smell faintly of hay and of something animal, earthy and not unpleasant, now that they are so dry. A fine brown dust rises from them as they fall into the basket, and it glitters in the low sun. The wind up here is stronger than in the courtyard, and colder, and it finds its way at once through the gap at your collar. From the roof you can see the whole valley, the brown fields far below with their stone walls, the frozen silver thread of the stream, a cluster of white village houses with smoke rising from them, straight up into the still air, and the path that winds up toward you through the stones.

In our own time, most of us have never had to think about where the heat in our homes comes from. Here, it comes from a summer's worth of careful gathering, and every single cake on this roof was lifted by somebody's hand.

Historians and scientists do not entirely agree about how this treeless high land came to be. For a long time, it was thought that the plateau had simply always been too high and cold and dry for forest. More recently, some researchers studying ancient pollen and soils have argued that parts of southern and eastern Tibet once held more woodland, of juniper especially, and that thousands of years of grazing, fire and cutting by people slowly cleared it. Others think the climate itself did most of the work. The truth may well be a mixture, and differ from valley to valley.

Lobsang carries the full basket down a notched log ladder into the kitchen, balancing it on one hip, and Tashi and Dawa follow him in out of the sun.

The kitchen of the White Rock is the warmest room in the monastery, and the darkest. It has one small window high up, and a hole in the roof for the smoke, and its walls and ceiling beams are black with a century of soot. Against the back wall stands the stove, built of clay and stones, about as long as a bed and waist high, with two round openings on top where the great copper pots sit, and a mouth at the front where the fuel goes in. It is never cold. Even at night, Lobsang banks it, and in the morning there are red coals under the ash.

Lobsang kneels at the stove mouth and feeds it two new cakes. Then he picks up the bellows. It is a whole goatskin, sewn up into a bag, with a short iron pipe at one end and an opening at the other that he can open and close with his hand like a mouth. He squeezes it gently, and the coals under the dung brighten from dull red to orange, and a small flame licks up around the edges of the new cakes, blue at first, then yellow.

"Here," he says to Dawa, and holds out the goatskin.

Dawa takes it in both hands, very seriously. He squeezes. Nothing happens, except a noise like a sigh. He squeezes again, harder, and a puff of ash flies up out of the stove mouth into his face, and he sits back, blinking, gray-eyebrowed, and Tashi laughs so hard he has to put his tea bowl down.

"You have to open your hand on the way back, and close it on the way in," Lobsang says, and shows him, slowly. Open, and the skin fills with air. Close, and push, and the air goes into the fire through the pipe. Open. Close. Push. On the third try the coals glow orange for Dawa too, and he looks up with an expression of pure astonishment, as if he had made the fire himself out of nothing.

"Good," says Lobsang. "Now you are a cook."

On the larger pot, the water for the evening tea is just beginning to stir. It came up from the spring an hour ago in a wooden barrel on Sonam's back, half water and half slush, with a skin of ice on top. Lobsang drops in the pieces of tea that Tashi broke on the step, and a pinch of something gray and powdery from a small leather bag. The water turns, very slowly, a dark reddish brown.

You are sitting now on a low wooden bench against the kitchen wall, just inside the door, and the warmth of the stove reaches you in slow waves. It is a strange warmth, close and smoky and uneven. Your shins and your face, turned toward the fire mouth, are almost hot. Your back, against the old stone wall, is still cold. The smoke from the dung fire does not all find its way out through the hole in the roof, and a thin blue haze hangs in the upper half of the room, so that the black beams overhead seem to float in it. Your eyes sting a little. The smell is of smoke and hay and boiling tea, and under it, faintly, of butter, because butter is everywhere in this kitchen, in the wooden churn by the stove, in a lump on a slate shelf, in the grain of the bench under your hands, in the very walls. Copper ladles hang on pegs, dented and bright. A row of wooden bowls sits upside down on a shelf. In the corner, a tall earthen jar holds water, with a flat board over its mouth, and beside it a wooden bucket with a crust of ice still clinging inside its rim. Light comes from the stove mouth and from the little window high up, a square of brilliant blue sky, so bright that the rest of the room looks darker for it.

Out in the courtyard, there is a heavy step, and Sonam comes in with the second barrel of the afternoon.

The spring that feeds the White Rock rises from under a slab of rock about two hundred paces down the slope, below the buildings, and the path to it is steep and stony and, in winter, glazed with ice where the overflow has spilled across it and frozen. Even in the coldest weeks, the spring itself does not quite freeze, because the water comes up from deep in the mountain, a little warmer than the air. But it steams in the morning cold, and a thick collar of ice builds up around it, and every day somebody must break it open with an iron bar. The barrel is carried on the back, in a sling of rope and leather, and when it is full it weighs more than a nine-year-old boy. Sonam has carried two today. In summer, the novices help with the water. In the depth of winter, with the path like glass, the old lama has said that the bigger monks will carry it, and the boys will do other things.

Sonam sets the barrel down by the earthen jar, unhooks the sling, and stands for a moment with his hands held out toward the stove, opening and closing his fingers. They are red and stiff. His breath, which was a white cloud outside, has vanished in the warmth of the kitchen. He pours the barrel into the jar with a long, cold, gurgling rush, and fragments of ice knock against the inside of the jar like little bells.

"Is the path bad?" asks Lobsang, without turning round from the stove.

"Like butter on a plate," Sonam says. "I sat down twice."

"Only twice," Lobsang says. "You are getting better."

Sonam laughs too, and holds his hands closer to the fire, and you can see the red slowly fading from his knuckles as the warmth soaks back into them. Water, in a place like this, is a great deal of work, and every drop of it is carried by hand, up a steep path, in the cold. Nobody here washes very much in winter. Bowls are wiped clean with a scrap of cloth or a finger, not scrubbed. Faces are rubbed with a little butter, to keep the skin from cracking in the dry wind and the burning high sun, rather than washed. And the great copper pot on the stove, once it is full, is kept simmering almost all day, so that the house never has to start again from ice.

Lobsang lifts the lid of the pot and looks in, and the steam rises round his face in a soft white cloud. He nods to himself, and puts the lid back, and puts two more cakes of dung into the stove.

Across the courtyard, through the open door of the shrine hall, you can just see the warm gold glow of the lamps, quite steady, in the dim.

## Part Three — Around Half Past Four: The Lamp Keeper's Butter

By half past four, the shadow of the western ridge has reached the foot of the monastery's own slope, and the fields below have gone from gold to a cold, flat blue. The sun still touches the white walls of the White Rock, but low now, sideways, so that every stone in the courtyard throws a long shadow toward the east. In the shrine hall, Kunga is getting ready for the night.

What is a butter lamp, and how do you keep one burning all night long?

The common fact first. A butter lamp is about as simple as a lamp can be. It is a small bowl on a short stem, like a little goblet, usually made of brass, sometimes of silver in a rich monastery, sometimes of clay in a poor one. The bowl is filled with butter. Into the middle of the butter goes a wick, made of a twist of cotton wound round a thin, stiff stem of dried grass or a sliver of wood, so that it stands upright on its own. You light the top of the wick. The little flame warms the butter around its base, and the butter melts into a clear golden pool, and the wick drinks it up, and the flame keeps burning, steadily, for as long as there is butter left in the bowl.

In Tibet, lamps like these were lit not mainly for seeing by, but as offerings. Light was one of the classic offerings set before the images of the Buddha and the great teachers, along with water, flowers, incense, food and music. A lamp stood for wisdom, for the light of understanding that clears away the darkness of the mind. To offer a lamp was a way of giving something precious, because butter was precious, and of making a wish for that light, for oneself and for everyone. Pilgrims came to great temples carrying lumps of butter wrapped in cloth, or flasks of it melted, to add to the lamps. In the greatest shrines, the lamps were counted in hundreds, even thousands, and the shrine keepers worked in shifts to tend them.

At the White Rock, there are twenty-one lamps on the long shelf before the altar, and one great lamp in the middle, larger than the others, as wide across as a man's two hands. That one, the big one, is never allowed to go out. In the summer, when butter is plentiful, all the lamps burn all day and all night. In the lean months of winter, Kunga lets some of the smaller ones burn down at night and lights them again in the morning. But the great lamp, and the six beside it, burn on through every night of the year.

Here is the surprising detail. The butter in these lamps is usually called yak butter, but strictly speaking that is not quite right. In Tibetan, the word yak means the male animal only, the bull. The female is called a dri, and it is the dri that gives milk, and so it is the dri's milk that is churned into butter. A herder in this valley, hearing a foreigner ask for yak butter, might well smile at the phrase.

Dri milk is very rich, much richer than the milk of most cows, and the butter is pale yellow and strong-tasting and keeps well in the cold. It was stored and carried in bags made from sheepskin or from the stomach of a sheep, sewn up tight, and kept for many months. Old butter, gone strong and sour, was not wasted. It was used for the lamps, while the freshest went into tea.

An Englishman named Charles Bell spent many years in Tibet and the lands around it in the early twentieth century, as a British official, and came to speak Tibetan and to know a great many Tibetan families. In 1928, he published a book called *The People of Tibet*, about ordinary Tibetan life, which he illustrated with photographs. One of them shows six bags of butter, sewn up in sheepskin, the way butter was packed for the market and for the long journeys of the caravans. It is a plain photograph of a plain thing. But it shows us exactly what was in Kunga's storeroom, at the back of the shrine hall, on our winter night: a stack of sewn skins, full of butter, gifts from the herding families of the valley, enough to keep the lamps alive until the spring.

You are standing just inside the door of the shrine hall, letting your eyes grow used to the dark. It takes a long moment. The hall has no windows on three sides, and only two small ones high in the south wall, and the light that comes through them now is thin and reddish. Little by little the room appears around you. Two rows of thick, low cushions run down the middle of the floor, facing each other, with a narrow aisle between them. Wooden pillars painted red hold up the roof, and long banners of colored silk hang from the beams, stirring very slightly in the cold air from the door. On the walls are paintings, so dark with age and lamp smoke that you can only just make out the figures, faces and hands and robes and flames, deep red, deep blue, old gold. And at the far end, behind the shelf of lamps, sit the images, statues larger than a man, gilded, serene, their faces calm and their eyes half closed, catching the lamplight in soft gleams. In the middle sits the Buddha, golden, one hand resting in his lap and the other reaching down to touch the earth. On his left is a figure in a tall hat, with a fierce mustache and a staff in the crook of his arm, the great teacher who, Tibetans say, brought Buddhism over the mountains from India long ago. And on his right sits a smaller, gentler figure, painted not gold but pale, dressed only in a thin white cloth, with his right hand cupped beside his ear, as if he were listening to something far away, or singing. That one is Milarepa, the poet and hermit, the most beloved saint of this monastery's teaching line. We will hear more about him later tonight. The smell of the room is thick and sweet and warm, of old butter and incense and smoke and dust and cloth. It is very quiet. Your breath shows faintly in the air, even in here. And the lamps, all twenty-two of them, burn in a long, low line of small gold flames, each one standing perfectly still, because there is no draft at that end of the room.

Kunga is there before the shelf. He has rolled back the long sleeves of his robe. On a low stool beside him is a wooden bowl of butter, which he has softened by holding it near the kitchen stove, and a small flat spoon of horn. He goes down the row, lamp by lamp. In each one, he looks at the pool of melted butter, and at the height of the wick. If the pool is low, he adds a spoonful of soft butter beside the flame, and it slides into the golden pool and melts. If the wick has grown a black cap of soot, he pinches it off with a tiny pair of iron tongs, and the flame brightens at once. He does it all without hurrying, and without a sound, and he does not seem to notice that he is doing it, any more than you notice breathing.

There is one more thing Kunga does at this hour, every evening of the year. On a lower shelf, in front of the lamps, stands a row of seven small brass bowls. Each morning, at first light, he fills them with clean water from the kitchen jar, one after another, in a straight line, so close together that a grain of barley could barely pass between them. Water was the simplest of all offerings, because it costs nothing, and so it could be given by anyone, rich or poor, without any pride or regret. And each evening, before dark, the water is emptied and the bowls are wiped dry and turned upside down, or stacked, so that they rest overnight. Now, as the sun goes, Kunga lifts each bowl in turn, and pours its water carefully into a jug, and dries it with a cloth, and sets it face down on the shelf. The water in the jug is not thrown away carelessly. He will pour it out later in a clean place, on the earth outside, where nobody walks.

The smoke of the lamps leaves its mark on everything. Over the years, the soot rises from all those small flames and settles on the ceiling, on the silk banners, on the faces of the painted figures on the walls, slowly darkening them, the way the paintings in this room have been darkened over more than a century. Every few years, when the soot grows too thick, the images and their glass and gilt are carefully cleaned. And sometimes, after many years, painters are invited, often from far away, to restore the old pictures, or to paint them afresh in bright new colors. And then the soot begins again.

In our own time, light is so cheap and so easy that we rarely think of it as a gift at all. Here, every flame in this room is a little lump of butter that someone milked and churned and carried up the mountain, and then gave away.

Historians do not entirely agree about when the great Tibetan tradition of butter offerings took its present shape. Many Tibetan accounts trace the famous butter festivals, with their huge sculptures of colored butter displayed under lamps on the fifteenth night of the new year, back to the great teacher Tsongkhapa and the year 1409. When the French priest Évariste Huc visited the monastery of Kumbum in the 1840s, he saw just such a festival, and described sculptures of butter so elaborate that he could hardly believe what they were made of. Other scholars think that the offering of butter and butter lamps is much older than 1409, as old as Buddhism in Tibet, and that the festivals simply gave it its grandest form. Both views agree that by the 1920s, it was ancient.

Dawa has come to the door of the shrine hall. He is not supposed to come in without a reason, and so he has found a reason. He is carrying a little clay pot of wicks that Kunga asked for this morning and forgot.

"Come," Kunga says, quietly. In here, everyone speaks quietly.

Dawa comes down the aisle between the cushions on silent feet, holding the pot in both hands. He stops beside the stool. Up close, the lamps are taller than they look from the door, and their flames are bigger, and he can feel the faint warmth of them on his cheeks.

Kunga takes the pot, and takes out a single wick, and holds it up between finger and thumb. It is a little stick of dried grass about as long as a finger, with white cotton wound tight around it from end to end.

"Do you know how to make these?" he asks.

Dawa shakes his head.

"Tomorrow I will show you," Kunga says. "You take the cotton, and you pull it thin, and you roll it on your knee, round the grass, like this." He rolls the finished wick slowly against his own knee, to show the movement. "Too thick, and the lamp smokes and burns the butter too fast. Too thin, and the flame is small and dies. A good wick is a little bit of patience." He smiles. "So is a good monk."

Dawa looks at the great lamp in the middle of the shelf. Its flame is as long as his thumb.

"Does that one ever go out?" he asks.

"Not while I am here," Kunga says. "And when I am not here, someone else will be here. Before I came, there was old Pema, and before Pema, someone else. It has been burning since before Lama Ngawang came, and he came here as a boy younger than you."

Dawa thinks about that. It is longer than he can imagine.

Kunga hands him the horn spoon. "Here. That one is low. Gently, at the side, not on the flame."

And Dawa, holding his breath, dips the spoon into the bowl of soft butter, and carries it across to the great lamp, and lays a little curl of butter at the edge of the golden pool, very carefully, the way you would put a blanket on a sleeping child. It slides into the pool and melts, and the flame does not flicker at all.

You stay a while longer, after Dawa has gone back out into the cold blue of the courtyard, and Kunga has carried his jug of water out to pour it away. You are alone in the hall. If you hold your hand out toward the great lamp, a little way above it, you feel its warmth on your palm, small and real, like the warmth of a cup of tea. If you hold it to one side, you feel nothing at all. One lamp cannot warm a room like this, or even a corner of it. Twenty-two of them cannot either. They are not here for warmth. But somehow, with the light of them on the gilded faces, and the soft sweet smell of the butter in the cold air, the room does not feel cold to you, though your breath still shows in front of your face. It feels kept. It feels watched over. And the long low line of flames burns on, without a flicker, while the light in the two high windows turns from red to rose to gray.

## Part Four — Around Five O'Clock: The Conch at Sunset

A little before five o'clock by the sun, the last gold light lifts off the white walls of the White Rock and climbs the mountain behind it, and is gone. Across the valley the snow peaks still glow, pink and then rose and then a deep, cold violet. Down in the courtyard, it is suddenly, sharply colder, as if a door had been opened onto the night.

How does a monastery with no clock know when it is time to gather?

There is no clock at the White Rock. There are very few clocks anywhere in this valley in 1924, and nobody here would think of needing one. The monks tell the time the way people had told it for thousands of years before clocks came: by the sun. They know where the shadow of the ridge falls at each hour of the morning, and which rock it touches by midday. They know the moment when the light leaves the courtyard wall, and the moment when it leaves the snow peaks. At night, they know the stars. And for measuring shorter times, in many monasteries, they used sticks of incense: a session of meditation might last as long as one stick took to burn from end to end.

And the signal that tells the whole monastery the hour has come is a sound.

Tashi is up on the flat roof of the shrine hall, standing beside the little clay hearth where juniper is burned in the mornings. He is holding, in both hands, a large white seashell. It is a conch, the shell of a big sea snail, as long as his forearm, smooth and white and spiraled, with a mouthpiece of silver fitted to its pointed end, and a little flap of brocade tied to its side. He faces the valley. He sets the mouthpiece to his lips, and takes a long breath of the thin cold air, and blows.

The sound is deep and round and long, a low, hollow, mournful note, like a horn heard across water. It rolls out over the valley and comes back off the far mountains a moment later, fainter. He takes another breath and blows again. And a third time. Then he lowers the shell, red in the face and pleased with himself, and climbs down the ladder.

Here is the surprising detail. Tibet is about as far from the sea as anywhere in Asia. The nearest ocean, the Bay of Bengal, lies hundreds of miles to the south, beyond the whole wall of the Himalaya. And yet the conch shell, a creature of warm tropical seas, was one of the holiest objects in a Tibetan monastery. It came from India, carried up over the mountain passes by traders and pilgrims, and it was one of the eight auspicious symbols of Buddhism, standing for the sound of the teaching spreading out in every direction, as the note of a conch carries across a valley. Almost all conch shells spiral the same way. A rare few spiral the opposite way, and these, which Tibetans called right-turning conches, were treasured above almost anything, and kept in the greatest temples. The shell on the roof of the White Rock is an ordinary one, old and worn, with a crack in its silver mouthpiece that has been mended with thread. It has been calling monks to prayer on this mountainside for longer than anyone can say.

Before the monks come out of their rooms to the hall, it is worth stopping here for a moment, because by now we have nearly everything we need to answer the first of our three questions.

How do you stay warm at fifteen thousand feet, in winter, with almost no wood?

The first part of the answer is the sun. The White Rock was built facing south, on a ledge above the cold valley floor, so that it catches every hour of winter sunlight there is. Its walls are of stone and packed earth, more than two feet thick, and all day they soak up the sun's heat and give it back slowly through the evening, like a hearthstone. Its windows are few and small, so that little of that warmth escapes. Its roofs are flat and thick, of packed earth on twigs and beams, with the winter fuel stacked along their edges, and the rooms beneath them have low ceilings, so there is less air to heat.

The second part is the fuel itself. Not wood, but yak dung, gathered all summer, dried in the sun, and stored dry on the roof. And it is burned in only one place for most of the day, in the clay stove in the kitchen, which is never allowed to grow cold. The kitchen is the warm heart of the house. Most of the other rooms are never heated at all.

The third part is the body. The monks dress in layers of thick wool, a sleeveless vest, a long skirt, a long shawl wrapped over the shoulders, and in winter, a heavy wool cloak over everything, and boots of wool and felt with thick leather soles. They eat the richest food that this high land can give: roasted barley flour and dri butter, and all day long, they drink hot tea churned with butter and salt, bowl after bowl of it. Butter, in this cold, is fuel for a person, as dung is fuel for a stove.

And the last part is each other. When the monks sit together in the hall, shoulder to shoulder on their thick cushions, the room fills with the warmth of their bodies and their breath, and of the lamps, and it is often warmer at the end of an hour of chanting than at the start.

So that is the answer to our first question. You stay warm on the roof of the world with the sun, with thick earth walls, with a summer's harvest of dung, with wool and butter, and with one another. And it is never truly warm, not as we would mean it. But it is warm enough.

Out of the small rooms around the courtyard, the monks come. Lama Ngawang, slowly, with Sonam at his elbow. Jampa, from the slope behind the buildings, his prayer beads still in his hand. Lobsang, from the kitchen, wiping his hands on a cloth. Kunga is already in the hall. And Dawa comes running across the courtyard and remembers just in time not to run, and walks the last few steps very correctly, with his hands folded.

There was a Japanese monk named Ekai Kawaguchi who, at the very end of the nineteenth century, made his way secretly into Tibet to study the Buddhist scriptures, and in 1901 he lived for a time as a student at Sera, one of the great monasteries near Lhasa, which held thousands of monks. He had crossed the Himalaya from Nepal, on foot, in 1900, by remote high passes, disguised for much of the way as a monk from China, and had nearly died of cold and hunger more than once on the way. Like Alexandra David-Néel after him, he knew the language well, and he had come not for adventure but to study. In his book *Three Years in Tibet*, published in English in 1909, he wrote at length about monastic life there, about the huge assembly halls, the prayers and the long hours of debate, and about the tea that was served to the monks at their assemblies. The White Rock is the smallest of shadows beside a place like Sera. But the shape of the day, the call to the hall, the rows of cushions, the chanting and the tea, would have been familiar to Kawaguchi at once.

You follow the monks into the hall, and take a place at the very end of one row, on a cushion that is hard and cold at first under you. It is darker now than it was an hour ago, and the lamps are the only light, a long gold line at the far end, with the faces of the images floating above them. The monks settle onto their cushions, cross-legged, and draw their cloaks around them, and pull the long ends of their shawls across their knees. Lama Ngawang sits on a slightly higher seat at the head of one row, very still. Jampa sits at the head of the other. For a long moment nobody makes a sound. You can hear the faint hiss of the lamps, and somebody's breathing, and the wind on the roof.

You tuck your hands into your sleeves, the way the monks do, and fold your arms against your chest. Your breath clouds in the lamplight, and drifts, and vanishes. Then Jampa begins. His voice is so deep it seems to come up out of the floor, a long, low, rolling note, and the words are carried on it, slowly, in a steady rhythm, almost a hum. The others join him, one by one, until the whole row is chanting in one deep voice, and you feel it in the cushion under you, and in your chest, and in the bones of your face. Now and then Lobsang strikes a pair of small cymbals together, a bright shimmering crash, and a hand drum answers it, and the chant rolls on. The air grows thicker and warmer. Your breath stops showing in front of you. Your feet are still cold. But your hands, tucked inside your sleeves, have begun to warm.

In our own time, when the evening comes, most of us scatter, each to a separate screen in a separate room. Here, at nightfall, the whole household gathers in one place, and sits close, and makes one sound together.

Historians do not entirely agree about how cold Tibetan homes and monasteries really were inside. Some travelers of this period wrote of bitterly cold rooms, of ice in the water jars indoors, of monks shivering through the dawn prayers. Others were struck by how snug a Tibetan house could feel, with its thick walls, its rugs and its smoky stove. Both, almost certainly, were right. It depended on the room, the hour, the wealth of the house, and whether you had just come in from the snow.

As the chant goes on, you notice that nobody is looking at a clock or a candle to know how long it will last. It lasts as long as the prayers last. Some evenings, in summer, when there are visitors from the valley or a special day to keep, the assembly can go on for hours. Tonight, in deep winter, with only seven in the house, it is shorter. At the end of the row, Dawa is sitting with his back very straight, his lips moving. He does not know most of the words yet. Tashi does, and chants them loudly, a little ahead of everyone else, until Jampa, without breaking the rhythm, gives him a long slow look, and Tashi falls back into step. Sonam, beside the lama, chants with his eyes half closed. Kunga, near the lamps, chants and watches the flames at the same time.

On the altar, among the lamps, stand a few small shapes that you did not notice in the daylight. They are cone-shaped, about as tall as a hand, some white and some a deep red, decorated with little discs and petals of colored butter pressed onto their sides. These are offering cakes, called torma. They are made of the same roasted barley flour that the monks eat, kneaded with butter and water into a stiff dough, shaped by hand, and then decorated, with great care, with butter colored by mineral powders and plant dyes. Some are offered fresh and replaced each day or each month. Others are kept on the altar for a year, drying slowly and darkening in the lamp smoke, until the time comes to make new ones. Lobsang makes the everyday torma in the kitchen, in a few minutes, with quick strong fingers. The finer ones are made by Kunga, slowly, on cold winter afternoons, with his long hands dipped in icy water from time to time so that the butter decorations do not melt under his warm fingertips.

The chanting goes on for a long while. The words are verses of praise and refuge and offering, and prayers for the protection of the monastery and the valley, and prayers for everyone in it, and for every living creature beyond it, as far as the world goes. Some of the verses Jampa has chanted every evening for more than forty years. He does not need the book, though a book lies open on the low table in front of him. Nobody in the hall is reading. They all know the words, or they are learning them, the way you learn a song you have heard all your life.

Toward the end, the rhythm changes. The chant slows, and softens, and Lobsang lays down the cymbals. Lama Ngawang speaks a few lines alone, in a thin old voice, and the others answer him. Then there is silence again, a long, full silence, which nobody hurries to break. In it, you can hear the wind rising outside, and the small dry tick of a beam in the roof, settling in the cold.

The assembly is over. But nobody moves just yet. They sit a moment longer in the warmth they have made together, the lamplight on their faces, before they rise and go out into the dark courtyard and the cold.

And in front of them all, the great lamp in the middle of the shelf burns tall and gold, its flame leaning very slightly, now and then, toward the deep sound of the voices, and then standing straight again.

## Part Five — Around Six O'Clock: Butter Tea and Barley Flour

By six o'clock it is fully dark at the White Rock, and the first stars are out, hard and bright and very many, over the black shape of the ridge. The courtyard is a well of shadow. The only light anywhere is the faint gold of the shrine hall door, and the warmer, redder glow from the kitchen, and it is to the kitchen that everyone goes.

Why would anyone put butter and salt into a cup of tea?

The common fact is that, in Tibet, they always did, and that the tea of Tibet is something quite different from a cup of tea as most of the world knows it. It begins with a brick. The tea leaves came from the far west of China, from the hills of Sichuan and Yunnan, where they were picked, steamed, and pressed hard into bricks. The bricks were wrapped and packed into long bundles, and carried up onto the plateau by caravans of yaks and mules, over high passes and along river gorges, for weeks and months on end. By the time a brick reached a valley like this one, it had traveled many hundreds of miles. Tea was so important to Tibetan life, and so valuable, that bricks of it were used in many places almost like money, to pay for goods and services.

To make the tea, a piece of the brick is broken off and boiled in water, for a long time, until the water turns a deep dark red. Lobsang has had the pot simmering since the afternoon. The pinch of gray powder he added earlier was soda, a natural salt gathered from the shores of dry lakes on the plateau, which helps draw the color and strength from the leaves. Then the dark tea is poured, through a strainer of woven horsehair or grass, into a churn: a tall wooden cylinder, about as tall as a boy, bound with bands of brass or leather, with a long wooden plunger that fits inside it. Into the churn go a large lump of butter and a handful of salt. And then the churning begins.

Lobsang stands over the churn and works the plunger up and down, with long, strong strokes, and a deep rhythmic slosh and thump fills the kitchen. He does it for a long time. The tea, the butter and the salt are beaten together until they are one smooth, pale, creamy liquid, the color of milky coffee, with a sheen of gold on top. If you are standing beside him, you feel the rhythm of it through the floor, steady as a heartbeat, and the smell of hot butter fills the whole room, so rich that you can almost taste it in the air. Then he pours it from the churn into a big copper kettle, and sets it at the edge of the stove to stay hot.

Here is the surprising detail, and it has to do with the thin air. At fifteen thousand feet, water does not boil at two hundred and twelve degrees Fahrenheit, as it does by the sea. It boils at only about one hundred and eighty-four degrees, which is about eighty-four degrees Celsius. With less air pressing down on its surface, the water starts to bubble long before it reaches the heat it would need down below. So, up here, boiling water is never as hot as boiling water at home. A pot of rice or meat takes much longer to cook through. And tea leaves give up their strength more slowly, which is one of the reasons the Tibetan tea is boiled so long. As for the butter and the salt, people on the plateau said, simply, that this was what tea was, that it warmed the body and kept the lips from cracking, and that a person could not live up here without it. A bowl of butter tea is not only a drink. It is food, and fat, and salt, and warmth, all at once.

Charles Bell, whose photograph of butter in sheepskin bags we met earlier, wrote a great deal about tea in *The People of Tibet* in 1928. In the same book he printed a photograph of a set of kitchen utensils, and among them is a wooden tea churn and its long dasher, exactly like the one in Lobsang's kitchen. In his pages, as in the pages of nearly every traveler of the time, tea appears again and again as the everyday drink of Tibetans of every kind, rich and poor, lay people and monks, drunk all through the day, wherever people sat down together. And all across Tibet, as visitors soon discovered, a guest's bowl was hardly ever allowed to stand empty. As soon as a little had been drunk, a host would fill it up again, and to refuse too firmly was to seem unhappy with the welcome.

You are sitting on the bench in the kitchen again, this time with a wooden bowl in your hands. It is a small, wide, shallow bowl, carved from a single knot of wood, smooth and dark from years of use, and surprisingly light. Lobsang tips the copper kettle over it and the tea pours in, pale and steaming, and the warmth of it comes straight through the wood into your cold palms. You lift it toward your face. The steam smells of smoke and salt and warm butter. You take a sip. It is not sweet at all. It is salty and rich, more like a thin warm soup than any tea you know, and a soft round film of butter coats your lips. The first sip is strange. The second is a little less strange. By the third, your whole chest is warm with it, and you begin to understand why people up here drink it all day long.

Before you have put the bowl down, Lobsang has filled it up again.

The bowl in your hands belongs to the house. But the monks each have their own, and each man's bowl is his own for life. A monk carries his bowl with him wherever he goes, tucked into the folds of his robe at the chest, where it rides warm against his body, and at the assembly in the hall, or in a house in the village, or on a pilgrimage, it is his own bowl that he takes out to be filled. The best bowls were carved from the hard, swirling knots of certain trees that grew in the warm, wooded valleys along the southern borders of Tibet, and carried up to the plateau for sale. A fine one, with a beautiful grain, could be worth a great deal, and some were lined with silver. Lama Ngawang's bowl is plain and very old, and darker than any other in the kitchen, and he has had it, Sonam once worked out, for more than sixty years.

Then comes the barley. On the low table in the middle of the kitchen stands a leather bag, and in it is tsampa, the roasted barley flour that is the bread of Tibet. Barley is the one grain that grows well this high, in the short summer, in the fields below the monastery. The grain is roasted in hot sand in an iron pan until it puffs and cracks, then ground into a fine, pale, toasted flour, which smells like warm biscuits. Because it is already cooked, it needs no oven and no more fire. You simply mix it with tea.

Lobsang shows you how. You drink your tea down until only a little is left in the bottom of your bowl. Into what remains you tip a big spoonful of tsampa, and perhaps a small piece of butter, and then with the fingers of one hand you begin to mix it, turning the bowl slowly with the other hand, pressing and folding, until the flour and tea come together into a soft, warm dough. Then you squeeze a little of it into a ball in your fist, and eat it. It tastes of toasted grain and butter and salt. It is filling, and comforting, and plain, and with the tea it is enough.

Tsampa is the perfect food for a cold, high, treeless land, and Tibetans knew it. It is light to carry. It keeps for months in a leather bag without spoiling. It needs no fuel to cook, only a little tea or water, or even, in a pinch, cold water from a stream. A traveler crossing the plateau could carry enough of it on his back to last for weeks, and this is exactly what Alexandra David-Néel and Yongden did on their long walk to Lhasa the winter before our night, with a little butter and a little brick tea, and not much else. And tsampa was more than food. At the Tibetan New Year, and at weddings and other happy occasions, people took a pinch of it between their fingers and tossed it up into the air, three times, with a wish for good fortune, so that the air was full of a fine pale dust of barley, and everyone's hair and shoulders turned white with it.

You try a second ball of your own tsampa. It is better than the first. You have put in less tea.

The old rules for monks, which came from India more than two thousand years ago, said that a monk should not eat after midday. In the cold of Tibet, many monasteries kept that rule in their own way, by calling the evening meal something other than a meal, a soup or a little tsampa taken almost as medicine, against the cold. At the White Rock, on winter nights, there is tsampa and tea, and on some nights a thin soup of barley and dried meat and a few dried vegetables, from a pot that has been at the back of the stove all afternoon.

There is meat too, sometimes. That may surprise people who think of all Buddhist monks as vegetarian. Up here, where so little grows, many Tibetans, including many monks, ate meat, mostly yak and mutton, because there was very little else to eat for much of the year. Buddhist teachers often spoke against killing, and many monks would never kill an animal themselves, or eat an animal killed for them, and some refused meat altogether. Meat was usually cut in early winter into long strips and hung up in a cold, dry, airy room to freeze and dry in the wind, until it was hard and dark and would keep for months. At the White Rock, there is a little of it in the soup tonight, a gift from a herding family in the valley, and the old lama says a short prayer over the pot before Lobsang ladles it out.

In our own time, many of us count every spoonful of butter we eat. Up here, in the cold, a family or a monastery counted how much butter they had left until spring.

Historians do not entirely agree about when and how tea first came to Tibet. Tibetan tradition tells of tea arriving with a Chinese princess in the seventh century, or of a king healed by a bird that brought him a tea leaf. Many historians think the great tea trade across the mountains really grew much later, between about the tenth and fifteenth centuries, when tea was traded for horses along the high roads. Both agree that by the 1920s, Tibetans had been drinking tea in enormous quantities for many hundreds of years.

In the kitchen of the White Rock, the household sits close together around the stove. Lama Ngawang has the place nearest the fire, on a cushion with a small rug folded under it, and Sonam sits at his side, ready to fill his bowl. Jampa sits with his back to the wall and his eyes half closed, his big hands round his bowl. Kunga eats quickly, because he wants to go back to the lamps. Lobsang hardly sits down at all. He moves round the room with the copper kettle, and he pours in a fixed order, as tea was poured in every monastery and every household in Tibet: first for the lama, the eldest and most senior, then for Jampa, then for Kunga, then for Sonam, and last of all for the two boys, who wait for their turn with their bowls held out in both hands. Nobody would think of reaching for the kettle out of turn, any more than they would sit in the lama's place by the fire. He fills bowl after bowl, and only when everyone else has eaten does he crouch by the stove and eat his own.

Dawa has made his tsampa too wet, and it has turned into a kind of paste that sticks to all his fingers. He looks at it in dismay.

"More flour," Tashi tells him, with the air of a man who has been making tsampa all his life. "And less tea. You always put in too much tea."

"I only made it twice," Dawa says.

"That is what I mean," says Tashi.

Lama Ngawang reaches over, very slowly, and drops a pinch of tsampa from his own bag into Dawa's bowl, and with one old crooked finger, gives it a stir. "There," he says. "Now it will be good." And Dawa mixes it again, and it is.

Outside, a dog barks once, far down the slope, and is answered by another, further off, from the village. Then the night is quiet again. For a while nobody says anything. There is only the hiss and click of the stove, and the small wet sound of fingers kneading tsampa, and Lobsang pouring tea. The firelight moves on the black walls. Outside, the wind has dropped. Through the kitchen door, which stands a little open, you can see across the dark courtyard to the doorway of the shrine hall, and the soft gold glow inside it, quite still, where the lamps are burning on alone.

## Part Six — Around Seven O'Clock: Lessons by Lamplight

By seven o'clock, supper is over, and the bowls have been wiped clean with a finger and a scrap of cloth and tucked away. The stars over the courtyard have multiplied until the sky is crowded with them. But the kitchen is still the warm heart of the house, and nobody is in a hurry to leave it, because now, by the light of the stove and one small butter lamp on the low table, it is time for lessons.

How does a nine-year-old boy learn a whole book by heart?

The common fact is that, in a Tibetan monastery of the 1920s, learning began with memory. Before a young monk was expected to understand the great texts, he was expected to know them, word for word, so well that he could recite them aloud, at speed, without a single slip. A novice might spend years memorizing prayers and scriptures, hundreds of pages of them, before his teachers began to explain to him in depth what they meant. The explanation would come later, in lessons and in debate. The words came first, so that they would be inside him for the rest of his life, wherever he went, whether or not he had a book.

The method was simple and very old. The boy sat with his book, and read a few lines aloud, again and again, rocking gently back and forth to the rhythm of the words, until he could say them with his eyes closed. Then a few more lines. Then the first lines and the new lines together. Every evening he recited what he had learned to his teacher, and every morning he went over it again. In a large monastery, at certain hours, you could hear hundreds of young voices at once, each reciting a different text at a different speed, a great humming babble like a hive of bees, rising from every doorway and rooftop.

And a book, in Tibet, did not look like a book as we know it. It was a stack of long, narrow, loose pages, each one perhaps as long as your forearm and only as wide as your hand, printed or written on both sides, with the lines running the long way. The pages were not bound. They were kept in order, piled one on top of another, between two wooden boards, the whole bundle wrapped in a cloth of yellow or red or orange silk or cotton, and tied with a strap. To read, you untied the strap, unwrapped the cloth, lifted off the top board, and turned the pages over one by one, laying each one face down on the cloth beside you as you finished it, so that the stack slowly moved from one side to the other. Books were holy objects. A book was never laid on the bare floor, never stepped over, and always placed high, above the level of the feet.

Here is the surprising detail. The paper in many of these books was made from the roots of a small mountain plant, a low wild shrub with clusters of tiny flowers, which grows on the dry grasslands of the plateau. The roots were dug, pounded, soaked and boiled, and the pulp spread out on a frame of cloth to dry in the sun. And the roots of this plant are poisonous. The paper made from them keeps a little of that poison. So the insects and mice that eat ordinary paper leave Tibetan books alone, and a book printed on paper like this could last, in a dry monastery, for centuries.

The printing itself was done from carved wooden blocks. Each page of a book was carved, in reverse, on a single long block of hard wood, letter by letter, by hand, and the block was inked and a sheet of paper laid on it and rubbed with a cloth pad. The greatest printing houses in Tibet held tens of thousands of these blocks, stacked on shelves in dark rooms, smelling of ink and wood. In the 1730s, at the monastery of Narthang, not far from the city of Shigatse, in the same part of southern Tibet as the White Rock, carvers finished a complete set of blocks for the Kangyur, the great collection of the Buddha's teachings translated into Tibetan, in about a hundred volumes. Books printed from the Narthang blocks traveled all over Tibet, and some of the books on the shelf in the White Rock's shrine hall, wrapped in faded cloth, came from there, carried up the valley on a yak's back long before anyone living was born.

Not every boy in Tibet learned to read, and not every monk became a great scholar. But in a monastery, reading was the door into everything else.

You are sitting by the low table in the kitchen, close to the little lamp, and opposite you sits Dawa. In front of him is a flat wooden board, about the size of a large book, with a handle at one end, and its surface has been dusted all over with a fine white powder, like chalk. In his fist he holds a thin pointed stick of bamboo. With great care, his tongue between his teeth, he is drawing a letter in the white dust, a shape a little like a small house with a flat roof. When he has finished it, he looks at it critically, then wipes it out with the side of his hand, and the board is clean, and he draws it again. The Tibetan alphabet has thirty letters, and Dawa has learned nineteen of them since the autumn. The lamp flame trembles a little each time he breathes on it. The powder smells faintly of dust and lime. Behind you, the stove ticks and sighs. The black beams overhead are lost in shadow, and the whole world seems to have shrunk to this circle of light, the board, the bamboo stick, the small brown hand, and the careful white letters, appearing and disappearing.

Dawa is murmuring as he draws, under his breath. Ka. Kha. Ga. Nga. These are the first four letters of the alphabet, and every child who has ever learned to read Tibetan has chanted them in exactly this order, in exactly this sing-song, rocking a little, ka, kha, ga, nga, ca, cha, ja, nya, the way children elsewhere chant their ABC. The first rows of letters are arranged by the place in the mouth where they are spoken, from the throat forward to the lips, so that the alphabet itself is a small lesson in how a voice works. The letters are hung, when they are written, from a line along their tops, like washing from a rope, rather than sitting on a line below them, as ours do. You lean closer, and watch the next one appear, a little crooked, hanging from its invisible line in the white dust. Then Dawa frowns, and wipes it out, and starts again.

In our own time, if we want a book, we can have almost any book there is, in a moment, on a screen in our hands. Here, one book is a treasure, carried up a mountain on the back of an animal, and the words in it are learned by heart, so that they will never need to be carried again.

Historians do not entirely agree about how old printing in Tibet really is. Some scholars think that Tibetans were printing books with wooden blocks only from about the fifteenth century, and that before that, all books were copied out by hand. Others point to evidence that some Tibetan texts were printed earlier, in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, in printing workshops outside Tibet, and that the art came in slowly from more than one direction. Both agree that by the eighteenth century, the great printing houses of Tibet were turning out books in enormous numbers.

At the other end of the table, Tashi is reciting to Lama Ngawang. The old man is sitting very still, with his eyes closed and his hands folded in his lap, as if he were asleep. He is not asleep. Tashi stands before him with his hands pressed together and his eyes fixed on the wall, and he recites, fast and high and almost on one note, the verses he has been learning all week. The words pour out of him in a long stream. He sways a little as he speaks. Once, halfway through, he stumbles, and stops, and his face goes red, and he goes back three lines and starts again. Lama Ngawang does not open his eyes. When Tashi has finished, there is a pause.

"Good," says the lama. "Except for one word. In the second verse. You said 'mountain', and it is 'ocean'."

Tashi thinks about it. He goes red again. "Yes, Lama."

"Tomorrow, again, from the beginning," says the lama, and opens his eyes, and they are smiling. "But it was good."

Sonam is sitting a little apart, against the wall, with a long narrow book open on his knees, and he is reading silently, his lips moving. He is learning a text called *The Way of the Bodhisattva*, a long poem written in India in the eighth century by a monk named Shantideva, and translated into Tibetan long ago. It is one of the best loved of all Buddhist books in Tibet. It is about how to live for the good of others, how to be patient, how to be generous, how to bear difficulty without anger. Sonam has been memorizing it, chapter by chapter, for two winters, and he is about halfway through. Lama Ngawang knows the whole of it by heart. He learned it when he was not much older than Sonam is now, in this same kitchen, by the light of a lamp like this one.

One of the verses Sonam learned last winter is a favorite of the old lama's, and he sometimes quotes it to the boys when they complain. In it, Shantideva asks where anyone could ever find enough leather to cover the whole surface of the earth, to protect his feet from every thorn and sharp stone. There is not that much leather in the world. But, he goes on, if you simply put leather on the soles of your own two feet, it is as if the whole earth were covered. In the same way, he says, we cannot change everything around us that troubles us. But we can learn to guard our own minds, and then it is as if the whole world were made gentle. Sonam thought, the first time he heard it, that it was a verse about shoes. He has been thinking about it, on and off, for a year.

The old lama, Sonam knows, can open any page of that book in his memory and begin to recite it, from any verse, and go on until he is asked to stop. When Sonam first came to the White Rock, he thought this was a kind of magic. Now he knows that it is only years, and lamps, and evenings like this one.

Jampa has gone out. Kunga has gone back to the shrine hall. Lobsang is cleaning the copper pots, scouring them with a handful of ash and a twist of grass, very quietly, so as not to disturb the recitations.

You step outside for a moment, into the cold, to see where Jampa has gone. The courtyard is very dark, and the air bites at your face at once, and the stars overhead are so many and so bright that the sky looks dusted with frost. You can hear him before you see him. Along the outside of the shrine hall wall, under the eaves, runs a long row of wooden drums, each one about the size of a small barrel, set upright on an iron spindle so that it can spin. They are prayer wheels. Inside each one, rolled up tight around the spindle, is a long scroll of thin paper printed over and over, thousands of times, with a short prayer, the six syllables Om mani padme hum, the mantra of compassion. To turn the wheel is to send the prayer out into the world, as many times as it is written inside. Jampa is walking slowly along the row in the dark, his right hand reaching out to each wheel in turn and setting it spinning, always clockwise, always with the wall on his right. Each wheel creaks softly as it turns, and a small bell on its frame rings once, high and thin, every time it comes round. Creak, and ring. Creak, and ring. You watch his dark shape go down the row and round the corner of the building, and hear the bells go on ringing behind him in the cold for a long time after he has passed. Then you go back in to the warmth.

Dawa holds up his board to show Tashi. On it, in the white dust, there is a row of five letters, a little crooked, but quite clear.

Tashi looks at it with great seriousness. "That one is upside down," he says, pointing.

Dawa looks. It is not upside down. He knows it is not upside down. He looks up at Tashi, and Tashi grins, and Dawa throws a pinch of the white powder at him, and Lobsang, without turning round from the pots, says, "Novices," in a voice that means nothing at all, and both boys bend their heads to their work again.

The lessons will end soon. Already Lama Ngawang has closed his eyes again, and this time he may really be dozing, and Sonam's lips have stopped moving over his book, and the little lamp on the table has burned down to a small round pool of gold. Out across the courtyard, in the cold, there is a faint warm gleam in the doorway of the shrine hall, where Kunga is going down the long row of lamps again, one by one, with his spoon of butter.

## Part Seven — Around Eight O'Clock: The Novices Go to Bed

By eight o'clock, the cold outside has deepened into the hard, still, glittering cold of a clear winter night at high altitude. The water in the bucket by the kitchen door has frozen solid again. The stars have wheeled a little way round the sky. And in the warm kitchen, Dawa has fallen asleep sitting up, with his writing board on his knees and the bamboo stick still in his fist, and his head nodding slowly toward the table.

Where do the two boys sleep, and how do they keep warm on a night like this?

They sleep directly above the kitchen, as you will see in a moment. There is a small room up there, reached by a notched log ladder from the kitchen itself, and it is the warmest room in the White Rock, apart from the kitchen below it. All evening long, the heat of the clay stove has been rising up through the beams and the packed earth of the kitchen ceiling, which is also the floor of the room above, so that the floor up there is faintly warm to the touch, like the back of a sleeping animal. A little of the smoke from the stove rises up there too, and the walls of the boys' room are almost as black as the kitchen's.

The room has no furniture except a low wooden platform along one wall, about a foot high, with thick felt mats laid on it. That is the bed. The boys sleep on it side by side, in their robes, with their cloaks over them, and over the cloaks, a heavy blanket of sheepskin, the wool turned inward, which smells of sheep and smoke and butter. Their boots stand by the ladder, and their bowls are tucked inside their robes, as always. The window, no bigger than a book, is stuffed with a wad of old felt to keep out the wind.

It was common in Tibet, in the early twentieth century, for boys to enter a monastery very young, often at seven or eight years old, and sometimes even younger. A family might decide to give a son to the monastery as a religious act, an offering, or because a lama had suggested it, or because the boy himself seemed drawn to it. In some regions, it was the custom for a family with several sons to send one of them to be a monk. The boy's head was shaved, his ordinary clothes were exchanged for a small red robe, and he was given a new religious name. And he was placed in the care of an older monk, who became something like his guardian, his teacher and his parent, all at once, responsible for his food, his clothes, his lessons and his behavior. At the White Rock, the boys' guardian is Lama Ngawang himself, and it is Sonam, as his attendant, who sees to them day to day.

Here is the surprising detail. A boy in a monastery was not yet a fully ordained monk. Full ordination, as a monk with the complete vows, could come only when he was an adult, usually at twenty or older. Before that, a boy might take the vows of a novice, a smaller set of vows, and live by them for years, learning and serving and growing up. The two boys at the White Rock are, in that sense, monks in training. Sonam, who is twenty, took his full vows only last spring. And the word that Tibetans most often used for a monk, any ordinary monk, young or old, was simply trapa, which means something like a student, a pupil. Even the old lama, who has been a monk for nearly seventy years, would call himself a student still.

Alexandra David-Néel wrote about these boys too. In her book *Initiations and Initiates in Tibet*, published in French in 1930 and in English the following year, she described the life of the young novices she had seen in Tibetan monasteries: how they were brought by their families as small children, how each was entrusted to an older monk who was responsible for him, and how their days were filled with lessons, memorizing and chores. She noted how much depended on the character of the guardian, who might be gentle or strict, learned or simple, and how a boy's whole life could be shaped by the man he was given to at the age of seven. The novices of the White Rock, by any standard, have been lucky.

You are climbing the ladder now, behind Sonam, who has Dawa over his shoulder, limp and heavy with sleep. The rungs are notches cut into a single log, smooth with years of feet, and they are warm under your hands from the kitchen below. You come up through a square hole in the floor into the dark little room, and the warmth here is softer than in the kitchen, and closer. It smells of smoke and sheepskin and old felt and butter. A single small lamp, which Sonam has brought up in his free hand, throws long shadows across the black walls. Sonam lays Dawa down on the felt, and the boy curls up at once, without waking, like a cat, and Sonam pulls his cloak over him, and then the sheepskin over that, and tucks it in round his feet. The floor under your own knees is faintly warm through your clothes. Through the stuffed window you can hear the wind beginning to rise again, a long low moan across the mountainside, and a loose prayer flag on the roof snapping now and then in it. Here, under the sheepskin, the wind does not matter at all.

Tashi comes up the ladder after you, and pulls off his boots, and stands them by the hole. He sits on the edge of the platform and looks at Dawa.

"He was crying last night," he says to Sonam, quietly. "When he thought I was asleep."

"I know," says Sonam.

"I told him I cried too, my first winter. For about a month." Tashi considers. "Maybe two months."

Sonam waits.

Tashi shrugs. "He stopped." He lies down beside Dawa, under the edge of the sheepskin, and pulls it up to his chin. "Then he told me about his mother's dog. For a long time."

Sonam smiles in the dark. He remembers his own first winter here very well, though it was eleven years ago, and he remembers the old lama sitting with him in this same room, saying nothing, just sitting, until he fell asleep.

Historians do not entirely agree about why so many Tibetan families sent sons to the monasteries. Some emphasize faith: to give a child to the monastery was one of the most meritorious acts a family could perform, and many families were proud to do it, and many boys grew up to love the life. Others emphasize the practical side: a monastery could feed, house and educate a boy whom a poor family could not, and it offered a path to learning and respect that few other paths in Tibet could match. Most historians would say that, for most families, it was both.

The two boys go to bed earlier than anyone else in the White Rock. And they will get up earlier than anyone else too, long before dawn, in the black cold, while the stars are still out and the old lama is still asleep.

Why should the youngest be the first to rise?

The reason is the morning, and the morning begins with fire and tea. Before the monks can gather for the dawn assembly in the hall, the kitchen stove has to be woken from its ashes, and fed, and blown up with the goatskin bellows into a real fire. A great pot of water must be heated, and tea boiled and churned, so that it is ready to be poured the moment the chanting begins. The juniper must be carried up to the clay hearth on the roof, for the morning offering. The floor of the hall must be swept. And somebody must climb onto the roof in the dark and blow the conch shell, three long notes across the sleeping valley, to wake the house.

All of these are the work of the youngest. Tashi blows the conch. Dawa carries the fuel and works the bellows. Both of them help Lobsang with the tea. And in the quiet hour before the assembly, while the tea is boiling, they sit by the stove and recite their verses, because it was said in monasteries that the mind is clearest and the memory strongest in the very early morning, before the day has filled it with other things.

But there is a deeper reason, too. In a monastery, to serve was part of learning. The youngest served the eldest, as a matter of course, and that service was not a punishment or a burden laid on them for being small. It was a training in humility, and patience, and care for others, which was the very heart of what they had come to learn. Every senior monk at the White Rock once climbed down that ladder in the dark and blew on the coals. Lobsang did. Kunga did. Jampa did. So did Lama Ngawang, seventy years ago, as a boy younger than Dawa.

And that is the answer to our second question. The youngest monks rise earliest because the whole house's day can only begin when the fire is lit, the tea is made and the conch has sounded, and those are the first tasks a monk ever learns. In serving the house before dawn, the boys are already doing what they came here to do.

Before Tashi closes his eyes, Sonam leans over and touches his shoulder.

"Which side?" he says.

Tashi sighs, and turns over onto his right side, and puts his right hand under his cheek, and lays his left arm along his body. This is how monks are taught to lie down to sleep: on the right side, with the knees drawn up a little, one hand under the head. It was called the lion's posture, because it was said to be the way a lion lies down to rest, and because it was the way the Buddha himself was said to have lain, at the very end of his life. Tashi does it every night now without thinking, but he still likes to be asked. Sonam reaches across and, gently, without waking him, turns Dawa onto his right side too. Dawa grumbles something in his sleep about a dog, and is quiet again.

Then the two of them, Tashi in a sleepy murmur and Sonam in a low voice, say a short prayer together, a few lines of refuge and good wishes that every child in this monastery learns before anything else. May all beings be happy. May all beings be free from suffering. The words are very old and very simple, and they fill the little dark room for a moment, like warmth.

You are sitting on the floor by the ladder hole, with your back against the black wall, and your knees drawn up under your coat. The floor is warm under you. The faint red light of the stove glows up through the cracks around the hole, and every now and then, from below, comes a soft scrape, as Lobsang moves the coals. You can hear the boys breathing, Tashi's quick and light, Dawa's slower, with a small catch in it now and then, like a sigh. The sheepskin rises and falls over them. Outside the stuffed window, the wind moans across the mountain and drops away, and moans again. You find that your own eyes are heavy. Your own breathing has slowed to match theirs. It would be very easy, you think, to lie down right here, on the warm floor, and sleep.

Sonam sits on the edge of the platform a little longer, until Tashi's breathing grows slow and even too. Then he blows out the small lamp, and the room is dark, except for a faint red glimmer that comes up through the cracks round the ladder hole from the stove below.

He climbs down quietly. In the kitchen, Lobsang is banking the stove for the night. He has raked the red coals together into a mound at the back, and laid a few cakes of dung around them, and now he is covering the whole thing with a thick layer of fine ash, so that it will smolder slowly through the night without burning out, and be ready, at the bottom of the dark, for two small boys and a goatskin bellows.

Beside the stove he sets out everything the boys will need: the goatskin bellows, a basket of dry dung cakes, a small bundle of brushwood for kindling, and the big copper pot, full of water from the jar, ready to go on the moment the fire is awake. He puts the brick of tea beside it, and the little leather bag of soda, and a lump of butter on the slate. He does this every night. He did it for Tashi's first winter, and he will do it for Dawa's. And then, because he knows that Dawa will be frightened of the dark kitchen, he leaves a single small butter lamp burning low on the shelf by the ladder, its flame no bigger than a barley grain, so that when the boy climbs down in the black hour before dawn, there will be one small light to greet him.

He looks up at the ladder hole for a moment, listening. There is no sound from the boys' room above. He smiles, and pulls his blankets from their shelf.

Across the courtyard, in the shrine hall, the line of lamps burns on.

## Part Eight — Around Nine O'Clock: The Old Lama's Room

At nine o'clock, the White Rock is quiet. The boys are asleep above the kitchen. The stove is banked under its ash. The wind has settled into a long, steady breath across the mountain, and the stars over the courtyard are so thick and bright that the white walls of the buildings glow faintly blue beneath them. You watch Sonam cross the courtyard carefully, carrying a small copper pot of tea wrapped in a cloth, to the old lama's room.

Why does the head of the monastery not have a bed?

Lama Ngawang's room is at the top of the courtyard, up a short flight of stone steps, and it is very small, perhaps ten feet across. There is a little altar against one wall, with a small statue, a painting on cloth hanging above it, and a single butter lamp. There is a low shelf of books in their cloth wrappings. There is a window, with a wooden shutter, looking south across the valley toward the snow peaks. And there is the thing that, to a visitor, makes the room look strange. Where a bed would be, there is a box.

It is a square wooden box, open at the top and at the front, about three feet across on each side, with a low back and low sides, like a very deep, very plain armchair with no legs. It is lined with a thick cushion and a folded rug, and a sheepskin lies over the back of it. This is where the old lama sits all day, to read and to pray and to receive visitors. And it is where he sleeps at night, sitting up, cross-legged, with his back against the low wall of the box and a heavy cloak around his shoulders.

The common fact is this. In the teaching line that the White Rock follows, the most serious practitioners undertook a long retreat, in a sealed house set apart, for three years and three months, and by many accounts three days more. For all that time they saw no one but their teacher and their fellow retreatants. They did not leave the retreat house. And in many retreats, they slept not lying down, but sitting upright in a meditation box like this one, so that even sleep became a kind of practice, and the mind stayed close to waking. After the retreat, many of them simply never went back to sleeping lying down. Lama Ngawang finished his own retreat more than fifty years ago, and he has slept sitting up every night since.

Here is the surprising detail. If you sat in it yourself, you would understand at once. The box is warmer than a bed. Its sides and back block the cold drafts that creep along the floor and across the room. A sleeper in a box, wrapped in a cloak, with a sheepskin round his shoulders and his legs folded under him, has very little of his body exposed to the cold air, much less than a person stretched out flat. The box is, in a sense, a small room within the room. On a winter night in a stone cell at fifteen thousand feet, that matters a great deal.

During the long retreat, the practitioners were taught a set of advanced practices that had been handed down, teacher to student, from India many centuries before. One of these was the practice of inner heat. We will come back to it, later in the night.

More than ten years before our evening, in about 1914, Alexandra David-Néel went to live near a hermit in the mountains of Sikkim, a small Himalayan kingdom on the southern edge of Tibet. He was the gomchen, the great meditator, of a place called Lachen, and he lived alone in a cave high above the village. She settled in another cave nearby, at about twelve thousand feet, with a small wooden room built against its mouth, and there she spent long months, through the winter, studying with him, with the snow piled outside and the paths closed. In her book *With Mystics and Magicians in Tibet*, published in English in 1931, she wrote of that time. She described the silence and the cold, the hermit's austere life, and how he taught her, among other things, something of the practice of inner heat. She was in her middle forties, and she had been studying Buddhism for more than twenty years, and she had never before lived like this, alone in the snow with nothing but her practice.

You are standing just inside the door of the lama's room, which Sonam has pushed open with his shoulder. The cold of the courtyard follows you in for a moment, and then the door is closed behind you, and the room is still. It is cold in here, colder than the kitchen, but not as cold as the courtyard. The one butter lamp on the altar gives a small, soft light, enough to show the old man's face, the gold of the little statue, the dark folds of the painting above it, and almost nothing else. The painting above the altar is a thangka, a picture painted on cotton cloth and mounted in a border of silk, with a wooden rod at the bottom so that it can be rolled up like a scroll. Its colors were ground by hand from minerals and plants, blue from a stone, red from a mineral of mercury, green from copper, yellow from arsenic ores, and real gold for the highlights, and they were mixed with a glue made from animal hide. A thangka could be rolled up and carried on a journey, or hung in a tent, or in a cave, and then unrolled wherever its owner stopped to pray. This one shows Milarepa, seated on a deerskin in front of a cave, his right hand cupped to his ear. It was painted for Lama Ngawang's own teacher, and given to him by that teacher before he died.

The walls are bare and whitewashed and slightly uneven, and they glow faintly in the lamplight, warm and pale. The air smells of butter and incense and of old cloth and old books. And the old lama is sitting in his box, wrapped in his cloak to the chin, his hands hidden in his lap, so still that you might think he had fallen asleep. Then he turns his head toward you, and his eyes are bright and awake in the lamplight, and he smiles.

In the lama's lap, half hidden by the folds of the cloak, is a string of prayer beads, dark and smooth with handling. There are one hundred and eight of them, the number of beads on most Tibetan rosaries, a number with many meanings in Buddhist tradition. On two short cords hanging from the string are small silver counters, like tiny beads on a thread, which he slides along to keep count each time he completes a round of the whole string, so that he can count his prayers in the hundreds and the thousands. His thumb moves on the beads, one by one, without stopping, even as he looks up at you.

Sonam kneels and pours the tea into the lama's bowl, the old dark bowl he has had for sixty years, and holds it out in both hands. The old man takes it in both hands, and holds it a moment under his face for the warmth of the steam, and drinks.

Historians do not entirely agree about how old this pattern of three-year retreats really is. Tibetan tradition traces the practices themselves back to great teachers of the eleventh century, and some say that long retreats of about this length were kept for many centuries. Others point out that the exact form of the retreat as it was kept in the 1920s, with its fixed program and its timetable, was written down and organized by a great lama of eastern Tibet in the nineteenth century, and spread widely only after that. Both views can be true at once: old practices, in a newer frame.

"Are the boys asleep?" asks Lama Ngawang.

"Yes, Lama. Dawa was asleep before we reached the ladder."

"Good." The old man sips his tea. "He is homesick still."

"Tashi is looking after him."

"Tashi," says the lama, and laughs softly. "When Tashi came, he cried for a month. Two months. I sat with him every night." He drinks again. "It is good that he remembers."

Sonam sits back on his heels. In the small light, the old man's face is all lines and shadows, deep creases at the eyes and round the mouth, and the skin on his hands, holding the bowl, is thin and brown and spotted. He was born, by his own reckoning, in a wood year, more than seventy-six winters ago, in a herding family in the high pastures to the north. He was brought to the White Rock as a small boy by his father, walking, with a bundle on his back, exactly like Dawa. He has rarely left it since, except for pilgrimage, and once, as a young man, to study for some years at a great monastery to the west.

In all that time, the old lama has seen a great deal come and go. He has seen four shrine keepers tend the lamps in the hall, of whom Kunga is the fourth. He has taught more novices to read than he can count, some of whom are now old men themselves, and some of whom have gone away to great monasteries and become learned teachers, and some of whom went home to their families and became herders and farmers, and still come up the path each spring with butter for the lamps. He has buried his own teacher, and two of his friends. He has seen summers of good grass and winters of hunger. And through all of it, the great lamp in the hall has never once gone out.

"Lama," Sonam says, "were you cold, in the retreat house?"

The old man thinks about it. "The first winter, very cold," he says. "So cold I could not think of anything else. My hands were cracked, here, and here." He shows the backs of his fingers. "The second winter, less. By the third winter," he smiles, "I had other things to think about."

There is a soft knock at the door, and Jampa comes in, ducking under the low lintel. He bows his head to the lama, and the lama nods to him, and Jampa sits down on the floor by the door, folding his big body down slowly, and waits.

"You will sit tonight," the lama says to him. It is not quite a question.

"If you allow it, Lama."

"Of course." The old man looks at him for a long moment, kindly. "It is a cold night. A good night for it."

Sonam looks from one to the other. He knows what they mean. Jampa, who also completed the long retreat, many years ago, sits up late on some winter nights in his own cell, to practice inner heat. Sonam has never been permitted to learn it. That will come, if it comes, much later, after years of other practice, and only if the lama judges him ready. He has asked once. The lama told him that wanting it was the first thing he would have to give up.

Jampa rises, bows again, and goes out into the cold, and the door closes behind him.

"Go to bed, Sonam," says the lama. "Leave the tea."

Sonam bows and goes. His own room is next door, through a wall so thin that on quiet nights he can hear the old man's beads. It is as small as a cupboard, with a felt mat on a low platform, a sheepskin, a shelf for his books and his bowl, and nothing else. Sonam still sleeps lying down, on his right side, as the boys do. He lies there in the dark for a while, under the heavy sheepskin, with his knees drawn up against the cold, going over the verses he learned tonight, one by one, in a whisper, until the words begin to blur together, and the whisper stops, and he is asleep. Lama Ngawang sits a long while alone, with the bowl of tea cooling in his hands, looking at the small flame on his altar.

You stay with him. You sit down on the cold floor by the door where Jampa sat, and draw your coat around you, and for a while neither of you moves. Then, slowly, the old man sets down his bowl, and leans over, and pushes open the wooden shutter of the little window a hand's width. The cold comes in at once, a thin, sharp stream of it, and the lamp flame on the altar leans and shivers, and steadies. Through the gap you can see the night. The whole valley lies below in starlight, gray and silver and black. Across it, the great snow peaks stand up into the sky, pale and luminous, as if they were lit from inside, and above them the stars are so many and so close that they seem to crowd down onto the summits. There is no other light anywhere, not a single one, in all that wide dark land, except, very far below, one tiny red spark of a fire in a village house. The silence is complete. You can hear the old lama breathing, slow and even, and the faint click of his beads.

He looks at the mountains for a long time. He has looked at them, from this window, for more than seventy years. Then he pulls the shutter closed again, and settles back into his box, and draws the sheepskin up round his shoulders, and his thumb begins again on the beads.

And down across the courtyard, in the shrine hall, Kunga is going down the row of lamps once more before bed, adding a curl of butter to each, and pinching the soot from the great lamp's wick, so that it will burn bright and steady through the small hours, while he sleeps.

## Part Nine — Around Ten O'Clock: The Cotton-Clad Saint

By ten o'clock, most of the White Rock has gone to bed. The courtyard is empty and silver under the stars. Lobsang has rolled himself into his blankets on a mat beside the kitchen stove, where he always sleeps in winter, close to the banked coals, like a dog by a hearth. Kunga has gone to his narrow cell behind the shrine hall. Sonam is asleep in his room beside the lama's. Only two lights still show anywhere in the monastery: the long, low glow of the lamps in the hall, and a faint gleam under the door of one small cell on the far side of the courtyard, where Jampa is sitting alone.

Who was the saint in the thin white cloth, with his hand cupped to his ear?

His name was Milarepa, and he is perhaps the most beloved figure in all of Tibetan Buddhism. He is the great saint of the teaching line that the White Rock follows, and his image sits in the shrine hall, and on the painting in the old lama's room, and his songs are sung in this valley by monks and herders alike. His name tells us something about him. Mila was his family's name. Repa means one who wears cotton, a cotton-clad one, because for most of his life, in the high mountains and the snow, he wore nothing but a single thin robe of white cotton cloth.

The story of his life is told in a famous book, written down by a yogi named Tsangnyön Heruka and finished in the year 1488, at a holy mountain called Lapchi, on the border between Tibet and Nepal. It became one of the best loved books in Tibet, read aloud, and told and retold, in monasteries and around the fires of herding tents. In 1928, just four years after our night, it was published in English for the first time, translated by a Sikkimese scholar and teacher named Kazi Dawa Samdup and edited by an American scholar, Walter Evans-Wentz, under the title *Tibet's Great Yogi Milarepa*.

According to that story, Milarepa lived in the eleventh and twelfth centuries, in southern Tibet, not so very far from where the White Rock stands. When he was a boy, his father died, and relatives took his family's land and goods, and his mother and sister were left poor and humiliated. In his anger and grief, the young Milarepa did great harm to those who had wronged them. Then, overwhelmed with sorrow at what he had done, he went in search of a teacher who could help him undo it.

He found the teacher he needed in a man named Marpa, a great translator, who had traveled to India three times to bring back the teachings. Marpa did not teach him at once. For years, the story tells, he made Milarepa build towers of stone with his own hands, alone, carrying every stone, and then ordered him to tear each one down again and build another, until his back was raw and he was in despair. Through all those years, the story tells, there was one person in Marpa's house who took pity on him. That was Marpa's wife, Dagmema, who brought him food, and dressed the sores on his back, and pleaded with her husband to be gentler, and wept for the young man when he wept. Marpa himself, in the story, is a strange and wonderful figure: not a monk at all, but a farmer and a householder with a wife and sons, a hot-tempered man who shouted and threw things, and who was also, his students believed, one of the greatest teachers who ever lived. His harshness, the story makes clear in the end, was a kind of love, and a cure, measured exactly to the sickness in his student's heart. Only then, when the young man's pride and anger had been worn away, did Marpa give him the teachings, among them the practice of inner heat. And then he sent him to the mountains to meditate.

Here is the surprising detail, or at least the most remembered one. In the caves of the high Himalaya, the story says, Milarepa meditated for years, alone, with almost no food. For a long time he lived on nothing but nettles, the wild nettles that grew around the mouth of his cave, boiled into a thin green soup, until his body grew thin as a skeleton and his skin turned a pale green. Hunters who came upon him at first took him for a ghost. In many paintings he is still shown with a faint green tinge to his skin, in memory of it. And all through the winters, in the snow, he wore only his single cotton cloth, warmed, the story tells, by the inner heat that Marpa had taught him.

One of the best known of all the stories is about the snow. One winter, Milarepa went to meditate in a cave on the mountain of Lapchi, and a great storm came, and the snow fell for days on end, heavier than anyone in the valley had ever seen, and buried the passes. No one could reach him. For months, his followers in the village below believed that he must be dead. When the snows finally melted in the spring, they climbed up to the cave, expecting to find his body. Instead they found him alive and well, and he sang them a song about the snow, and about the warmth he had found within himself while it fell.

And Milarepa sang. That is perhaps the thing Tibetans love most about him. Whenever something happened to him, when a student came, or a hunter, or a demon, or when the snow fell, or the spring came, or someone asked him a question, he answered in song, making up the verses as he went, in plain, beautiful, vivid words that anyone could understand. Hundreds of his songs were gathered together into a great book, *The Hundred Thousand Songs of Milarepa*, which was sung and recited all over Tibet. Many of them are about his life in the mountains: the joy of solitude, the beauty of the snow peaks and the clouds and the wild animals that came to his cave, the sweetness of a simple life with nothing in it but practice. In some of them he sings, almost laughing, about his own poverty, his single cloth and his nettle soup, as though they were the greatest riches in the world. Herders in this valley sing them still, on the high pastures in summer, without always knowing where the words came from.

You are standing in the courtyard now, in the starlight, alone. The cold is very deep. It stings your cheeks and the tip of your nose and makes your eyes water, and every breath is a white cloud that hangs in front of you and drifts away. The ground is frozen as hard as iron under your boots. The white walls of the buildings are pale gray in the starlight, and the shadows between them are black. Above you, the sky is crowded with stars from one ridge to the other, and the long pale river of the Milky Way runs right across it, so bright up here that it seems almost to cast a light of its own. You can hear nothing but your own breathing, and far off, very faint, the creak and ring of a single prayer wheel turning in the wind. To your left, the doorway of the shrine hall glows softly gold. To your right, across the courtyard, a thin line of lamplight shows under Jampa's door.

You walk across to the doorway of the shrine hall, and push the heavy door open a hand's width, and look in. The hall is empty now, and very dark, darker than you have seen it, except at the far end. There, the long line of lamps burns low and gold on its shelf, a little row of small flames, perfectly still, each one standing in its own soft circle of light. Above them the gilded faces of the images float in the dark, calm and gentle, their eyes half closed, catching the light. And on the right, the pale figure of Milarepa sits with his hand to his ear, as if he were listening to the silence of the night. The smell of warm butter drifts out to you, sweet and heavy in the cold. Nobody is there. Nobody needs to be, for these few hours. You pull the door gently closed again, and the gold light narrows to a thread, and is gone.

Lapchi, the snowy mountain where Milarepa sat out that great storm, is still a place of pilgrimage. Pilgrims walk the long, high path around it, as they walk around many holy mountains in Tibet, always keeping the mountain on their right, as Jampa kept the shrine hall on his right this evening, turning the prayer wheels.

Historians do not entirely agree about Milarepa's dates. The book of 1488 gives one set of dates for his life, placing his birth in the year 1052, and some other Tibetan sources give dates about twelve years earlier. And scholars differ, too, about how to read the book itself. Some see it mainly as history, a careful record of a real man's life, handed down by his followers. Others read it as a great work of religious literature, shaped and polished over four centuries to teach and inspire, around the memory of a real teacher. To the monks of the White Rock, the question would have seemed a little beside the point. They knew his songs.

On your way across the courtyard, you look in for a moment at the kitchen door, which stands open a crack. Inside it is dark except for a dull red glow at the bottom of the stove, where the banked coals breathe under their ash. The warmth that comes out through the crack is soft and smoky. Lobsang is a long dark shape on the mat beside the stove, rolled tight in his blankets, with only the top of his shaved head showing, and he is snoring, gently and steadily, a low comfortable rumble like a cat. On the shelf above him, the copper pots gleam faintly in the red light. The water jar is covered with its board. The churn stands in the corner, washed and empty. Everything is in its place for the morning, and for the two small boys asleep above.

At the back of the shrine hall, in a small cell no bigger than a cupboard, Kunga is lying down too. He sleeps closest of all to the lamps, with only a single wall between his head and the altar, and he has slept like this for nearly twenty years. He says that he can tell, even asleep, if the great lamp is burning low. The other monks laugh at this. But he has never once let it go out.

Jampa's cell is the plainest room in the monastery. It is barely wide enough for a man to lie down in, if he ever lay down. It has a meditation box, like the old lama's, and a shelf with three books, and a small altar with a single tiny butter lamp, and that is all. The walls are bare stone, whitewashed. The window is shuttered. A wad of felt is pushed against the bottom of the door, and still a fine thread of cold air finds its way under it and across the floor.

Jampa is sitting in his box, cross-legged, very straight. He has taken off his heavy cloak and folded it beside him. He has taken off his outer shawl, too. He is sitting in only the long skirt of his robe and his sleeveless vest, with his arms and shoulders bare in the cold, in a room where his breath shows in the lamplight.

He sits like that for a long time, without moving, just breathing. Then he closes his eyes.

Long ago, before he ever began, his teacher told him this story, the story of Milarepa in the snow at Lapchi. Every student of the practice is told it. Jampa remembers the night he first heard it, in the kitchen of the White Rock, at about Dawa's age, sitting on the bench by the stove with a bowl of tea, while an old monk who is long gone now told it slowly to all the boys, and he remembers that he did not believe it. He remembers going outside afterward into the snow in his bare feet to see how long he could stand it, and running back in after about a count of twenty, and the old monk laughing at him, not unkindly, and telling him that the heat would not come from his feet.

He remembers, too, the first time he felt it himself, years later, in the retreat house, at the end of a long winter of practice. It did not come like a fire, as he had imagined as a boy. It came very quietly, like the warmth of the sun on a wall, slowly, from inside, until one morning he realized that he had been sitting in the frozen dark for an hour with his shawl around his waist instead of his shoulders, and had not noticed. He told his teacher. His teacher only nodded, and told him not to think about it, and to go on.

You can see none of this, of course, from outside. All you can see, from the cold courtyard, is a thin line of light under a door, and the faint white cloud of your own breath, rising toward the stars.

He is fifty-four now. He has sat like this on many winter nights.

On the altar beside him, the little lamp burns very still. And across the courtyard, behind the closed door of the shrine hall, the great lamp burns on in its long line of small flames, watched over by no one at all for these few hours, and steady, all the same, in the dark.

## Part Ten — Around Eleven O'Clock: The Fire Within

Eleven o'clock comes to the White Rock without a sound. The stars have turned a little further round the sky, and the great river of the Milky Way has tilted over the western ridge. The cold has settled into the stones of the courtyard and the beams of the roofs. In the shrine hall, the lamps burn low and gold. And in his small bare cell, Jampa sits on in the cold, with his arms and shoulders bare, breathing slowly.

What is he doing?

He is practicing what Tibetans call tummo, a word usually translated as inner heat, or inner fire. The word itself is unusual. It means something like the fierce one, and in Tibetan it has a feminine ending, so that it might be called the fierce lady, or the fierce woman, a name for a power that is gentle and burning at once.

Tummo is one of a group of six advanced practices, the Six Yogas of Naropa, which are named after a great Indian teacher of the eleventh century. Naropa himself, as Tibetan tradition remembers him, had been a great scholar, one of the most learned teachers at the famous Buddhist university of Nalanda, in northern India. One day, the story goes, he realized that for all his learning he understood the words of the teachings but not their meaning, and he left his high position to wander in search of a teacher who could show him. He found that teacher, a wild and mysterious yogi named Tilopa, only after long years of searching and hardship. It is a story that Tibetans love to tell young scholars like Sonam, who know their texts by heart. Naropa taught these practices to his students in India. Marpa the translator, Milarepa's teacher, carried them over the mountains to Tibet. And from Marpa they passed to Milarepa, and from him, teacher to student, in an unbroken line, down through the centuries, to the old lama's teacher, to Lama Ngawang, and to Jampa. They were never taught openly. A student was given them only by a qualified teacher, in person, and only after years of other practice to prepare the ground. Even now, the details are kept private, between teacher and student.

What can be said, in outline, is this. The practitioner sits upright, in a firm, steady posture. He works with the breath, in particular with a way of drawing the breath deep down into the belly and holding it there, gently, which is sometimes called the vase breath, because the belly is rounded and filled like a vase. And he works with the mind, with visualization: he imagines his body as hollow and clear, like a tent of light, with fine channels running through it, and, below the navel, a tiny flame, very small and very hot. As the practice goes on, that flame is imagined to blaze up, and to warm and melt and fill the whole body with heat and bliss.

But the heat was never the point. In the eyes of the teachers who handed it down, the warmth of the body was only a sign, a side effect, a kind of proof along the way. The purpose of tummo was to bring the mind to a state of deep bliss and clarity, and through that, closer to the awakening that the Buddha taught. A practitioner who sought only to be warm, they would have said, had missed everything that mattered. That is what the old lama meant when he told Sonam that wanting it was the first thing he would have to give up.

Here is the surprising detail. In Tibet, there were traditions of testing the practitioners of inner heat. Alexandra David-Néel, who had learned something of it herself from the hermit of Lachen, described one such test in *With Mystics and Magicians in Tibet*. As she told it, on a winter night of hard frost, the students who had trained in tummo would sit by a frozen stream or lake, wearing almost nothing. Sheets of cloth were dipped in the icy water, through a hole broken in the ice, and wrapped around each one in turn, and each student had to dry the sheet with the heat of his own body. When it was dry, it was dipped again, and wrapped around him again. And so on, all through the night, until the dawn, when the one who had dried the most sheets was honored above the rest. Those who did well in such trials, as she understood it, earned the right to wear the single white cotton robe of the accomplished practitioner, and to be called by the name that Milarepa had made famous. She wrote, too, of the respas, the cotton-clad hermits, named like Milarepa, who were said to live through the whole Tibetan winter in the mountains wearing nothing but one thin cotton cloth.

Stories like these, for many years, were simply stories, to most readers in the West. Then, in the late 1970s, a cardiologist at Harvard Medical School named Herbert Benson began to look into them. He had spent years studying how meditation affects the body, the heart rate, the breathing, the blood pressure, and in 1975 he had written a popular book, *The Relaxation Response*, about the quiet, restful state that he found the body enters during meditation and prayer. And he had read the accounts of tummo. So in February 1981, he and his colleagues traveled to a town in the hills of northern India, and there three Tibetan monks who were skilled in tummo agreed to let the scientists to attach small temperature sensors to their bodies, and then sat down to practice.

The results were published in the journal *Nature*, in January 1982. While the monks meditated, the temperature of their fingers and toes rose, in some cases by more than eight degrees Celsius, about fifteen degrees Fahrenheit. Their hands and feet, the parts of the body that grow coldest first, grew markedly warmer. The scientists wrote that "the most likely mechanism to account for the increase in finger and toe temperature is vasodilation," a widening of the small blood vessels near the skin, so that more warm blood flowed out to the fingers and toes.

A few years later, in 1985, Benson's team returned, and this time they filmed monks practicing the old test of the wet sheets, drying cold, wet cloths on their bare bodies while wearing sensors. Two of the monks dried the sheets. But Benson himself was careful not to claim too much. Years afterward, speaking to the *Harvard Gazette*, he said that "the room was not cold enough to do the tests properly."

Benson's team went on studying Tibetan meditators for years. In another study, published in 1990, they worked with monks at a monastery in Sikkim, and found that during certain advanced practices, some of them could slow their body's use of oxygen far below its ordinary resting level, more than the scientists had ever seen, while in other practices they could raise it. The body, it seemed, was answering the mind in ways that the textbooks of the time had not expected.

You are sitting now in the corner of Jampa's cell, on the cold floor, with your back against the stone, very still. The small lamp on the altar gives just enough light to see him. He sits upright in his box, his hands resting in his lap, his eyes closed. His bare shoulders and arms are brown in the lamplight, and you can see the slow rise and fall of his breath, deep and unhurried, his belly filling and holding, and releasing, and filling again. The cold in the room is fierce. Your own fingers, tucked in your armpits, are aching with it. Your breath clouds in front of your face. And yet his shoulders do not shiver. His face is calm, utterly calm, as if he were sitting in the summer sun. Once, very faintly, you seem to see a thin haze of mist rise from his skin in the cold air, and drift up past the lamp, though when you look again, it is gone, and you cannot be sure you saw it at all. The only sound is his breathing, and the small dry hiss of the lamp.

The rhythm of his breathing has changed now. It is slower, and fuller, with long pauses in it, when the breath is held, and the room is so quiet that you can hear the lamp flame fluttering very softly on its wick. Then a long, slow breath out, like a sigh, and the cloud of it drifts up in the lamplight. Then the slow breath in again, deep, deep, down into the belly, and the pause. You find, without meaning to, that your own breathing has begun to follow his. In. Hold. Out. The cold is still in your fingers and your feet. But your chest feels warmer, and steadier, and your mind feels very quiet, as quiet as the room. Whatever Jampa is seeing behind his closed eyes, whatever small fierce flame he is tending there, you cannot see it. You can only sit with him in the stillness and the cold, and breathe, and watch the lamp.

The science did not stop there. In 2013, a team of scientists led by a psychologist named Maria Kozhevnikov published the results of new measurements, taken in monasteries in eastern Tibet. They found that during one particular style of tummo, which uses a forceful kind of breathing, the practitioners' body temperatures, measured under the arm, rose as high as about thirty-eight degrees Celsius, a little over one hundred degrees Fahrenheit, the warmth of a mild fever, in a cold room. And they found that the visualization, the meditation of the inner flame, seemed to be what allowed the practitioners to keep that warmth going for long periods. Ordinary volunteers who were taught only the breathing could raise their temperatures a little too, but only for a short time.

Scientists do not entirely agree about what all this means. Some, from the very beginning, urged caution. When Benson's first results appeared in 1982, another scientist wrote to *Nature* to point out that the study had no control group, no ordinary people sitting in the same room to compare with, and that without one it was hard to say how much the meditation itself was doing. Benson answered, in the same journal, that the monks could not easily be used as their own comparison on another day, because whenever they sat quietly, they slipped naturally into their meditation. For men who had practiced for so long, simply sitting still was already a kind of practice. Others think that most of the warmth can be explained by the breathing and by the body's muscles and blood vessels, and that the mind plays a smaller part. Others again, including the scientists of 2013, believe the meditation is essential. And the practitioners themselves, if asked, tended to say gently that the thermometers measured the least important thing about it.

In the cell, Jampa's breathing slows, and lengthens, and at last grows quiet and natural, and he opens his eyes. He sits a while longer without moving, looking at the small flame on his altar. Then he reaches for his shawl, and his heavy cloak, and draws them slowly round his shoulders, and settles back into his box. If you were to touch his hand now, it would be warm, as warm as a hand that has just been holding a bowl of tea. You uncurl your own stiff fingers from under your arms, and blow on them, and they ache as the blood comes back. The cold in the little room has not changed at all. The frost on the inside of the shutter glitters faintly in the lamplight, a fine white fur of it along the cracks. Your breath still clouds. But Jampa, wrapped now in his heavy cloak, sits as easily as a man by a summer fire, and when he breathes out, slowly, his breath clouds in the lamplight too, and drifts, and fades. He does not feel the cold. He feels very peaceful, and very tired, and a long way from the world.

Before he sleeps, he says the words that close every practice in this monastery, from the shortest prayer to the longest retreat. He says them silently, with his lips moving a little in the lamplight. Whatever good has come from this, he wishes, may it go to all beings, everywhere, without exception. May it ease their suffering. May it bring them happiness. May it help them, one day, to wake. Every monk at the White Rock ends his practice this way, and every novice is taught to, even before he knows what the words mean. Nothing is kept back. The warmth, whatever it was, is given away.

And that is the answer to our third question. Inner heat is a practice of breath and body and mind, handed down from teacher to student in Tibet for nearly a thousand years, as a path toward wisdom and bliss, of which the warmth is only a sign along the way. And when scientists came to measure it, they found that the warmth, at least, was real: fingers and toes growing warmer in the cold, and bodies running warmer than they should, in ways that are still not wholly understood. The monks of the White Rock, of course, never needed a thermometer to tell them that. They had Milarepa's songs, and their teachers' word, and the long line of practitioners before them, each of whom had sat, on nights like this one, in the cold, and found the fire.

Jampa closes his eyes again. His head bows a little, onto his chest. And across the courtyard, behind the closed door of the hall, the long low line of lamps burns on in the dark, gold and patient, keeping the night.

## Part Eleven — Around One in the Morning: The Moon Over the Snow Peaks

Midnight has come and gone at the White Rock, and nobody heard it pass. There was nothing to hear. No bell, no clock, no voice. Only the slow turning of the stars, and the deep, still cold, and the quiet breathing of seven sleepers in their small rooms.

Who keeps the lamps alive, while the lamp keeper sleeps?

The lamp keeper does.

At about one o'clock, in his narrow cell behind the shrine hall, Kunga opens his eyes. Nothing has woken him. No sound, no light. He simply wakes, the way he has woken at this hour on almost every night for twenty years, as if a quiet hand had touched his shoulder. He lies still for a moment under his sheepskin, listening. Then he sits up, and finds his cloak in the dark, and wraps it round him, and pushes his feet into his felt boots, and goes out.

You go with him.

The courtyard is silver. While everyone slept, the moon has risen over the eastern ridge, a thin waning moon, a little less than half, lying on its back above the mountains. Its light is cool and pale and very clear, and it falls across the white walls and the frozen ground and the stacks of fuel on the roofs, and every stone in the courtyard has a soft blue shadow. Far across the valley, the great snow peaks shine in it, white and silent and enormous, as if they were made of moonlight themselves. Your breath is a small white cloud. The cold is deep, but it is a still cold now, without wind, and it does not bite as it did. It only holds you, close and quiet.

Kunga crosses the courtyard without a sound, and lifts the latch of the shrine hall door, and slips inside, and you follow him into the warm gold dark.

The lamps have burned low. Not out, not one of them. But low. The pools of butter in the small bowls have shrunk to little gold coins at the bottom, and the flames have grown smaller and rounder, and they sit very still on their wicks. The great lamp in the middle still burns tall, because it holds so much more. Its light is the strongest thing in the room. The gilded faces of the images above it glow softly, warm and calm, as if they had been waiting.

Kunga lights a taper from the great lamp, and sets it in a holder on his stool, so that he can see. Then he takes the bowl of soft butter, which he left by the stove in the kitchen last evening, wrapped in a cloth, so that it would still be soft. And he begins.

You watch him go down the line, one lamp at a time. He looks. He lays a little curl of butter beside the flame, and it slides into the pool and melts, golden and clear. He pinches the black cap from the wick with his tiny iron tongs. The flame brightens and steadies. He moves on to the next. There is no hurry in anything he does. There is no sound except the soft scrape of the horn spoon against the bowl, and the faint hiss of the flames, and his own slow breathing.

You stand beside him, and the warmth of the lamps is on your face, small and gentle. The smell of the butter is sweet and heavy and close. In the gold light, your own hands, held out in front of you, look warm and brown and old, like the hands in the paintings on the walls.

As he works, Kunga murmurs something under his breath, so quietly that at first you think he is only breathing. Then you hear the words. It is a verse from the book that Sonam is learning, *The Way of the Bodhisattva*, written by Shantideva in India in the eighth century, more than eleven hundred years before tonight. It is the verse that Tibetans love perhaps most of all in the whole book, and it comes near the very end. In it, Shantideva prays that for as long as space itself remains, and for as long as there are living beings in the world, he too may remain, to ease the sorrows of the world. Kunga says it over each lamp in turn, very softly, as he feeds it. He has said it over these lamps every night for twenty years. He will say it tomorrow night too.

People who write about the old monasteries of Tibet have sometimes wondered, gently, whether a quiet life like this one, in a small house on a mountain, was a turning away from the world, or a gift to it. Some see in it a withdrawal: men who left their families and fields to tend their own minds. Others see something closer to Kunga's verse: a small light kept burning on behalf of everyone, night after night, whether anyone in the valley below is awake to see it or not. Perhaps, here, in the gold dark, at one o'clock in the morning, it is not necessary to choose.

At the end of the row, Kunga stands back and looks at the lamps, all twenty-two of them, burning bright and steady again, in their long, low, golden line. He nods, very slightly, the way a farmer nods at a field of good barley, to himself, or to the images, or to nobody. Then he blows out his taper, and the hall is lit only by the lamps once more. He bows three times, slowly, before the altar, his hands together at his forehead, his throat, his heart. And he turns to go.

You go out with him into the moonlight.

After the gold warmth of the hall, the courtyard feels very wide and very quiet. The moonlight lies on everything like a thin fall of new snow. It silvers the edges of the fuel stacks along the roofs, and the curve of the prayer wheels under the eaves, and the round mouth of the water jar by the kitchen door. Your shadow and Kunga's walk beside you across the frozen ground, long and soft and blue. Neither of you says anything. There is nothing that needs saying.

On the way back across the courtyard, as he does every night, he stops at each door.

At the kitchen door, which stands a crack open, he looks in. The banked stove breathes its faint red glow under the ash. Lobsang is a long dark shape beside it, rolled in his blankets, snoring softly, slow and deep and comfortable. On the shelf by the ladder, the one small lamp that Lobsang left for Dawa burns no bigger than a barley grain. Kunga leans in, very quietly, and adds a crumb of butter from the end of his spoon to that little lamp too. Then he draws the door almost closed.

He looks up at the ladder hole above the stove, where the boys sleep. There is no sound from up there at all. They are deep in the warm heart of the house, under their sheepskin, side by side, curled on their right sides, as they were taught. Tashi has one arm flung across Dawa's shoulders. Dawa's face, if you could see it, is quiet, and not sad any more. He is dreaming, perhaps, of his mother's dog, or of the great lamp, or of nothing at all.

At Sonam's door, Kunga does not stop. He can hear, through the wood, the long slow breathing of a young man fast asleep.

At the lama's door, he pauses. Through the gap under it, the faint glow of the lama's own small lamp shows on the step. Inside, Lama Ngawang sits in his box, wrapped to the chin in his cloak, the sheepskin round his shoulders, his head bowed a little forward onto his chest. His hands rest in his lap, and the string of beads lies still in them now. His breathing is so soft and slow that you would have to lean very close to hear it. He looks, sitting there in the small light, like one of the gilded images in the hall, small and calm and old and peaceful, with his eyes closed.

And at Jampa's door, the thin line of light under it has grown very faint. Inside, Jampa too is asleep, sitting upright in his box, wrapped in his heavy cloak, warm right through, his big hands folded together, his head bowed. The frost glitters on the inside of his shutter. He does not feel it at all.

Kunga goes back to his own cell, and closes the door behind him, and takes off his boots, and lies down under his sheepskin, with only the one thin wall between his head and the lamps. In a few breaths, he is asleep again, as easily as a child, and the cell is dark and warm around him.

And now you are alone in the courtyard of the White Rock, the only one awake in all that silent house.

You stand very still. The moon has climbed a little higher above the eastern ridge, and its light is brighter now, and the snow peaks shine across the valley like great white sails. The stars around the moon have dimmed a little in its light, but away from it, overhead and in the west, they are as many and as bright as ever. There is no wind at all. The prayer flags on the roof hang still. The prayer wheels along the wall of the hall are still. There is no sound in the whole wide valley. Not a dog. Not a stream. Not a single voice. Only, if you listen very closely, the faint creak of the beams of the old buildings around you, settling in the cold, as an old house does at night, the way a sleeper turns over.

You think of the long day that has passed here. The last gold sun on the white walls. Tashi on the kitchen step, tapping the brick of tea. The dung cakes on the roof, and the dust glittering in the low light. The conch at sunset, three long notes across the valley. The deep voice of the chant, and the warmth it made. The butter tea, salty and rich, and the bowl that was always filled again. Dawa's white letters in the dust, appearing and disappearing. The prayer wheels turning in the dark, and the little bells. The old lama's window, opened a hand's width onto the stars. Jampa in his cell, breathing slowly, finding the fire.

All of it is quiet now.

None of it needs remembering now. It is all folded away, like a book in its cloth, between its two wooden boards, wrapped up and tied with its strap, and set on a high shelf, safe, for another day. Tomorrow there will be more tea, and more lessons, and more butter for the lamps, and the sun on the white walls again. But tomorrow is a long way off. Tonight is soft and silver and still, and it is nearly over, and it has been a good night.

Everyone is asleep. The doors are closed. The stove is banked. The water is in its jar, and the fuel is on the roof, and the tea is ready for the morning. Nothing more needs to be done tonight. Nothing at all.

Somewhere above the kitchen, very faintly, one of the boys murmurs in his sleep, and turns over, and is quiet. Somewhere in the hall behind you, a lamp flame gives one small soft flutter, and is still. You draw your coat round you and stand a moment longer in the moonlight, and breathe the cold, clean, thin air, slowly, in and out.

Then, slowly and quietly, you sit down on the stone step of the shrine hall, with your back against the old wooden door, and pull your knees up under your coat, and wrap your arms round them. The stone is cold, but the door behind you is faintly warm, from the lamps inside, and you can feel that small warmth between your shoulders, gentle and steady, like a hand resting there.

Let your shoulders drop now, the way Jampa's did, sitting in the cold. Let your hands grow loose and heavy in your lap. Let your breathing slow, until it is as long and as quiet as the old lama's, in and out, in and out, with a soft pause at the end of each breath, like the pause between two notes of the chant. There is nothing to watch for. There is nothing to wait for. The lamps are fed. The fire is banked. Everyone you have met tonight is asleep and safe and warm, under sheepskin and wool, in their small rooms around you, with the thick earth walls between them and the cold.

Above you, the moon goes slowly on its way. Across the valley, the snow peaks shine. And here, on the step, with your eyes half closed, you can feel yourself growing warm too, slowly, from the inside, the way Jampa remembered it coming: not like a fire, but like the sun on a wall.

And behind you, through the crack of the shrine hall door, a thread of warm gold light lies across the silver ground of the courtyard, from the long, low line of lamps, newly fed, burning on.

## Part Twelve — Around Five in the Morning: Before the First Light

The hours go by softly now. Two o'clock. Three. Four. Nobody counts them. The moon climbs higher over the valley, small and pale, and the stars turn slowly round it, and the White Rock sleeps on, white and still on its ledge of rock, under the enormous sky.

What does a monastery on a mountain sound like, at five o'clock on a winter morning?

Almost nothing at all. Only the cold, and the stillness, and the lamps.

You are sitting in the shrine hall now, at the very end of one of the long rows, on a thick cushion, with a heavy wool cloak round your shoulders, and you are listening. There is no wind. There are no voices. There is no clatter from the kitchen yet, no creak of the ladder, no slosh of the churn. The prayer flags on the roof hang still. The prayer wheels under the eaves are silent. The whole valley below lies hushed in the cold, every field and wall and stone of it, and the village houses are dark, and the stream is frozen still, and even the dogs are asleep. If you listen very hard, you can hear the faint hiss of the lamps, a sound so small it is almost not a sound. And under it, perhaps, your own heart, slow and steady.

That is all. And it is enough.

If you could drift up out of the hall, and through the roof, and out over the courtyard, you would find the whole monastery silver and still beneath you. The moon is high now, small and white. Its light lies on the flat roofs and the brown stacks of fuel along their edges, on the white walls and the dark red band of twigs along the top of the hall, on the long mound of carved stones on the slope above, where Jampa walked in the afternoon. Below, the valley is a great bowl of gray and silver shadow, and in the village, far down, every house is dark, every fire banked, every family asleep under its sheepskins. And beyond the valley, the snow peaks stand in a long white line under the stars, silent, enormous and gentle, as they have stood over this valley for longer than anyone can imagine.

And then you drift back down, through the roof, into the warm gold dark of the hall, and settle onto your cushion again, and pull the heavy cloak closer round your shoulders.

In front of you, at the far end of the hall, the long line of lamps burns on its shelf. Twenty-two small gold flames, standing perfectly still in the windless dark. Kunga fed them at one o'clock, and they have burned down a little since, but only a little, and they are bright and patient and calm. Above them, the gilded faces of the images float in their soft light, with their half-closed eyes, as if they too were listening to the silence. And on the right, the pale figure of the cotton-clad saint sits with his hand cupped to his ear.

You watch the great lamp in the middle of the row, for a long, unhurried while. Its flame is as long as a thumb, and it stands perfectly upright, with a small clear blue at its root and a soft gold body and a fine bright tip, and now and then it gives the smallest shiver, as if it were breathing, and then is still again. The pool of butter around its wick is clear and golden, like warm honey, and the light of it trembles very faintly on the brass of the bowl. If you look at it long enough, the rest of the hall seems to fall away, the pillars and the banners and the dark walls, until there is nothing but the small steady flame, and the warm sweet smell, and the quiet. It is not a large light. It does not need to be. It has only to keep burning, gently, through the dark, and it does.

Our last source of the night is the oldest story we have heard. It is the one Jampa first heard as a boy, in the kitchen of the White Rock, and the one that every student of inner heat is told: Milarepa's winter in the snow, as it was written down at Lapchi in 1488. In the story, when his followers climbed up to the cave in the spring, expecting the worst, they found him well and calm, and he sang for them. In his song, as the story tells it, he sang of the great snow that had fallen around him, and of how it had not troubled him, because he had found within himself a warmth that the snow could not reach. It is a good story to hold in your mind, in a dark hall, at five o'clock in the morning, beside a row of small warm lamps.

People who think about nights like this one sometimes wonder whether we picture the old monasteries of Tibet too gently, and forget the cold and the hunger and the hard work of them. Others say that the monks who lived there remembered the gentleness too, the tea and the lamps and the songs, and that we are right to keep it. Perhaps both are true, here, in the dark. The hard work of the day is done. The gentle part is all that is left.

You think about the three questions you have carried through the night. How to stay warm, so high, with almost no wood: with the sun and the thick earth walls, with a summer's harvest of dung on the roof, with wool and butter and tea, and with each other, shoulder to shoulder in the hall. Why the youngest rise earliest: because the day begins with a fire, and a pot of tea, and a conch, and those are the first things a monk ever learns to give. And the inner heat: a practice of breath and body and mind, handed down for centuries, and quietly at work tonight in a small bare cell across the courtyard. You do not need to think about any of them any more. They have all been answered. They can rest now, like everything else in this house.

You let your eyes close for a moment. The warmth of the lamps stays with you, faint and gold, on the inside of your eyelids, and the quiet stays too, and you do not need to open them again until you want to.

Across the courtyard, in his small room, Lama Ngawang sleeps in his box, wrapped in his cloak, his head bowed, his old dark bowl on the shelf beside him, and his beads at rest in his lap. His breathing is long and slow and very quiet. He is not thinking about the boys, or the butter, or the winter. He is not thinking about anything.

In the next room, Sonam sleeps on his right side, his knees drawn up, under his heavy sheepskin. The verses he was learning last night have drifted away from him, into dreams. They will come back in the morning.

In his plain cell, Jampa sleeps sitting upright, warm under his cloak, his big hands folded, his face peaceful. His little lamp has burned down to a soft gold glow.

Behind the shrine hall, Kunga sleeps with only one thin wall between his head and the lamps, as he has for twenty years. If the great lamp were burning low, he would know. It is not burning low.

In the kitchen, Lobsang sleeps rolled in his blankets beside the banked stove, snoring gently, with the bellows and the basket of fuel and the copper pot all set out beside him, ready.

And above the kitchen, in the warmest room in the house, Tashi and Dawa sleep side by side under the sheepskin, on their right sides, the way they were taught. Tashi's arm is still across Dawa's shoulders. Dawa is smiling a little in his sleep.

Seven people under one roof, asleep at the top of the world, in the long, quiet hour before the dawn.

And you, on your cushion at the end of the row, can rest too. There is nothing left to do tonight. Nothing to carry, nothing to light, nothing to watch over. The lamps have been fed. The stove is banked. The water is in its jar, and the fuel is on the roof. The boys are asleep, and their bellows are ready for them.

So let your breathing slow, the way theirs has. In, and out. Let the cloak be heavy and warm on your shoulders. Let your hands lie loose in your lap. Feel the faint, gentle warmth of the lamps on your face, and the soft dark of the hall all around you, and the deep quiet of the sleeping mountain beyond the walls. You are safe here. You are warm enough. You can stay as long as you like.

Soon, very soon, something will change. It will be very slight at first. Over the eastern ridge, the sky, which has been black and full of stars all night, will begin to thin. It will not be light yet. Only a little less dark. A deep, cold blue, and then a gray, very faint, along the line of the mountains, as if someone had breathed on a dark window. And one by one, the stars in the east will fade.

In a little while after that, Tashi will wake. He will lie still a moment under the sheepskin, in the warm dark, and remember where he is, and what day it is, and what he has to do. Then he will sit up, and shake Dawa gently by the shoulder, and the two of them will find their boots in the dark and climb down the ladder, toward the one small lamp that Lobsang left them, no bigger than a barley grain, still burning on its shelf. Dawa will kneel at the stove mouth, and rake back the ash, and find the red coals waiting underneath, and lay on two cakes of dung, and lift the goatskin bellows. Open. Close. Push. And the coals will glow orange, and the fire will wake. And Tashi will climb up onto the roof in the cold gray light, with the white conch shell in his hands, and face the valley, and take a long breath of the thin cold air.

And then, one by one, the house will wake around them. Lobsang will roll out of his blankets, yawning, and find the tea already boiling, and pretend to be surprised. Kunga will come into the hall with the water jug, and fill the seven small bowls in their straight line before the lamps, so close that a grain of barley could barely pass between them. Sonam will wake with the first verse of the day already on his lips. Jampa will open his eyes in his box, rested and warm. The old lama will lift his head, and look toward his shuttered window, and his thumb will begin again, slowly, on his beads. A thin blue thread of juniper smoke will rise from the clay hearth on the roof, sweet and sharp, straight up into the still air. And the conch will sound, three long notes, rolling out over the valley and coming back, fainter, off the far mountains, and the snow peaks will turn, very slowly, from gray to pink to gold.

But not yet. Not yet.

For now, everyone is still asleep.

Lama Ngawang is asleep in his box, with his beads in his lap. Jampa is asleep in his cell, warm right through, sitting upright in the cold. Kunga is asleep behind the shrine hall, with his head beside the lamps. Lobsang is asleep by the stove, with everything ready for the morning. Sonam is asleep on his right side, with his verses waiting for him. Tashi is asleep with his arm across his friend's shoulders, and Dawa is asleep beside him, not homesick at all tonight, in the warm heart of the White Rock.

The house is quiet. The house is warm enough. The night has passed safely, every hour of it.

And in the shrine hall, in the last of the dark, the butter lamps are still burning, twenty-two small gold flames in a long, low, steady line, and in the middle of them all, the great lamp, tall and calm and bright, just as it was when we found it yesterday afternoon. It has burned through the whole long winter night. It burned before the old lama came here as a boy. It will burn on through the morning, and the day, and the next long night, and the next. And it is waiting, quietly, in the stillness, for the conch, and the chanting, and the first light on the snow.

If you enjoyed spending this winter night at the White Rock, a like or a subscription would mean a great deal, but only if you truly enjoyed it. And if there is a time or a place you would love to spend a night in, leave it in the comments, because your ideas truly help.

Next time, we will visit a very different kind of house, on a very different kind of night, somewhere far from these mountains, and much lower down.

Sleep well, friend. Good night.
