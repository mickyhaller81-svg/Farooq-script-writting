## Part One — Around Half Past Four in the Morning: The Roof Before Dawn

On the flat mud roof of a courtyard house, a family lies asleep on thin mattresses, under light cotton sheets, and the air on your face is soft and cool, the only cool air this day will offer. Far below them, at the bottom of a long dark stair, water from under the desert is sliding quietly into a stone pool.

Not the Persia of the picture books, with its flying carpets and its genies in bottles. Just one ordinary family on one ordinary summer day.

Without a few old tricks, you would not have lasted until nightfall.

So tonight I would like you to carry three small questions with you. The first is this: how do you make ice in the desert? The second: how does a tower cool a house with no machine? And the third: why does the whole family sleep on the roof?

These are not three separate questions, really. They are three ways of asking how one family kept cool through one long desert day, and we will answer them one at a time as the hours go by.

Now let your head grow heavy on the pillow. Let your shoulders loosen and drop, and let your eyes close whenever they like, because there is nothing you need to watch. We will go back to that gray sky and that quiet roof together, and we will stay with this family from first light until the stars come out again. So let us begin.

The house stands in the old part of Yazd, in a maze of narrow lanes walled on both sides with tan mud brick. From the street, there is almost nothing to see. A plain wall. A wooden door set deep in an arch. And above the roofline, here and there across the whole city, tall square towers rise into the sky, each one open near the top, like a chimney turned upside down. In the half-light before dawn they stand all around, dozens of them, dark against the paling stars.

Yazd sits on a high, dry plain, almost four thousand feet above the sea, between two great deserts. To the north lies the salt desert the Persians call the Dasht-e Kavir. To the southeast, beyond the mountains, lies the Dasht-e Lut, one of the hottest and emptiest places on earth. Mountains stand to the south and west, and the tallest of them, Shir Kuh, holds patches of snow on its upper slopes long into the spring. Everything in this city, every garden, every pool, every cup of water, depends in the end on those mountains, and on a hidden river of human making that we will follow later this morning.

This family never quite existed under one name, but every detail of their day is real.

There are seven of them under this roof, and one cat. Ostad Hossein is the grandfather, sixty-nine years old, lean and brown, with a white beard trimmed short. The word Ostad means master, and he earned it. For forty years he was a qanat digger, one of the men who tunnel under the desert to bring water to the city. His knees no longer let him climb down the shafts, but every digger in this quarter still comes to ask his advice. His wife, Bibi Khadijeh, is sixty-four. She runs the house, she keeps the keys, and above all she keeps the water, which in Yazd is the same thing as keeping the family alive.

Their son, Ali Akbar, is forty-two. He is a silk weaver, with two looms in a long room off the courtyard, and his hands are quick and careful. His wife, Fatemeh, is thirty-six. She cooks, she makes the syrups and the yogurt, and she embroiders silk in the hour after noon when the house is still. Their eldest, Mahmud, is sixteen, and he is learning the loom from his father, though his grandfather privately hopes he will one day learn the tunnels instead. Maryam is eleven. She helps her mother, she draws water, and she is very proud of the fact that she can carry a full jar without spilling it on the stairs. And Reza is six. His main work, as far as he is concerned, is asking questions, and the cat, a long-haired gray creature called Pishi, is his closest friend.

Here is a small question to begin with, and we will answer it before this part is over. Why is this family awake and stirring before the sun has even risen?

Let us start with what everyone in this city knew. A Yazd summer is long, dry and fierce. By the middle of July, the afternoon heat here climbs to a little over one hundred degrees Fahrenheit, around thirty-nine Celsius, almost every day, and on the worst days it pushes higher still. Rain is something people remember rather than expect. In an ordinary year, the whole city receives only a few inches of it, and nearly all of that falls in winter. From late spring until autumn, the sky is a hard, clean blue, without a single cloud for weeks at a time.

But the desert has a gift hidden inside its cruelty. Because the air is so dry, it cannot hold the heat after dark. Once the sun goes down, the warmth of the day escapes straight up into the clear sky, and by the hour before dawn, the air on the rooftops has fallen into the seventies. That is still warm, by the standards of a northern night. But after a day of over a hundred degrees, it feels like a blessing. And it will not last.

So the answer to our small question is this. The first hour of the morning is the only truly comfortable hour of the whole day. Everything that needs strength or care, the carrying, the baking, the sweeping, the shopping, the watering of the plants, has to be done now, before the sun climbs. By nine o'clock the heat will be pressing down. By eleven, nobody with any choice will be out in it. The family wakes before sunrise not because they are told to, but because the desert sets their clock.

And today the clock is very precise. In Yazd, on this morning in July, the first gray light came at a little after half past four. The sun itself will rise at about four minutes past five, and it will not set again until a few minutes past seven in the evening. That is fourteen hours of sun. Our family will spend most of those hours learning, as they have every summer of their lives, how to hide from it.

Here is a surprising detail, and it comes from the most famous traveler of the Middle Ages. Nearly four hundred years before our morning, a young Venetian merchant passed through this very city on his way east. His name was Marco Polo. In the book of his travels, written down in the late twelve nineties, he described Yazd in a single warm line. He called it "a good and noble city," and he said it had a great trade, and that its weavers made a fine silk cloth, which was known after the city's name.

So the silk on Ali Akbar's looms, four centuries later, belongs to a very old story. Yazd was a city of weavers long before Marco Polo, and it still is in our year of 1670. Its silks traveled west by caravan to Isfahan, and from there to the ports of the Persian Gulf, and from there to India and Europe. In a city with very little water and almost no rain, it made perfect sense to live by something light and precious, something you could carry on a camel's back across the desert, something worth far more than its weight.

Historians do not all agree about exactly how Marco Polo traveled through Persia, or in which order he visited its cities. Some think his book's route through Yazd and Kerman is reliable, and that he described what he saw with his own eyes. Others point out that the book was written down years later, in a prison in Genoa, with the help of a writer of romances, and that some of its details may have come from other merchants rather than from Polo himself. The fairest view is probably that the line about Yazd, a good and noble city of silk, rings true either way, because every later traveler said much the same.

Now, come up onto the roof for a moment, just you, in the last minutes before the sun.

You are lying on a thin cotton mattress, rolled out across a floor of hard, smooth mud plaster. The roof is flat here, though just beyond you it swells up into a low, rounded dome, the top of the room beneath. A low wall runs around the edge, about as high as your waist, so that nobody in the lane below can see who is sleeping. The plaster under your hand is cool. It has been giving its heat up to the sky all night, and now, for a little while, it is the coolest thing in the city.

You sit up slowly. The sheet slides off your shoulders. The air is still and dry and faintly scented with dust and with something green, perhaps from the fig tree in a neighbor's courtyard. Above you, the stars are fading one by one, the faint ones first, then the brighter ones, until only a few are left, burning white in a sky that is turning, very slowly, from gray to the palest blue.

All around you, across the city, the wind towers stand like a forest of tall square trunks. Beyond them, to the south, the mountains are a long dark line against the sky, and just above the line, the light is beginning to turn gold.

You hear the city waking. A rooster, somewhere in the next lane. A donkey, complaining. The soft slap of sandals on a stair. A woman's voice, low, calling a child's name. And then, rising over all of it from the direction of the great mosque, the long call to the dawn prayer, drawn out and gentle, floating over the rooftops in the cool air.

Below you, at the foot of the house, you can hear another sound, so faint that you have to hold your breath to notice it. It is the sound of moving water. Somewhere down there, deep beneath the floors, water is running.

Today, in Yazd, many families still sleep on the roofs on the hottest summer nights, though now there is usually an electric fan or an air conditioner humming in the rooms below. And the old city, with its mud lanes and its wind towers, was named a World Heritage Site in 2017.

On the roof, Bibi Khadijeh is already up. She is always the first, every morning of the year. She is folding her own mattress into a neat roll, and her husband's, because Ostad Hossein is still sitting on the edge of his, rubbing his knees and murmuring the words of the morning prayer under his breath.

Fatemeh is waking the children. Mahmud groans and pulls the sheet over his head, and she pulls it right back off again, without a word. Maryam is up at once, already rolling her mattress. Reza is fast asleep, flat on his back, with his arms flung wide and his mouth open, and the cat, Pishi, is curled in the hollow of his elbow.

"Up, little one," says Fatemeh softly, and she lifts him, mattress and cat and all. The cat jumps down in disgust and stalks off along the parapet with her tail in the air.

Ali Akbar stands at the edge of the roof and looks east. The light over the mountains is gold now, with a line of rose above it. He can already feel what kind of day it will be. There is no breeze at all. The air is perfectly still.

"Hot," he says.

"It is July," says his mother. "It is always hot."

The bedding is carried down in rolls, one by one, by everyone except Reza, who is carried down himself. The stair from the roof is narrow and steep, built into the thickness of the wall, and it comes out onto a little landing above the courtyard. From there you can look down into the whole house, a rectangle of rooms around an open court, with a long shallow pool in the middle and a single pomegranate tree at one end, its leaves glossy in the gray light, its small green fruit still hard.

Bibi Khadijeh does not go to the kitchen first. She never does. She goes to a low doorway in the corner of the courtyard, where a stair leads downward into the dark. She takes the oil lamp from its niche beside the door, lights it from the ember Fatemeh has already coaxed alive in the kitchen, and begins, slowly, to go down.

Forty steps. She has counted them for forty-five years. At the bottom the air is cool and damp and still, and the lamplight shines on water. A stone pool lies there, eight-sided, wide enough to sit beside, and water flows into it from a dark opening in the wall, crosses it with a soft sound, and slips out again on the far side. She dips her fingers into it. It is cold, almost shockingly cold, as it has been every morning of her life.

The pool in the cool room under the house is full, and the water is moving, and the day can begin.

## Part Two — Around Five in the Morning: Sunrise and the Turn of the Water

At about four minutes past five, the sun lifts itself clear of the eastern hills, and in a single moment every wind tower in Yazd catches the light along one edge and turns the color of honey.

In the courtyard, the shadows are long and blue, and the air is still cool enough to be pleasant. The family moves quickly now, the way people move when they know a door is about to close. And this morning there is something special to wait for, because today is the day of their water.

Here is a small question for this part. In a city where it almost never rains, how does a family know when the water is theirs?

To answer that, we have to understand that in Yazd, water was not simply something that fell from the sky or came out of a well. It was property. It was bought and sold, inherited and divided, written into marriage contracts and into wills, exactly like land or houses or shops. A house in this city was worth far less without its share of water, and a garden without water was not a garden at all. It was simply a patch of desert with a wall around it.

The water itself came from the qanats, the long underground channels that carried it from the foot of the mountains, many miles away, into the city. Each qanat was owned by a group of shareholders, sometimes a few families, sometimes hundreds. When the water came out into the open, or ran through its channels beneath the lanes, it was divided among them, not by the bucket or the jar, but by time. One family might own a share of so many minutes of the whole flow, every so many days. Another might own twice as much. A rich garden might own a great deal.

So the water moves around the quarter in a slow circle. For a set span of time, the whole stream is turned into one family's channel, to fill their pool and soak their garden beds. Then a small dam of earth and stone is shifted, and the stream turns toward the next house, and the next, until every share has had its turn. Then the circle begins again.

Drinking water was a different matter. Any household along the channel could lower a jar or a bucket to fill it for drinking and cooking, at any hour, because in this quarter, as in most of Yazd, water for the mouth was not something you refused a neighbor. But to take the whole stream, to let it run into your own pool and your own garden, you needed your turn.

So the answer to our small question is this. The family knows when the water is theirs because someone is measuring it, minute by minute, and that someone is about to knock on their door.

Here is our surprising detail, and it is one of the loveliest inventions of the desert. Those minutes were measured with a clock made of water. In many Persian towns and villages, the man in charge of the water, who was called the mirab, kept a large basin filled with water, and floating in it, a small bowl, usually of copper, with a tiny hole pierced in its bottom. Slowly, very slowly, water seeped in through the hole, and the bowl sank lower and lower, until at last it filled and slipped beneath the surface without a sound.

That was one unit of time. The mirab lifted the bowl out, emptied it, set it floating again, and dropped a pebble into a jar to keep count. One bowl, then another, then another. When the right number of pebbles lay in the jar, a family's turn was over. The bowl itself became a measure of wealth. A household's water share was written down as so many bowls, and people spoke of buying or selling a bowl of water the way people elsewhere might speak of buying an acre.

Not every family in Yazd owned a share, of course. For those who did not, and for travelers and the poor, there were the great public cisterns, called ab anbars, which means storehouses of water. An ab anbar was a deep tank, sunk far below the street and roofed with a tall dome of brick, with a long flight of steps leading down to a tap at the bottom. In winter, when water was plentiful and cold, the cistern was filled from the qanats. Then it was sealed, and for the rest of the year anyone could walk down the steps with a jar and fill it. The water at the bottom stayed cool through the whole summer, because the earth around it was cool, and because the dome above kept the sun away.

Building an ab anbar was one of the most admired acts of charity in the city. Wealthy merchants and pious widows paid for them, and had their names written over the entrance, so that every thirsty stranger who went down the steps would know whom to thank. There was one at the end of our family's lane, and on the hottest afternoons, a line of people with jars would stand in the shade of its doorway, waiting their turn to go down.

At night, the turns went on, because water flows in the dark as well as in the light. Some shares were day shares and some were night shares, and night water was often worth a little less, because it was harder to use. The mirab kept watch through the dark hours by his basin and his lamp, and in some places, by the slow wheeling of the stars.

Our source for this part is very old indeed. Around the middle of the second century before Christ, a Greek historian named Polybius was writing a great history of the Mediterranean world. In the tenth book of that history, describing a march across the dry country of northern Persia, he paused to explain why there was any water there at all. In the old days, he wrote, when the Persian kings ruled Asia, they had made a promise. Anyone who brought water to a place that had never been watered before would be given the right to farm that land, and his family after him, for five generations.

That promise, nearly two thousand years before our morning, says a great deal. It says that in Persia, bringing water to dry land was the greatest gift a person could give, and that kings knew it. And it says that the people who did it, the diggers, the builders, the men like Ostad Hossein, were worth rewarding for a very long time.

Historians do not all agree about what Polybius was really describing. Some are sure that he was writing about qanats, and that his account is among the earliest mentions of them anywhere. Others point out that he speaks of hidden wells and springs, and of channels running underground, in a way that suggests he, or the soldier who told him, did not fully understand how the system worked. The fairest view is probably that he knew something remarkable was happening under the desert, even if he could not quite see it.

Now, come out into the lane, just you, as the water arrives.

You step through the deep doorway into the street. The lane is narrow, so narrow that you could almost touch both walls at once with your arms stretched out, and the walls rise high on either side, smooth and tan and windowless. The sun has not yet reached down this far. The air between the walls is still cool, and smells of damp earth and of bread baking somewhere close by.

Along one side of the lane, set into the ground, runs a narrow channel lined with fired brick, and in it you can hear water moving. It is not a loud sound. It is a soft, steady chuckle, like someone laughing quietly to themselves in another room.

A boy comes along the lane, a little older than Reza, barefoot, with a short wooden spade over his shoulder. He is the mirab's runner. He stops at a place where the channel divides, kneels, and scoops away a little wall of packed earth and stone with his hands. Then he uses the spade to build a new one just across the other branch.

And the water turns. You watch it happen. The stream hesitates, swirls, and then pours into the branch that leads under your own doorway, gathering speed, glinting as it catches the first sunlight from a gap in the roofs.

You follow it back inside. In the courtyard, the long shallow pool is filling. The water slides in from a spout of carved stone at one end, clear and cold, and spreads across the bottom, stirring up a faint cloud of fine silt that slowly settles again. You kneel at the edge and put your hand in. It is cold enough to make your fingers ache. It has traveled for miles underground, in the dark, and the sun has not touched it once.

Today, most of Yazd's water comes through pipes and pumps, and the old clocks are kept in museums. But in a few places in Iran, a mirab still sits beside his basin, watching a bowl sink, dropping his pebbles into a jar, as his grandfather did.

In the courtyard, everyone has a job. Ali Akbar has rolled up his trousers to the knee and is standing in the garden bed at the far end of the courtyard, guiding the overflow from the pool with a hoe, so that it soaks the roots of the pomegranate tree and the little row of herbs, mint and basil and tarragon, that Fatemeh grows along the wall. Mahmud is carrying jars. Maryam is carrying jars too, though smaller ones, and she is concentrating so hard on not spilling them that her tongue is sticking out of the corner of her mouth.

Bibi Khadijeh stands beside the pool and watches the water, and counts. She does not need to see the mirab's bowl. She has known the length of this family's turn since the day she married into the house, and she can feel it in her body, the way you might know without looking how long a song lasts.

Reza is squatting at the edge of the pool, poking the water with a stick. Pishi the cat sits beside him, watching the ripples with great seriousness, her tail curled neatly over her paws.

"Grandmother, where does the water come from?" Reza asks.

"From the mountains," says his grandmother.

"But the mountains are far away. I can only just see them from the roof."

"That is true."

"So it cannot walk here by itself."

Bibi Khadijeh looks across the courtyard at her husband, who is sitting on the low bench in the shade of the summer room, with his bad knee stretched out in front of him.

"Ask your grandfather," she says. "He carried some of it here himself."

Ostad Hossein smiles. He has been waiting for this question for about three years.

"After breakfast," he says. "Bring me your bread, and I will tell you."

Mahmud has gone out to the baker at the corner, and now he comes back through the door with a stack of flatbread wrapped in a cloth, still hot, so hot that he is juggling it from hand to hand. The baker's oven is a deep clay pit, sunk in the ground, and the dough is slapped against its burning sides and peeled off again a minute later, blistered and golden. Every family in the lane buys bread there in the morning, so that nobody has to light a large fire of their own in July.

Breakfast is laid on a cloth spread on the floor of the summer room, on the shaded side of the courtyard. Bread, still warm. A dish of white sheep's cheese. A bundle of fresh herbs from the garden, rinsed in the cold pool water. A few dates. A bowl of thin yogurt mixed with water and a pinch of salt, cool and sour, which in Persia is called doogh. And a jar of plain water, drawn from far below the house, so cold that the outside of the jar beads with moisture.

They eat slowly, sitting cross-legged, tearing the bread and wrapping it around a piece of cheese and a few leaves of mint. Nobody hurries breakfast, even on a morning like this, because the cool hour is for work, but the meal is for the family. Ostad Hossein says a few words of thanks before he eats. Reza says them after him, a little too loudly, with his mouth already full, and his mother gives him a look that he pretends not to see.

Bibi Khadijeh pours the water herself. She fills each cup only halfway, and refills it when it is empty, because in this house no water is ever poured that will not be drunk. Even the dregs at the bottom of each cup go back into the jar for the plants. It is not that the family is poor. Ali Akbar's silks sell well, and this is a comfortable house. It is simply that she grew up in this city, and in this city, wasting water is simply not done, the way a family in a cold country would not leave a door standing open in winter.

The water's turn ends. The runner's spade knocks against the channel outside, the dam is shifted, and the stream in the courtyard slows, trickles, and stops. The pool lies full and still.

But far beneath the courtyard, in the cool room at the bottom of the stair, the other pool, the one that never belongs to anyone's turn, goes on filling and emptying as it always does, the water sliding through it in the dark.

## Part Three — Around Seven in the Morning: The Old Man Who Dug Under the Desert

By seven o'clock the sun has climbed over the eastern wall of the courtyard, and a hard bright line of light has begun to creep across the brick floor, inch by inch, toward the pool.

The summer room is still in shade. It faces north, away from the sun, and it is open along its whole front to the courtyard, like a deep stage with the curtain raised. Here, on a cotton rug, Ostad Hossein sits with his back to the cool wall, and Reza sits in front of him with the last of his bread, waiting for the story he was promised.

Here is a small question for this part. How can water travel for many miles under the desert, without a single pump or wheel or animal to push it?

The answer begins, as most things in Yazd begin, with the mountains. When snow falls on the high slopes in winter and melts in spring, some of it runs off in streams that soon vanish into the sand. But much of it soaks down into the gravel at the foot of the mountains, and there, deep underground, it gathers into a hidden layer of water, moving very slowly downhill through the stones. That hidden water is the treasure. The question every qanat digger has to answer is how to reach it and bring it out into the light, far away, where people actually live.

The first step was to find it. The diggers walked the slopes at the foot of the mountains, looking for signs, a certain kind of plant, a certain color of soil, a dampness in a dry streambed. Then they dug a test well, straight down, sometimes for hundreds of feet, until they struck water. This first deep shaft was called the mother well.

Then came the clever part. Starting not at the mountain, but far away at the place where they wanted the water to come out, the diggers began to tunnel back toward the mother well, uphill, at the gentlest possible slope. The tunnel was barely tall enough for a man to stoop through, and only as wide as his shoulders. Every so often, along its line, they dug another shaft down from the surface, to haul up the earth and to let air down to the men below. From a hilltop, a qanat looks like a long line of little craters, each one ringed by a mound of spoil, marching away across the plain toward the hills.

When the tunnel finally reached the bottom of the mother well, the water began to flow. And this is the answer to our small question. The tunnel falls just a little less steeply than the land above it. So the water, moving gently downhill through the dark, the whole way by its own weight, comes closer and closer to the surface, until at last it runs out into the open, at the edge of a town or a garden, miles from where it began. Nothing pushes it. It simply falls, very slowly, all the way.

Inside the city, the water's path decided almost everything. A qanat comes out of the ground at one particular place, and from there its water runs downhill through the town in open channels and covered ones. The houses and gardens nearest the outlet get the water first, cleanest and coldest. Those farther down the slope get it later, after it has passed through other people's channels and pools. So in Yazd, as in many Persian towns, the finest houses tended to cluster near the head of the water, and a family's place along the stream was a kind of rank.

Some houses lay right above the line of an underground channel, as it passed beneath the lanes toward the gardens beyond. And the families in those houses could do something wonderful. They could dig down, straight through their own floor, to the level of the passing water, and build a stair to reach it. A stair like that was called a payab, which means the foot of the water. At the bottom, the family could fill their jars from the moving stream, cold and fresh, without ever stepping into the street. Often they widened the space at the bottom into a small vaulted room. Our family's house has one. Its forty steps go down through the earth to the very channel that Ostad Hossein once helped to clean.

The diggers themselves were a world apart. The craft passed from father to son, and the men of Yazd had a reputation across Persia as some of the best in the trade. They worked in teams, one man below with his short-handled pick and his lamp, others above at the windlass, hauling up leather buckets of earth and stone. They worked mostly in the cooler months, and they knew things that no book could teach, how to read the color of the clay, how to tell from the sound of a pick whether the rock ahead was sound, how to keep a tunnel straight in total darkness by sighting along the flames of two lamps, one behind the other.

Here is a surprising detail. The qanat that feeds much of the plain around Yazd, the great qanat of Zarch, is around forty-four miles long. Along its course, more than two thousand shafts have been dug down to it from the surface. Nobody knows exactly how old it is. Some say a thousand years, some say much more. But it was flowing long before our family's grandparents were born, and in our summer of 1670 it is flowing still. In 2016, it was named, along with ten other ancient qanats across the country, as part of a single World Heritage Site called the Persian Qanat.

Our source for this part was written down more than six hundred years before our morning, in Persia itself. In the early eleventh century, a mathematician named al-Karaji, who had worked for years in Baghdad and then returned to the mountain country of western Persia, wrote a book with a beautiful title. In English it is usually called The Extraction of Hidden Waters. It is a practical book, a manual for engineers. It explains how to look for signs of water underground, how to level the line of a tunnel so the water will flow, how to measure the slope, and how to look after a qanat once it is built. Al-Karaji wrote that a qanat must be cleaned and repaired, year after year, or it will slowly fill with silt and die. That is the work that men like Ostad Hossein spent their lives doing.

Historians do not all agree about where the qanat was first invented. Many believe it began on the Iranian plateau, early in the first thousand years before Christ, perhaps among miners who found water while tunneling for metal, and that the Persian kings later spread it across their empire. Others point to very early underground channels in the dry lands of southeastern Arabia, in what is now Oman and the Emirates, and argue that the idea may have arisen there too, or even first. The fairest view is probably that desert people in more than one place learned the same lesson, that water hidden underground can be led to the surface, if you are patient enough.

Now, let your grandfather take you down, just you, in your imagination, the way he went down when he was young.

You are standing on the open plain outside the city, at the edge of a round hole in the earth, ringed by a mound of pale sand. A wooden windlass stands over it, with a rope wound around its drum. The sun is hot on the back of your neck. You sit on a loop at the end of the rope, and two men lower you slowly, hand over hand, into the shaft.

The light shrinks above you to a small blue circle. The walls are close and cool, damp clay, scored by the marks of the diggers' picks. Your feet touch bottom. You crouch, and turn, and there in front of you is the tunnel, low and round and dark, and running along its floor, a stream of water, ankle-deep, clear and cold and silent, moving away from you into the blackness.

Someone hands you a small clay oil lamp. You hold it up. Its flame is steady and yellow, and that is good news, because it means the air down here is good. The old diggers watched their lamps the way sailors watch the sky.

You begin to walk, bent over, the water sliding past your shins. The walls glisten. The only sounds are the drip of water and the soft splash of your own steps, and far ahead, a faint glow where the next shaft lets down a column of daylight. The air is cool. It is the coolest air you have felt in many months. Above you, perhaps a hundred feet up, the desert is beginning to bake. Down here, it might be early spring.

Today, the old qanat diggers of Yazd are fewer than they were, and many qanats have dried, because modern pumped wells have drawn the hidden water down below their reach. But the city has a water museum, in an old merchant's house, where you can see the diggers' tools and their lamps and the copper bowls of the water clocks.

In the summer room, Reza has stopped chewing. He is staring at his grandfather with his mouth slightly open.

"Were you scared?" he asks.

Ostad Hossein laughs. "The first time, very. I was fourteen. My father went down first, and I went down after him, and when I got to the bottom I could not see him at all, only his lamp, far away in the tunnel, like a little star. He called back to me to come, and I came."

"Tell me what happened next."

"And then I dug for forty years," says the old man. "Cleaning, mostly. Not making new ones. A qanat is like a garden. If you do not tend it every year, the walls fall in a little here, the silt builds up a little there, and one spring the water does not come. So every winter, when the ground was cool, we went down and cleaned. My father, and his father before him, and then me."

Mahmud has come to the doorway of the weaving room. He has been sent to start setting up the loom, and he has been standing there for some time, holding a bobbin of silk, listening. His father glances at him from inside, and says nothing, and goes back to his threads.

"Grandfather," says Reza, "is the water in our pool from your tunnel?"

"Some of it, I would think," says Ostad Hossein. "I cleaned that channel more times than I can count. Every time you put your hand in that water down there, you can say that your grandfather once walked beside it in the dark."

Reza thinks about this very seriously. Then he gets up, puts the end of his bread into his mouth, and runs to the corner of the courtyard, to the low doorway with the stair going down.

"Not now," calls his grandmother from the kitchen. "Later. When it is hot. Then we will all go down."

Bibi Khadijeh is already beginning to close the house. That is the work of the next hour, and it is serious work. She has drawn the cotton curtains across the open front of the winter rooms on the south side of the courtyard, the side that will catch the full sun. Fatemeh is in the kitchen, a small dark room in one corner, setting a pot of milk to warm over a little charcoal fire, to make tomorrow's yogurt. She works quickly, because a kitchen fire in July is a misery, and she wants it out before the heat really begins.

From the weaving room comes the first knock of the loom, then another, then a steady rhythm, the clack of the beater against the cloth and the soft swish of the shuttle passing through. Ali Akbar likes to weave in the early morning, while his hands are cool and dry, because sweat spoils silk. On the loom today is a length of plain crimson taffeta, as smooth as water, ordered by a merchant who will carry it to Isfahan before the end of the month. Mahmud sits beside him and winds the bobbins, and tries very hard to look as though he had never been listening to stories at all.

Maryam has been given a broom of dry reeds and set to sweep the courtyard while the shade still covers part of it. She sweeps in long, even strokes, raising a little dust that hangs in the shafts of sunlight. Pishi the cat watches her from the top of the garden wall, then stretches, yawns, and jumps down into the shade of the pomegranate tree, where she lies down flat on the damp earth that Ali Akbar watered an hour ago.

Ostad Hossein closes his eyes for a moment. He can still feel it, the cold of the qanat water on his shins, the narrow walls on either side of his shoulders, the little flame of the lamp in his hand. He has not been down a shaft in six years. But the water he walked beside is down there, under his own house, still moving.

And at the bottom of the stair, in the cool room, it fills the stone pool, and slips out again, and goes on its way.

## Part Four — Around Nine in the Morning: The Ice Seller at the Door

By nine o'clock the line of sunlight has crossed the whole courtyard and climbed halfway up the wall of the summer room, and the brick floor, swept and damp an hour ago, is already dry and warm under your bare feet.

Then comes a knock at the street door. Not the heavy iron knocker, the one that thuds, but a lighter double rap, the knock of someone who comes every day. And a voice in the lane calls out a single word, long and drawn out, the way street sellers in Yazd have always called. The word is yakh. It means ice.

Here is a small question for this part. How do you carry a block of ice through the streets of a desert city in July, without it melting away into a puddle before it reaches anyone's door?

The answer is that you carry it early, you carry it fast, and you wrap it very well. The ice seller has been up since long before dawn. He walked out through the gardens at the edge of the city in the dark, to the great ice house, and loaded his donkey there with blocks of ice, each one bundled in coarse sacking and packed around with straw or chaff. Straw is full of tiny pockets of still air, and still air is a very poor carrier of heat, so a thick wrapping of straw slows the melting enormously. Then he set off back into the city, along the lanes that are still in shadow, and began to go from door to door. By the late morning his load will be gone. No ice seller in this quarter stays out in the afternoon heat.

So now, in the shade of the deep doorway, Bibi Khadijeh is bargaining for a block, and you can see it, when the seller pulls back the sacking. It is not clear like glass. It is cloudy and white, and a little gray in places, with flecks of straw frozen into its surface. But it is ice. Solid, cold, dripping ice, in the middle of a desert, in the middle of July.

The knock itself told the household something. Many street doors in Persian towns carried two knockers, not one. On one side hung a heavy bar of iron, which struck the door with a deep, dull thud. On the other hung a ring, which made a lighter, sharper rap. Men used the bar and women used the ring, so that anyone inside could tell from the sound alone whether to send a man or a woman to answer. The ice seller, who comes every morning and is known to everyone, simply raps with his knuckles and calls out, and it is always Bibi Khadijeh who goes.

Ice was not a luxury for the rich in a city like this, though the rich certainly had more of it. In summer, Persian towns were full of cold things. Sellers of cold water walked the bazaars with leather bags on their backs and brass cups in their hands, and passed a cup to anyone who paid a coin, or sometimes to anyone at all, as an act of piety. Sweet drinks were cooled with shavings of ice. Fruit was laid on ice. Melons were sunk in cold pools. To be able to offer a guest a cup of iced water on an afternoon in July was one of the small pleasures of ordinary life, and most families of any comfort managed it, if only with a small block like this one.

The ice seller's donkey knows the way better than he does. It stops by itself at the doors where it is always given a handful of something, and walks straight past the ones where it is not. It has been doing this round every summer morning for years, out to the ice house in the dark and back through the lanes, and it has very firm opinions about which households are generous.

And that brings us, at last, to the first of our three questions. How do you make ice in the desert?

The answer is that you do not make it in summer. You make it in winter, and you keep it. Yazd may be fiercely hot in July, but it is high on a desert plateau, and in December and January its nights are cold, often dropping below freezing. And the desert air, which is so cruel in summer, becomes an ally in winter. Because it is so dry and so clear, it lets the warmth of the ground and of any water lying on it escape straight up into the black sky, so that water can freeze on a still winter night even when the air around it is a few degrees above freezing.

So outside every Persian town of any size, there stood an ice house, called a yakhchal, which simply means ice pit. It had three parts. First, a long, high wall of mud brick, running from east to west, sometimes thirty or forty feet tall. On the north side of that wall, in its shadow, lay a row of long, shallow pools. Second, a deep pit dug into the ground nearby, with its sides lined in brick. And third, over that pit, a great dome of mud brick, rising in a cone, some of them over fifty feet high.

On clear, still winter nights, the keeper of the ice house opened a channel from the qanat and let a thin sheet of water, only a few inches deep, flow into the shaded pools. Through the night, under the open sky, the water froze. Before dawn, workers walked out onto the pools, broke the ice with mallets into slabs, and slid it into the pit under the dome, packing each layer with straw. The next night they did it again, and the next, all through the cold months, until the pit was full. Then they sealed the doors and waited for summer. The wall had kept the sun off the pools by day. Now the thick dome and the cool earth kept the heat off the ice. And when the heat came, the ice was still there, waiting, deep under the dome, to be dug out block by block and sold at the door.

Here is a surprising detail. The Persians were not the first to store ice in a hot country, and neither, perhaps, were they the last to boast about it. Nearly three and a half thousand years before our morning, a king named Zimri-Lim ruled a city called Mari, on the Euphrates River, far to the west of here in the land we now call Syria. A clay tablet survives from his reign, now kept in the Louvre in Paris, which records that he built an ice house in the town of Terqa, on the bank of the river. And the tablet proudly declares that no king before him had ever built one there.

So the idea of gathering the cold of winter and keeping it for summer is very, very old in this part of the world. What the Persians did was to make it ordinary, to put an ice house outside almost every town, and to make it something that a weaver's family in Yazd could buy at their own front door.

Our source for this part was living in Persia in the very years of our story. His name was Jean Chardin. He was a French jeweler's son, born in Paris in 1643, who traveled to Persia in his early twenties to trade in precious stones, and fell in love with the country. He spent the years from 1666 to 1670 in Isfahan, the Persian capital, less than two weeks northwest of Yazd by caravan, and he came back for many more years after that. He learned Persian. He traded with the Shah's own court. And he wrote one of the greatest descriptions of Persia that any outsider has ever written, published in French in the years after 1686.

Chardin described the ice pools of Isfahan. He explained how, in winter, water was let into shallow pits in the shade and left to freeze overnight, and how in the morning it was broken and carried into deep storehouses, and how the ice was then sold all through the summer. To a man from Paris, in a century when ice in summer was a luxury for princes, it was a marvel that ordinary people in Persia could cool their water with it.

Historians do not all agree about how old the ice houses we can still see today really are. Some of the great mud domes in the desert towns are thought to be several hundred years old, built under the Safavid kings of Chardin's own time. Others say that mud brick needs constant repair, that the domes have been rebuilt again and again, and that what stands today is mostly from the last two centuries. The fairest view is probably that the buildings are younger than the idea, and the idea is very old indeed.

Now, come to the doorway, just you, and hold the ice.

You step into the shadow of the deep entrance. The donkey stands patiently in the lane, flicking its ears at the flies, its sides hung with dripping sacks. The seller is a thin man in a brown cloak, with a cloth wound around his head, and his hands are red and wet from handling the cold.

He breaks a small piece off the corner of a block with a little iron hammer and hands it to you, the way a baker might hand a child a crust.

It is so cold in your palm that it almost burns. Water runs between your fingers at once and drips onto the dusty step, and the drops darken the brick and then fade, almost as fast as they land. You hold the shard up to the light. It is cloudy and white, full of tiny bubbles, frozen in some shallow pool outside the city on a night last January, when the stars were bright and the frost lay on the gardens. It has waited in the dark for six months to reach you.

You touch it to your forehead. Then to the back of your neck. A thin, cold line of water runs down your spine, and for one long, delicious moment, you forget that it is July.

Today, of course, ice in Yazd comes out of electric freezers, in neat white cubes. But the old ice houses still stand at the edges of several towns in the desert, in Meybod and Abarkuh and elsewhere, their great conical domes rising out of the sand, and visitors walk inside and look up into the dim cool space where the ice once waited for summer.

Bibi Khadijeh pays for her block with a few small copper coins, counted out from a knotted handkerchief. She does not buy a large one. A small block, enough for the day. The seller ties it in a fresh piece of sacking, lifts it onto her shoulder, and moves on down the lane, calling his long word.

Ostad Hossein has been watching from his place in the summer room.

"I worked one winter at an ice house," he tells Reza, who has come running to see. "When I was young. Before I went down the shafts. Out in the cold, before the sun came up, breaking the ice on the pools with a wooden mallet. My hands were so cold I could not feel them, and then they would burn when they got warm again. And the sky. You never saw so many stars."

"I would like to see that," says Reza.

"It was cold," says his grandfather. "I did not like it. But now, in summer, every time I hold a piece of ice, I remember those nights, and I like them very much."

In the kitchen doorway, Fatemeh has already decided what the ice is for. Not for the morning, and not for the hottest hour, when everyone will be too tired to enjoy it. It is for the evening. She has a jar of sour cherry syrup, made in June when the cherries came in from the gardens, dark red and thick, and tonight, when the sun is down and the family sits together in the courtyard, she will shave the ice into a bowl and pour the syrup over it, and stir in cold water from the pool, and that will be the best moment of the whole day. She does not say any of this aloud. But Maryam, who knows her mother very well, sees her glance at the ice and then at the syrup jar on its shelf, and smiles.

The block of ice does not stay in the courtyard. Bibi Khadijeh carries it straight to the low door in the corner and goes carefully down the forty steps, holding the wall with her free hand. Maryam goes ahead of her with the lamp.

At the bottom, the cool room is dim and quiet. The stone pool lies there in the lamplight, its water moving slowly, steadily, in from the dark opening on one side and out on the other. On a ledge beside it stands a large clay jar, half buried in damp sand. Bibi Khadijeh lowers the ice into the jar, packs the straw back around it, and lays a folded cloth over the top.

Down here, beside the cold water, in the cool of the earth, the ice will keep until supper. And the pool beside it whispers on, as it has every day of Bibi Khadijeh's life.

## Part Five — Around Eleven in the Morning: Down the Steps to the Cool Room

By eleven o'clock the sun stands high and white over Yazd, the shadows have shrunk to thin dark strips at the foot of the walls, and the air in the courtyard has begun to shimmer above the bricks.

It is close to a hundred degrees Fahrenheit now, and still climbing. The pool in the courtyard, so cold at sunrise, is warm to the touch. The leaves of the pomegranate tree hang a little limp. Even the sparrows that hopped about the garden bed this morning have vanished into crevices in the walls. The lane outside is empty. The bazaar, a few streets away, is closing its shutters one by one, and the whole city is preparing to do what every sensible creature in the desert does at noon in July. It is going to hide.

Here is a small question for this part. Why are the walls of this house so very thick, and why is the courtyard sunk lower than the street?

Let us start with the walls. They are built of sun-dried mud brick, plastered over with a mixture of clay and chopped straw that the Persians call kahgel. In places they are two or three feet thick. From the outside they look plain and heavy, almost like the walls of a fortress. But that thickness is not for defense. It is for time.

A thick wall of earth takes a very long while to heat up. All through the morning, the sun beats on the outside of it, and the heat soaks in slowly, inch by inch, the way heat creeps into a loaf of bread in the oven. By the time the warmth of the noon sun has worked its way through to the inner face of the wall, many hours have passed. It is evening. The sun has gone down. The air outside is cooling. And so the heat that the wall drank in at noon comes out at night, when it can be let go through open doors and into the open sky, instead of in the middle of the day, when it would be unbearable.

This is the answer to the first half of our small question. The walls are thick so that the heat of noon arrives inside the house only after dark.

And the sunken courtyard has its own reason. Many houses in old Yazd were built with their courtyard floor several feet below the level of the lane, so that you step down into them from the street. Digging down brings the house closer to the cool, steady temperature of the earth. It brings it closer to the water channels that run beneath the city. And at night, cool air, which is heavier than warm air, slides down off the rooftops and settles into the courtyard like water into a basin, and stays there until morning. A sunken courtyard is a pool for holding the cold of the night.

The city itself was built the same way, for shade. The lanes of old Yazd are narrow and winding, and they turn corners often, so that the sun can reach the bottom of them only for an hour or two around noon. In many places, the lane passes beneath a covered archway, a little roofed passage that links the houses on either side, and under those arches the air stays cool and dim even in July. And the roofs are rounded into domes and vaults wherever they can be. A dome in the sun is never all in the sun at once. One side always lies in its own shadow, and as the day goes on, the shadow moves around it, so that the dome sheds heat into the air on its shaded side even while it is soaking it up on the other.

Even the windows are made for the heat. On the outside walls there are hardly any at all, only a few small openings set high up, under the roof, where they let out hot air without letting in the sun. The real windows of the house all face inward, onto the courtyard, and many of them are filled with screens of carved wood or plaster pierced in delicate patterns of stars and flowers, so that the light comes through broken into a hundred small pieces and the glare is softened before it reaches the room.

Every room in a house like this had its season. The rooms on the south side of the courtyard face north, into the shade, and they are for summer. The rooms on the north side face south, toward the low winter sun, and they are for winter. The family moves around its own courtyard as the year turns, the way some birds move between countries.

Here is our surprising detail. The deeper you go into the earth, the less it feels the seasons. A few feet down, the soil is still warm in summer and cold in winter. But a little deeper, around fifteen or twenty feet, the ground stays at nearly the same temperature all year round, close to the average of the whole year's weather above it. In Yazd, that average is somewhere in the upper sixties Fahrenheit, around nineteen or twenty Celsius. So a room dug down to that depth stays roughly at the temperature of a cool spring day, in July and in January, by day and by night. And when you add a pool of cold running water, and a breath of moving air passing over it, the room can feel cooler still.

That is the sardab, which means cold water. It is the deepest room of the house, and on a summer noon in Yazd, there is no better place to be.

Our source for this part is Jean Chardin again, the French jeweler who lived in Isfahan in the very years of our story. He noticed something about the houses there that delighted him. Many of them had deep cellars, he wrote, reached by long flights of steep steps. And the water drawn up from them in summer, he said, was "as cool as any Ice." For a traveler sweating through his first Persian summer in a heavy European coat, it must have seemed almost like magic, a house that kept winter hidden in its basement.

Historians and architects do not all agree about how much of the coolness of these rooms came from the earth itself, and how much from the water and the moving air. Some argue that the depth was what mattered most, and that any deep cellar in this climate would stay cool. Others point out that the coolest underground rooms were nearly always the ones with running water and with a wind tower feeding air down to them, and that the moving air passing over water did much of the work. The fairest view is probably that the Persian builders did not choose one or the other. They used all three together, earth and water and air, as if they had always known they belonged together.

Now, come down the stairs, just you, into the cold.

You stand at the low doorway in the corner of the courtyard, and the heat is pressing on your back and shoulders like a hand. You duck your head under the lintel. The first step is warm. The second is less warm. You go down, carefully, one hand on the smooth, cool wall, which has been polished by many generations of hands to the gloss of old leather.

With every step the air changes. It thickens and softens. It loses its dry, dusty bite and becomes damp and still and faintly scented with wet stone and clay, like the air inside a well. The light from the doorway above grows dimmer and dimmer, gold, then gray, until you are moving by touch and by the glow of the little oil lamp that Maryam carries below you.

Twenty steps. Thirty. Your skin, which has been prickling with heat all morning, begins to feel cool. The sweat on your neck turns cold. You let out a long breath, without meaning to.

Forty steps, and you are at the bottom. The room opens around you, a low vaulted chamber of fired brick, with a rug laid along one side and cotton cushions piled against the wall. In the middle lies the stone pool, eight-sided, with the lamp's reflection trembling on its surface. You hear the water now, clearly, the soft continuous murmur of it, coming in through the dark mouth in the wall and slipping out again on the far side.

You kneel at the edge and put your wrists into the water. The cold runs up your arms and into your chest. Up above, the city is burning. Down here, it is spring.

Today, people in hot countries all over the world cool their homes with machines that hum day and night. But architects have begun to look again at houses like this one, at the thick earth walls and the sunken rooms and the water underneath, to learn how to keep a building cool with no power at all.

The whole family comes down, one after another. Ali Akbar has left his loom, because by eleven his hands are too damp for fine silk, and he will not weave again until the evening. Mahmud follows him, glad of the excuse to stop. Fatemeh comes down with a large round melon in her arms, one of the long pale melons that Yazd is famous for, and sets it in the pool to cool, where it bobs gently in the current, turning slowly around like a small green moon.

Ostad Hossein comes down last, very slowly, one step at a time, with Mahmud holding his elbow. He lowers himself onto the cushions against the wall, closes his eyes, and lets out a long sigh of pure contentment.

"Now," he says. "Now I am a young man again."

Reza has already found his way to the edge of the pool, where the water comes in from the dark mouth in the wall. He leans over it, as far as his mother will let him, and peers into the opening. It is black inside, and the water comes out of it smooth and fast, without a ripple, as if it were glass.

"Is this where your tunnel is, Grandfather?" he says.

"That is where it comes in," says the old man, without opening his eyes. "It has been under the city for a long way already. And before that, under the desert for a very long way. And before that, in the mountains, as snow."

Reza puts his hand in the water, flat, and holds it there, feeling it press against his palm. Then he pulls it out, shaking it, because it is so cold.

Pishi the cat has come down the stairs on her own, silent and dignified, and she stretches herself out at full length on the cool brick floor beside the rug, with her chin on her paws, as close to the pool as a cat can bear to lie.

Bibi Khadijeh checks the clay jar on its ledge. The ice is there, still wrapped in its straw and its cloth, smaller than it was, but whole. She lays the cloth back over it and sits down on the rug beside her husband.

Along the walls of the cool room stand the things that must not spoil. A crock of yogurt, made yesterday. A round of white cheese in salted water. A pot of butter, covered with a cloth. A basket of eggs. And a row of large, unglazed clay water jars, standing in a shallow tray of sand. The jars are made of porous clay on purpose, so that a little water seeps slowly through their walls and dampens the outside. As that dampness dries in the air, it carries heat away with it, and the water inside the jar grows colder than the room around it. In a country this dry, a sweating jar is one of the oldest ways there is of keeping water cool, and every household in Yazd has a few.

Bibi Khadijeh looks around the room with quiet satisfaction. She remembers her first summer in this house, forty-five years ago, when she was nineteen and newly married and very homesick for her mother. Her husband's mother had brought her down these same steps at noon, sat her beside this same pool, and put a cup of cold water in her hands. And she had looked at the water moving in the lamplight, and decided that perhaps this house would be all right after all. She has never told anyone that. But she thinks of it every summer, on the first truly hot day, when the family comes down the stairs together for the first time.

Fatemeh lifts the melon out of the pool, wet and gleaming, and sets it on a wooden board. She cuts it with a long knife into crescent slices, pale green, almost white at the center, so juicy that the board is swimming. She passes the first slice to her father-in-law, and the second to her mother-in-law, and then the rest go around the room from hand to hand. Nobody talks while they eat it. There is only the soft sound of the water, and the sound of seven people eating melon in the cool dark, very contentedly.

For a while, no one speaks. The lamp burns low and steady. The melon turns slowly in the current. The water in the stone pool slides in from the dark and out into the dark, and the cold rises from it, softly, filling the room.

## Part Six — Around Noon: The Hottest Hours and the Midday Rest

At noon, the call to the midday prayer drifts down the forty steps from the bright square of the doorway, faint and far away, as if it were coming from another country.

Up there, the sun stands almost straight overhead. The courtyard floor is too hot to walk on barefoot. The lane outside is a white glare between two walls. But down here, in the cool room under the house, the lamp burns steady, the water murmurs, and the family is gathering for the midday meal.

At this hour, the sun over Yazd climbs to nearly eighty degrees above the horizon, so close to straight overhead that a man standing in the open lane would look down and find his shadow almost hidden beneath his own feet. Nobody is standing in the open lane. The carpenters and coppersmiths of the bazaar have pulled down their shutters and lain down in the backs of their shops. The camels of a caravan that arrived before dawn are kneeling in the shaded arcades of a caravanserai near the city gate, chewing slowly, their eyes half closed. In July, caravans crossing the Persian desert did not travel by day at all if they could help it. They rested through the heat in the shelter of the great roadside inns, and set out again at dusk, and walked all night under the stars.

Here is a small question for this part. What do you eat, at the hottest hour of the hottest month, in one of the hottest cities in Persia?

The answer, for a Persian family in 1670, was not simply whatever was cool to the touch. It was whatever was cool in its nature. Persians, like many peoples of the old world, believed that every food had a hidden temperament. Some foods were warm by nature, and some were cold, and some were moist, and some were dry, and a wise cook balanced them, according to the season and the person eating. This was not a folk superstition. It was the medicine of the age, taught by physicians, written in great books, and understood in every kitchen.

In summer, when the world itself was hot and dry, you leaned toward the cold foods. Yogurt was cold. Cucumber was cold. Most melons were cold. Mint and purslane were cooling, and so were sour fruits and vinegar and the juice of unripe grapes. Hot foods, like dates, honey, walnuts and most meats, were eaten more sparingly in July, and balanced with something cold beside them on the cloth.

So the answer to our small question is a dish of yogurt. Not hot rice, not stew, not roasted meat. Fatemeh has brought down a wide bowl of thick yogurt, stirred with finely chopped cucumber and a handful of mint, with a scattering of dried rose petals crumbled over the top. Beside it there is bread, and more cheese, and a dish of fresh herbs, and the rest of the melon. And there is cold water from the sweating jars, poured into each cup. It is a meal that asks nothing of the body. It is light and cool and sour and green, and it is exactly right.

There is one drink, too, that Fatemeh keeps for the hottest days. It is called sekanjabin, and it is made by boiling vinegar and sugar or honey together into a thick, sweet and sour syrup, sometimes with a few sprigs of mint dropped in as it cools. A spoonful of it, stirred into a cup of cold water, makes a drink that is sharp and sweet at once, and wonderfully refreshing. The old physicians approved of it, because it balanced the warmth of the sweetness with the coolness of the vinegar. Today, Fatemeh sets the jar of it beside the water, and each cup comes back empty.

The bread is torn into pieces and dipped in the yogurt. The herbs are rolled up in the bread with a little cheese. Ostad Hossein, who has fewer teeth than he used to, soaks his bread in his cup of water first, to soften it, and nobody remarks on it, because he has been doing it for years.

Here is a surprising detail. The idea behind that bowl of yogurt goes back, in written form, at least six and a half centuries before our noon, to a Persian physician whose name every educated person in Yazd would know. His name was Ibn Sina, and in Europe he was called Avicenna. Around the year 1025 he finished a vast medical encyclopedia called The Canon of Medicine, which gathered together the teachings of the Greeks and the Persians and his own long years of practice. For six hundred years afterward, it was studied in the medical schools of Persia and of Europe alike. In it, he wrote at length about the temperaments of the body and of foods, and about how a person should eat and drink and rest according to the season. A summer meal in a sardab in Yazd was, in its own quiet way, a page from Avicenna.

Our source for this part is another French traveler of these same years. His name was Jean-Baptiste Tavernier, and he was a dealer in diamonds and precious stones, who made six great journeys to Persia and India between 1630 and 1668. In 1676 he published an account of those journeys. In it he wrote about Yazd. He described the silks made there, some of them woven through with threads of gold and silver. He praised the wool cloth of the city, which he said was finer even than silk. And he wrote with real pleasure about the melons of Yazd, of several different kinds, whose flesh might be green, or yellow, or a deep red.

So the melon turning slowly in the cool pool this morning was no ordinary fruit. It was one of the things that travelers, crossing the desert for weeks, remembered about this city long after they had gone home.

Historians do not all agree about how much of what Tavernier wrote about Yazd he saw for himself. He was a careful observer of jewels and of prices, and much of his writing is sharp and reliable. But some scholars point out that parts of his account of Yazd, especially about its weaving, read as though they came from what merchants told him rather than from his own eyes. The fairest view is probably that his melons, at least, he tasted himself. A diamond merchant crossing the desert in July would not have forgotten a good melon.

Now, lie back, just you, in the hottest hour of the day.

You are lying on a cotton rug on the floor of the cool room, with a cushion under your head. Above you, the brick vault curves low and dim, its bricks laid in a gentle herringbone pattern that you can just make out in the lamplight. The air on your skin is cool and still and faintly damp.

Someone has dipped a cloth in the pool and laid it, folded, across your forehead. It is cold, and a single drop of water runs down from it into your hair. Beside you, Bibi Khadijeh is moving a fan slowly back and forth, a square fan of woven palm fiber on a short wooden handle, and every few seconds a soft breath of air passes over your face and is gone.

You can hear the water. You can always hear the water, down here. It does not stop and start. It does not rise or fall. It simply goes on, soft and endless, like someone breathing in the next room, and after a while you stop noticing it as a sound at all, and it becomes something more like a feeling, a coolness in the ear.

Far above you, at the top of the stairs, the doorway is a square of blinding white. Out there, the bricks of the courtyard are baking. The air above the roofs is quivering. The whole city lies silent under the sun, as if it were holding its breath. But none of that reaches you. You let your eyes close. The fan moves. The water flows. And the heat of the desert passes over your house like a storm passing over a ship, while you lie below, safe in the hold.

Today, in many towns in Iran, the old rhythm still holds in summer. Shops close in the early afternoon and open again in the evening, and the streets at three o'clock in July are almost as empty as they were in 1670.

The midday meal is over. The bowls have been rinsed in the outflow of the pool and stacked on the ledge. And now the cool room settles into rest.

Ostad Hossein is already asleep, sitting up against the wall, with his chin on his chest and his hands folded on his stomach. Bibi Khadijeh is beside him, not quite asleep, still moving the fan in slow, even strokes, more out of habit than need.

Fatemeh has taken her embroidery to the lowest step of the stair, where a faint gray light falls from the doorway far above, enough to see by. She is working a band of silk with small flowers, red and gold on a dark blue ground, to be sewn along the edge of a cushion. Her needle goes in and out, steady and small. She hums under her breath, a song with no words, so quietly that only Maryam, sitting beside her, can hear it.

Maryam is supposed to be resting. Instead she is watching her mother's needle, and every so often her mother hands her the cloth and lets her make one stitch, very carefully, and then takes it back.

On the rug, Mahmud and his father have set up a board for nard, the old Persian game of counters and dice that people elsewhere call backgammon. They play almost without speaking, the dice rattling softly in a little leather cup, the counters clicking on the wooden board. Ali Akbar is winning, as he usually does. Mahmud frowns at the board as if it has personally offended him.

According to an old Persian story, nard was invented by a wise minister at the court of an ancient Persian king, as an answer to the game of chess, which had been sent from India as a challenge. Ostad Hossein told Mahmud that story many years ago. Mahmud does not remember it well. He only remembers that the Persian minister won.

Between moves, Ali Akbar talks a little about the merchant who ordered the crimson taffeta. He is a Yazdi man, but he lives most of the year in Isfahan, where the court is, and where the great families pay well for fine cloth. He will leave with a caravan at the end of the month, traveling by night, and he wants the silk finished, folded and wrapped before then. Ali Akbar has promised it. That means weaving every cool hour from now until then, early in the morning and late into the evening, by lamplight if he must.

"Will you take me to Isfahan one day?" Mahmud asks, rattling the dice.

"One day," says his father. "When you can weave a length of cloth that I would put my name to. Then you can carry it there yourself, and see the great square, and the palace of the Shah, and the bazaar that goes on for a mile under its roofs. And then you will come home and tell me Yazd is better."

Mahmud does not believe this for a moment. But he throws the dice and moves his counters, and says nothing.

And Reza is asleep at last. He fell asleep in the middle of a sentence, lying on his stomach at the very edge of the rug, with one hand trailing over the edge of the pool and his fingers just touching the water. Pishi the cat is pressed against his side, a long gray shape in the dimness, her eyes half closed, her tail twitching now and then, as if she is dreaming of sparrows.

Ali Akbar wins the game at last, as everyone expected, and gathers the counters into the leather cup. Mahmud demands another game. His father shakes his head, smiling, and lies back on the rug with his hands behind his head, looking up at the brick vault. "Later," he says. "When it is cool enough to lose with dignity." And within a few breaths, he too is asleep.

Down here, even the sound of the game has stopped. There is only the lamp, and the water, and the slow breathing of the sleepers.

His mother looks over from the stair and sees his hand in the water. She thinks about moving it. Then she decides to leave it where it is.

Bibi Khadijeh's fan slows, and then stops. Her hand rests on her knee, still holding it. She is asleep after all, sitting upright beside her husband, her head tipped back against the cool brick of the wall, her face peaceful in the lamplight. Fatemeh glances at her, and at the old man, and at her sleeping son, and lowers her humming until it is no more than a breath.

Up above, the sun moves on, very slowly, across the white sky. Down here, the lamp burns, and the family rests, and the water from the mountains slides through the stone pool, in from the dark and out into the dark, cold and clear and quiet, carrying the heat of the day gently away.

## Part Seven — Around Three in the Afternoon: The Tower That Catches the Wind

By three o'clock the heat outside has reached its peak, a dry, blazing hundred and three degrees in the shade of the courtyard, and the family has begun, one by one, to wake from their rest.

They do not go up into the sun. Not yet. But they climb as far as the summer room, the deep open hall on the shaded side of the courtyard, because at this hour of the afternoon, something has begun to happen there. A breeze has risen over the desert. Not a strong one. Just a hot, restless stir of air, coming in off the plain from the northwest. And the tall square tower that stands above the summer room has begun to breathe.

Here is a small question for this part. Why does the family keep a small pool of water in the floor of the summer room, right beneath the tower?

The summer room is the grandest space in the house. It is a tall hall, open along its whole front to the courtyard, with a high vaulted ceiling and walls plastered smooth and white. In the floor, set right in the middle, there is a shallow basin of stone, a little square pool no deeper than your hand, with a thin jet of water rising from a spout in its center, fed by a pipe from the qanat channel below. And directly above it, in the ceiling, there is an opening, a dark square hole leading up into the tower.

The tower is called a badgir, which means wind catcher. It rises from the roof above the summer room, square and tall, some twenty or thirty feet above the flat roof, taller than any other part of the house. Near its top, on each of its four sides, there are long, narrow openings, like the slits of a castle wall, divided by thin brick fins. Inside, the tower is not one hollow shaft, but several, separated by walls of brick running straight down its middle, so that its inside, seen from below, looks like the cross-section of a cut fruit.

All across Yazd, these towers rise from the roofs. Some have openings on only one side. Some have four. Some, on the grandest houses, have six or even eight sides. They are the first thing every traveler notices when he comes over the last ridge and sees the city spread out on the plain, a forest of tan towers standing in the heat haze, and they are what Yazd is still known for today.

We will come back to how they work. But first, the answer to our small question.

The pool is there because water, as it dries into the air, takes heat with it. When a breath of hot, dry desert air comes down out of the tower, it passes over that shallow basin, and over the damp brick around it, and over the thin jet of water rising in the middle. A little of the water dries away into the air. And as it does, it draws heat out of the air itself, so that the breeze leaving the basin and spreading through the room is cooler and a little softer than the breeze that came down the tower. The pool is a small, quiet machine for turning dry heat into moist coolness. And in a city where the summer air is as dry as Yazd's, it works remarkably well.

Here is a surprising detail. The tallest wind catcher in Yazd, and often said to be the tallest in the world, stands in a garden on the western side of the city, called Dowlatabad. It rises around a hundred and ten feet into the air, about thirty-three meters, and it is eight-sided, with openings on every face. Below it lies a hall with a pool and a fountain, and on the hottest days the air that pours down out of that great tower over the water is cool enough to make visitors shiver.

But here is the honest part. That tower was not built until around 1747, about seventy-seven years after our summer afternoon. It belongs to a later Yazd. Most of the wind towers that stand in the city today are younger still, built or rebuilt in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, because mud brick in the desert has to be renewed again and again. The towers above our family's roof in 1670 were their ancestors, built to the same idea, in the same city, by the same kind of builders, but they have long since crumbled and been replaced.

Our source for this part is a modern record of a very old place. In July of 2017, the historic city of Yazd was inscribed as a World Heritage Site, and the record of that inscription describes the old town as a survivor of a whole way of living in the desert. It speaks of the city's earthen houses and narrow lanes, its bazaars and mosques, its water channels flowing underground, and, rising over all of it, its wind catchers. The record notes that the people of Yazd had found a way to live in one of the driest regions of the earth by using the materials of the desert itself, earth and water and wind, rather than fighting against them. It is, in a sense, an official confirmation of what every family here already knew.

Historians and architects do not all agree about where the wind catcher was first invented. Some believe it began on the Iranian plateau, in very ancient times, and spread from Persia to the rest of the Middle East along with the qanat. Others point to the houses of ancient Egypt, where similar slanted openings on the roof caught the cool wind off the Nile, and to the ports of the Persian Gulf, where wind towers were built over merchants' houses facing the sea breeze. The fairest view is probably that many peoples in hot countries looked up at the wind and had the same idea, and that the Persians, in towns like Yazd, simply carried it further than anyone.

Now, come and stand beneath the tower, just you.

You walk into the summer room from the courtyard, and the change is immediate. The light softens. The heat of the open air drops away from your shoulders like a cloak. The room is tall and dim and white, and it feels larger inside than it looked from outside.

You walk to the little stone pool in the middle of the floor and stand beside it. Then you look up.

Above you, the square opening in the ceiling leads up into darkness, and at the very top of it, far above, there are thin bright lines of daylight where the slits of the tower open to the sky. And you can feel it. A steady current of air is falling on your face from above, like water poured gently from a jar. It is not cold, exactly. It is the desert wind, after all. But it is moving, and as it passes over the little pool at your feet, it picks up the coolness of the water, and when it reaches your arms and your neck, it feels like the first breath of evening.

You hold your hand up into it. You can feel the air pressing against your palm. Then you lower your hand to the level of the pool, and the air there is cooler still, touched by the water, and smelling faintly of wet stone.

Around you, the curtains at the open front of the room stir and swell gently outward, toward the courtyard. The air that came down the tower is flowing out across the room and away, carrying the heat with it. The whole room is breathing, slowly, in from above and out through the front, as steadily as a sleeping animal.

The family has gathered here, on the rugs along the walls of the summer room, where the moving air is strongest. Ali Akbar has gone back to the weaving room, which opens off the summer room through an arch, and is threading the loom for the evening. Fatemeh is picking over a tray of lentils for supper, flicking out the little stones. Maryam is shelling a basket of almonds, cracking them one by one with a small stone. Mahmud is lying flat on his back on the rug, directly under the tower, with his arms spread wide, letting the falling air wash over him.

Bibi Khadijeh comes in from the courtyard with a jug of water from the pool, and walks slowly along the floor of the summer room, sprinkling it from her fingers over the bricks, the way a gardener sprinkles seedlings. The water darkens the brick in a scatter of spots, and within a few minutes the spots have faded and vanished, gone up into the moving air. Each one, as it dries, takes a little of the heat with it. She has done this every summer afternoon for forty-five years, and she could not tell you the reason in words. She only knows that a sprinkled floor makes a cooler room, and that a house where the women sprinkle the floors is a house that takes care of itself.

The tower itself was built by a master builder of the quarter, in the time of Ostad Hossein's grandfather, and it has been patched and replastered many times since. Each spring, before the heat begins, Mahmud and his father climb up onto the roof and check its openings, clear out the dust and the old nests, and spread a fresh coat of mud and straw over any crack. A wind tower, like a qanat, is only as good as the care it is given.

Reza is standing at the edge of the little pool, looking up into the tower, with his head tipped so far back that he is in danger of falling over.

"Grandfather," he says. "How does it know to come down?"

Ostad Hossein laughs, and beckons him over. He takes the boy's hand and holds it up, palm flat, into the falling air.

"Feel that," he says. "The wind is blowing across the top of the city, out there, over the roofs. Up high, it blows faster than down here, because there is nothing to slow it. The tower reaches up into it, high above the roofs, and the side that faces the wind catches it, like a sail catches it, and it has nowhere to go but down. So it comes down, into the house."

"And then it goes away again," says Reza, frowning.

"Out," agrees the old man. "Out across the room, and out into the courtyard. And at the same time, on the other side of the tower, the side away from the wind, the air is being pulled up and out, the way smoke is pulled up a chimney. So the tower pushes air in on one side, and draws it out on the other, and the house breathes through it. In through the tower, over the water, across the room, and away."

"But what if there is no wind?" Reza says. "Sometimes there is no wind at all."

"Ah," says Ostad Hossein, and he looks pleased, the way he does when a digger asks him a good question about a tunnel. "Then the tower works the other way. The sun beats on it all day, and the top of it gets very hot, hotter than anything else. And hot air rises. So the warm air inside the tower climbs up and out of the top, and as it goes, it pulls air up behind it, out of the house. And the house must get new air from somewhere, to fill the space."

Reza thinks. "From the courtyard."

"From the courtyard," agrees his grandfather. "And from below. From the cool room, at the bottom of the stairs, where the water runs."

So this is the answer to our second question. How does a tower cool a house with no machine? It catches the wind high above the roofs and sends it down into the house. On still days, it works like a chimney instead, letting the hot air rise out of the top and drawing cooler air in from below. And on its way through the house, that moving air passes over water, over the little pool in the summer room and, below it, over the cold stone pool in the cool room, so that it arrives on your skin cooler and softer than the desert ever made it. The tower is only brick and plaster and mud. Its engine is the wind, and the sun, and the water underneath the house.

Up inside the tower, out of sight, a pair of pigeons have built a nest on one of the brick fins, and every so often you can hear them, a soft, throaty murmur echoing down the shaft. Fatemeh does not approve of them. Feathers and dust come down the tower with the wind. But Ali Akbar likes the sound, and so for this summer at least, the pigeons stay.

Reza stands for a long time with his hand up in the falling air, thinking about this.

Then he says, "So the tower is breathing in the water from your tunnel."

His grandfather looks at him, and then begins to laugh, a long, low, delighted laugh, until he has to wipe his eyes.

"Yes," he says. "Yes, I suppose it is."

And far below them, at the bottom of the forty steps, the stone pool lies cool and dim in the lamplight, its water sliding steadily through it, and above it, from a narrow duct high in the wall that rises all the way to the foot of the tower, a soft, cool current of air moves gently down across the surface of the water, and out, and up the stairs, into the house.

## Part Eight — Around Half Past Five: The Courtyard Comes Back to Life

By half past five the sun has slipped low enough in the west that the shadow of the western wall has crept out across the courtyard and covered the whole of the long pool, and for the first time since morning, the water lies in shade.

It is still hot. The thermometer, if anyone in Yazd had one, would still read close to a hundred degrees. But the light has changed. It has lost its white glare and turned gold, and the bricks of the courtyard have stopped giving off that dry, shimmering heat you could see rising from them at noon. Somewhere in the lane, a door opens. Then another. A voice calls. A child laughs. The city, which has been lying flat and silent under the sun for six hours, is slowly beginning to sit up.

Here is a small question for this part. Why, at this hour of the evening, is every household in the lane throwing water onto the ground?

You can hear it from inside the house. A long, splashing swish, then a pause, then another. Up and down the lane, women and children and servants are stepping out of their doorways with jugs and buckets, and flinging the water out in wide, glittering arcs across the beaten earth in front of their doors. It darkens the dust in long fans. It smells, at once, of something deep and wonderful, the smell of rain on dry ground, which in Yazd is one of the rarest and best-loved smells there is.

The answer has three parts, and every family here could give them to you without thinking. First, it lays the dust. All day long, the hot wind has been lifting fine powder off the desert and dropping it over the city, and by evening it lies in the lanes like flour. A splash of water holds it down. Second, it cools. A wet patch of ground, drying in the evening air, takes heat from the air above it, just as the little pool in the summer room does, so that the lane in front of a sprinkled doorway feels softer and cooler than the lane in front of a dry one. And third, it is a kind of welcome. A swept and sprinkled doorstep says that this household is awake, and well kept, and ready to receive the evening.

In the bazaar, a few lanes away, the shutters are coming up again. The coppersmiths are taking up their hammers, and soon their tapping will ring through the covered streets until long after dark. The fruit sellers are setting out fresh baskets of grapes and figs and the first small apricots from the high villages. Evening in a Persian summer town is the busiest time of the whole day, a second morning, and many shops will stay open late into the night, lit by lamps, because that is when people can bear to come out and buy.

Inside the courtyard, the same thing is happening on a larger scale. Maryam and Mahmud have filled jugs from the long pool and are sprinkling the whole brick floor of the courtyard, every inch of it, while their mother sweeps the wet dust into the garden bed. The bricks hiss faintly as the water hits them. Steam would rise, if the air were not so dry. Instead, the water simply vanishes, almost as fast as it lands, and the courtyard grows cooler with every jug.

Here is a surprising detail. Not everyone in this lane, in 1670, is a Muslim. Yazd, more than almost any other city in Persia, was home to the Zoroastrians, the followers of the ancient faith of Persia, the religion of the old kings, long before Islam came to the country. In the centuries after the Arab conquest, most Persians slowly became Muslims. But in a few places, especially in Yazd and in the villages around it, and in Kerman to the southeast, Zoroastrian families held on to their faith, generation after generation. They kept their fire temples and their festivals and their prayers in the old language. And in and around Yazd, they kept a sacred fire, which Zoroastrians believe has been tended without ever going out for well over a thousand years.

So it is no surprise that the woman sprinkling the lane outside the house next door is not dressed like Fatemeh. She wears a loose, brightly colored tunic over full trousers, with a long scarf of printed cotton wrapped around her head and falling down her back. Her name is Banu, and she is a Zoroastrian, and she and Bibi Khadijeh have been neighbors for thirty years.

Many of the things that seem most Persian about this city are older than Islam, and some of them belong to both faiths at once. The Persian New Year, Nowruz, which falls on the first day of spring, was a Zoroastrian festival long before it was a national one, and in 1670 Muslim and Zoroastrian families in Yazd alike spring-clean their houses and set out green sprouts for it. And among the festivals Banu's family keeps there is one called Tirgan, a feast of water and of rain. On that day, children run through the lanes splashing each other with water, and people tie thin bands of twisted colored thread around their wrists, and wear them for some days, and then, by custom, untie them and let the wind carry them away from a rooftop. In a desert city, a festival of water is not hard to understand.

Our source for this part is Jean Chardin once more, our French jeweler in Isfahan. He took a great interest in the Zoroastrians, whom the Persians of his time called Gabrs. There was a small community of them in a suburb of Isfahan, and he visited them and asked them about their beliefs. And he wrote that most of the Zoroastrians left in Persia lived in the country around Yazd and Kerman, where, far from the capital, they farmed and gardened and kept their ancient ways. He described them as quiet, hardworking people, and especially good farmers, and he took care, as few European writers of his age did, to try to understand their faith on its own terms.

Historians do not all agree about how the Zoroastrians of Yazd were treated in these years. Some point to periods under the later Safavid kings when they faced real pressure, heavy taxes and attempts to force them to convert. Others point to the long ordinary life of the city itself, where Muslim and Zoroastrian families lived side by side in the same quarters, traded in the same bazaars, and drank from the same qanats, century after century. The fairest view is probably that both are true, and that what happened in the palace and what happened in the lane were often two different stories.

Now, step out into the lane, just you, at the turning of the day.

You come out through the deep doorway, and the first thing you notice is the smell. Wet earth. Warm, dark and rich, rising up from the sprinkled lane all around you. It fills your head. It is the smell of a garden after rain, in a city where it hardly ever rains.

The lane is in shadow now, from wall to wall. The sky above it has turned a deep, clear blue, and the tops of the wind towers, high above you, are still lit by the low sun, glowing orange against the blue like the tips of candles. A pair of swifts goes screaming past overhead, so fast you barely see them.

Children are coming out. Two small boys run past you, barefoot on the damp earth, chasing a hoop of bent willow with a stick. A girl sits on a doorstep with a baby in her lap, singing to it. Further along, an old man has brought out a stool and set it down in the shadow of an arch, and is sitting there with his hands on his knees, simply watching the lane, the way he has probably watched it every summer evening for fifty years.

You walk a little way, and the ground is cool under your feet. You pass a doorway where someone has just thrown out a bucket of water, and the splash catches your ankles, cool and sudden, and you laugh without meaning to. From somewhere ahead comes the sound of a metal bowl being struck, ringing softly, and the voice of the water seller, calling as he walks, his leather bag swinging on his back.

Above you, the sky is fading from blue to gold to the faintest rose. The heat is still there. It hangs in the walls on either side of you, and you can feel it on your face when you walk close to them. But the air itself is moving now, and softening, and the worst of the day is over.

In the doorway, Bibi Khadijeh and Banu are talking across the space between their two houses, each standing on her own damp doorstep, each holding an empty jug. They talk about the heat, as everyone in Yazd talks about the heat in July. They talk about Banu's grandson, who has a cough. They talk about the price of ice, which Banu says is too high, and Bibi Khadijeh says is always too high, and both of them agree that it was cheaper when they were girls.

Banu has brought something. She holds out a small basket covered with a fig leaf, and inside, when Bibi Khadijeh lifts the leaf, there are a dozen ripe figs from the tree in her own courtyard, purple and soft and just beginning to split.

"The first of the year," says Banu. "My tree is always earlier than yours."

"Your tree has more sun," says Bibi Khadijeh, which is what she says every year. And she goes inside and comes back with a little cloth bundle of the almonds that Maryam shelled this afternoon, and puts it into Banu's empty basket, because in Yazd, as in most of Persia, you never return a neighbor's basket empty.

Maryam has slipped out to stand beside her grandmother. Banu notices her, and smiles, and reaches into the fold of her scarf.

"I kept this for you," she says, and holds out a short length of thread, twisted from strands of red and green and yellow and blue, the kind her own grandchildren wore at Tirgan. "Tie it on your wrist. When it is time, give it to the wind, and make a wish when you do."

Maryam looks at her grandmother, who nods. And so Maryam holds out her wrist, and Banu ties the thread around it with a neat double knot, and Maryam stands looking at it for a long time afterward, turning her hand this way and that in the gold light, as if it were made of silk.

Bibi Khadijeh watches with a small smile. When she was a girl, Banu's mother tied the same kind of thread around her own wrist, on a summer evening very like this one, in this same lane. She remembers giving it to the wind from the roof, and watching it float away over the domes. She cannot remember what she wished for. But she remembers the thread.

Inside, the house is waking up fully. Ali Akbar is back at his loom, and the knock and swish of it has started again, steady as a heartbeat, because the light in the weaving room is good now, and his hands are dry, and the crimson taffeta must be finished before the end of the month. Mahmud is beside him, winding bobbins, his hair still wet from where he poured a jug of water over his own head in the courtyard.

Overhead, the first swallows are swooping low over the courtyard, skimming the long pool for insects, and the pomegranate leaves stir faintly in the moving air.

Fatemeh has lit a small charcoal fire in the kitchen, the first fire since morning, and set a pot of rice to soak. Supper is still more than an hour away. But the shadows are long, and the day is turning, and she can feel the evening coming.

Ostad Hossein has gone, very slowly, with his stick, to the little mosque at the end of the lane, for the evening prayer. He goes every evening when his knees allow it, because it is cooler now, and because the men of the quarter sit afterward on the platform outside, talking about water and weather and the price of things, and he likes to hear what they say, and to tell them, now and then, that they are wrong.

Reza is in the courtyard, crouching at the edge of the long pool with Pishi, watching a small green frog that has appeared from somewhere and is sitting on the rim, very still, its throat pulsing. Nobody in this house knows where the frogs come from. They simply appear in the pools in summer, as if the water itself had made them.

And far below, at the bottom of the forty steps, the stone pool lies cool and dark in its vaulted room, and a breath of evening air comes down the duct from the tower and moves across the water, and the clay jar of ice on its ledge sits waiting, smaller now, but still there, for the moment after sunset when the family will gather to share it.

## Part Nine — Around Seven in the Evening: Sunset, Ice and Supper by the Pool

At about six minutes past seven, the sun sinks behind the western hills, and the last orange light slides off the tops of the wind towers, one after another, like candles being put out across the city.

For half an hour afterward, the sky stays bright, a clear deep gold in the west, fading upward through pale green to blue. Then the first star appears, low in the west, and then another. The call to the sunset prayer rises from the mosques, one voice, then many, overlapping and drifting across the rooftops. And in the courtyard of the house, as the dusk deepens around half past seven, Fatemeh lights the lamps.

There are three of them. Small clay oil lamps, each with a cotton wick, set in niches in the walls around the courtyard. Their little flames are yellow and steady in the still air, and their light falls on the surface of the long pool, and doubles there, so that there seem to be six lamps instead of three.

Here is a small question for this part. What, exactly, is a Persian sherbet?

The word will be familiar to many people listening tonight, but in 1670 it meant something rather different from the fizzy powder or the frozen dessert it means in some countries now. In Persia, a sharbat was simply a sweet, cold drink. It was made by stirring a syrup into cold water. The syrup might be made from almost anything that grew in a Persian garden. Sour cherries. Pomegranates. Lemons and limes. Quinces. Rose petals, steeped and boiled with sugar into a pale pink syrup that smelled like a summer garden. The scented water of a particular kind of willow flower, which was thought to calm the heart. Or plain sugar and rosewater, the simplest and perhaps the best of all.

What made a sherbet a sherbet, and not just a sweet drink, was the cold. It had to be cold. And in summer that meant ice, or snow brought down from the mountains and kept in the ice houses, shaved or crushed and stirred into the cup.

So the answer to our small question is this. A Persian sherbet was a syrup of fruit or flowers, stirred into water and chilled with ice. It was the drink of hospitality, offered to every guest who came through the door. It was the drink of celebration, served at weddings and feasts. And on a summer evening in Yazd, it was the reward at the end of a long day of heat. The English word sherbet, incidentally, came from this one, by way of Turkish, early in the sixteen hundreds, carried home by travelers who had tasted it in the East and could not forget it.

The rosewater in those sherbets came, very often, from the mountain country around Kashan, to the north of here, where whole valleys were planted with pink damask roses. In late spring, before the heat, the villagers picked the flowers at dawn, while the dew was still on them, and boiled them in great copper stills, catching the steam as it cooled and dripped into glass bottles. The fragrant water that came out was carried all over Persia and far beyond, to India and to Turkey. A bottle of it stands on the shelf in Fatemeh's kitchen, and a few drops go into almost every sweet thing she makes.

Here is our surprising detail. In Isfahan, the capital, there was a sherbet house belonging to the government itself, a royal sherbet house, beside the great gateway of the royal bazaar, facing the vast square in front of the Shah's palace. People came there to drink cold sherbet in the summer, the way people elsewhere might gather at a tavern. But even in Chardin's time, the sherbet houses were facing a rival. Coffee had arrived in Persia, and coffee houses had opened in the capital, around that same great square, where men sat on carpets and benches under painted ceilings, drinking small cups of the dark bitter brew and listening to storytellers and poets. Over the following century, the coffee houses won.

Our source for this part was one of the first Europeans to describe that drink. His name was Pietro della Valle, a nobleman from Rome, who set out on a long journey to the East in 1614. He traveled first to Constantinople, then through Egypt and the Holy Land, then to Baghdad, and finally, in 1617, into Persia, where he spent several years at and around the court of the great Shah Abbas. All along the way he wrote long letters home to a friend in Naples, a doctor named Mario Schipano, and those letters were later published as the story of his travels.

In one of the earliest of them, written from Constantinople in 1615, della Valle described a black drink that the Turks took hot, in small cups, sipped slowly, at any hour of the day or night. He called it kahve. And he told his friend that he meant to bring some of it home to Italy with him, because he thought nobody there had ever tasted it. In Persia, a few years later, he found it there as well.

Historians do not all agree about how widely ordinary Persians drank coffee in the sixteen hundreds. Some point to the travelers' many descriptions of busy coffee houses in Isfahan, and argue that coffee was already a common pleasure. Others point out that most of those descriptions come from the capital, and that in a provincial city like Yazd, and especially among women and in ordinary homes, cold sherbets and plain water were still the drinks of every day. The fairest view is probably that coffee belonged, in our year of 1670, mostly to the men in the bazaars of the great cities, and the sherbet belonged to everyone.

Now, sit down at supper, just you, beside the pool.

You are sitting cross-legged on a carpet spread on the brick floor of the courtyard, right at the edge of the long pool. The carpet is old and soft, its colors faded to rose and indigo and cream. The bricks under it still hold the warmth of the day. But the air around you is cooling now, and moving, and you can feel the difference on your arms.

In front of you, on a cloth, the supper is laid. A wide copper platter of rice, steaming, long-grained and white, each grain separate, mixed through with brown lentils and soft sweet raisins and little pieces of date, with a golden crust of crisped rice broken over the top. A bowl of yogurt. A dish of fresh herbs, mint and basil and tarragon and green onions, glistening from the pool water they were rinsed in. Bread. The ripe figs that Banu brought. And a bowl of cucumbers, cut into long pieces, sprinkled with salt.

You eat with your right hand, as everyone does, taking a little rice and pressing it gently together with your fingers. It is warm and sweet and nutty. The lentils are soft. The crust crackles.

The rice took Fatemeh most of the evening, and it was made the Persian way, which travelers from Europe often praised as the best rice they had ever eaten. First it was washed many times and soaked in salted water. Then it was boiled briefly in a large pot, only until the grains began to soften, and drained. Then it went back into the pot, with a little butter in the bottom, layered with the lentils and the raisins, and the lid was wrapped in a cloth and set on tight, and the pot was left over a very low fire to steam slowly in its own heat. That is why each grain stands apart from the next. And that is how the crust forms, golden and crisp, where the rice touches the bottom of the pot. In this family, as in most, the crust is the part everyone wants, and Fatemeh breaks it into pieces and shares it out with great fairness, so that there is no argument.

And then Fatemeh brings the sherbet.

She has a wide shallow bowl, and in it, shaved into a soft heap with a knife, is the ice that came to the door this morning, brought up at last from the cool room under the house. Over it she has poured the dark red syrup of sour cherries, and then cold water from the pool, and stirred it all together with a long spoon, so that the ice turns slowly in the red, clinking softly against the sides of the bowl. She ladles it into small cups and passes them around.

You hold yours in both hands. The cup is so cold it makes your fingers ache. You lift it to your lips, and the first sip is sharp and sweet and sour at once, and so cold that it seems to go straight through your chest and down into your stomach, like a clean blade of winter. You close your eyes. Somewhere outside, a dog barks. A lamp flickers. And for a long moment, there is nothing in the world but that cold sweet taste, and the soft clink of ice in a clay cup.

Around the cloth, the family eats in the gentle way of a family that has eaten together every evening of their lives.

Ostad Hossein has come back from the mosque, and he sits in the place of honor, with his back to the summer room, facing the pool. He eats slowly, and talks between mouthfuls, telling his wife what the men said after prayers. The mirab's runner has been seen asleep at the dam. The price of barley has gone up again. Somebody's cousin in Isfahan has written to say that the new Shah, Suleiman, spends too much time in his harem and not enough time on his horse. The old man reports all of this with great satisfaction, and Bibi Khadijeh listens and says, "Hm," at intervals, and refills his cup.

Ali Akbar has stopped weaving for the night. His hands are tired and his eyes are tired, and he sits with his sleeves rolled up and his legs stretched out in front of him and lets the evening air move over him. The crimson taffeta is nearly done. Another two days, he thinks, perhaps three.

Fatemeh's rice is praised, as it is every night, and she accepts the praise as no more than her due.

Mahmud eats an enormous amount of rice, very fast, as boys of sixteen tend to do, wherever they live.

Maryam eats carefully, with her left hand in her lap, where she can keep looking at the bright thread around her wrist.

And Reza has been given his cup of sherbet, his first of the summer, and he is holding it in both hands, staring into it, as if it might be magic.

"It is from the winter," he announces to everyone. "Grandfather said. This ice was water in a pool, in the winter, and the stars froze it, and then it waited in the dark all summer, and now it is here."

"That is exactly right," says his grandfather.

Reza drinks the whole cup in one long swallow, and then sits very still with a look of wonder and pain on his face, because the cold has gone straight to the middle of his forehead, the way it does. Everyone laughs. Even Pishi the cat, sitting at the edge of the carpet with her tail around her paws, seems to be smiling.

Fatemeh sits back at last, with her own cup of sherbet, and looks around at them all in the lamplight. She has been on her feet since half past four this morning. She baked nothing, because the baker did it, and she cooked only once, because hardly anyone in Yazd cooks twice in a July day. But she has carried water and swept and sprinkled, made yogurt and syrup, cut the melon, embroidered a hand's width of silk, and kept a household of seven cool through a day that reached a hundred and three degrees. She sips the sherbet. It is very cold and very good. She decides, privately, that she has earned it.

Overhead, the sky has gone from gold to a deep, clear violet, and the stars are coming out in earnest now, faster than you can count them. There is no moon tonight. The new moon is so close that its thin edge will not show at all, and so the stars will have the whole sky to themselves.

The lamps burn steady in their niches. The pool holds their light. The evening air moves softly across the carpet.

When the meal is over, the dishes are carried to the kitchen, and the cloth is folded. The rice that is left is covered and set aside for tomorrow. The last chips of ice in the bottom of the sherbet bowl have melted into a pale pink water, and Fatemeh pours it, carefully, onto the roots of the pomegranate tree, because in this house nothing that was once ice is ever wasted.

And Bibi Khadijeh takes the empty clay jar, the one where the ice was kept all day, and carries it back down the forty steps to its ledge in the cool room, where the stone pool glimmers under the last lamp, and the water from the mountains slides through it in the dark, quiet and cold, as the night comes on above.

## Part Ten — Around Nine at Night: Carrying the Beds to the Roof

By nine o'clock the sky over the courtyard is fully dark, crowded with stars, and the air in the open court has cooled at last to something that feels, after the long day, almost gentle.

But the rooms around the courtyard have not cooled. Step inside any of them now, the summer room, the weaving room, the long room where the family sleeps in winter, and the air there is thick and close and warm, warmer than the courtyard, warmer, it almost seems, than it was at noon. The walls are giving something back. The family knows exactly what that means, and nobody even considers sleeping indoors tonight.

Here is a small question for this part. If the family sleeps on the roof every summer night, why do they carry their beds up every evening and down again every morning, instead of simply leaving them there?

The answer is the sun. A cotton mattress left out on a Yazd roof through a July day would be baked as hot as a loaf of bread by noon, and it would still be hot when the family wanted to lie down on it at night. The sun would fade its colors and weaken its threads. The dust that the afternoon wind carries in from the desert would settle into it. And the sparrows and pigeons would make themselves at home on it. So each morning the bedding is rolled up and carried down into the shade of the house, and stacked in a cool corner of the summer room under a cloth. And each evening it is carried up again, to a roof that has had a few hours to cool since sunset.

Before anyone goes up, there is washing. One by one, the family kneel at the edge of the long pool in the courtyard and wash the day off their hands and faces and feet. The water in the pool, so cold this morning, is warm now after a day in the sun, but it still feels good on dusty skin. Fatemeh washes Reza herself, scrubbing his feet with her hands while he squirms, because a small boy who has spent the day crouching by pools and chasing frogs is not a small boy who should be allowed onto a clean mattress unwashed. Ostad Hossein washes slowly and carefully, as he does before every prayer, his lips moving silently.

The roof itself is a piece of work as careful as the walls. Under its smooth surface lie vaults of mud brick, or in some rooms, a layer of poplar poles laid side by side, then reed matting, then a thick bed of earth, then a skin of mud and straw plaster on top, sloped very slightly so that the rare winter rain runs off through spouts in the parapet. A flat mud roof needs looking after. Every autumn, before the winter rains, families in towns across Persia replastered their roofs with a fresh coat of mud and straw, and some kept a heavy stone roller up there, to roll the surface smooth and hard after each rain, so that the water would not soak in. Our family's roller stands in the corner by the parapet, a short cylinder of gray stone with a wooden handle, and Reza likes to sit on it.

That is why there is such a procession up the narrow stair tonight. Each person carries a roll. The rolls are mattresses, thin pads of cotton batting stitched inside covers of printed cloth, rolled tight and tied with a cord. With them come long round pillows, and light cotton sheets, and, for the old people, a thin quilt, because Bibi Khadijeh says the roof is always colder by the hour before dawn than anyone expects, and she is always right.

Here is our surprising detail, and it brings us back to the cat. Pishi is a long-haired gray cat, with a thick ruff at her neck and a plumed tail. Cats like her were known in Persia for a long time. And many people believe that the first cats like Pishi to reach Europe were carried there by Pietro della Valle, the Roman traveler who wrote home about coffee. When he finally sailed home to Italy, in the middle of the sixteen twenties, after years in Persia and India, he is thought to have carried with him a small group of long-haired Persian cats. Many people believe that the great family of long-haired cats now loved all over the world, the cats called Persians, began, at least in Europe, with those few animals of della Valle's.

So when Reza scoops up Pishi under one arm and carries her up the stairs to the roof, protesting, with her long gray tail hanging down over his elbow, he is carrying a creature that a traveler once thought precious enough to bring across half the world.

Our source for this part is della Valle again, and another of his discoveries. In the autumn of 1621, on his way from Isfahan toward the Persian Gulf, he stopped at the ruins of Persepolis, the great palace city of the ancient Persian kings, which lies in the southern mountains, in the country beyond Yazd toward Shiraz. Its columns and stairways had been standing in silence for nearly two thousand years. And on its stones he saw rows of strange marks, shaped like small wedges and nails, carved in neat lines. He copied a few of them carefully into his notebook. And he wrote, correctly, as later scholars would confirm, that this mysterious script was written from left to right. It was some of the first cuneiform writing ever to reach Europe. Scholars in Europe would not begin to read it until the early eighteen hundreds.

Historians do not all agree about the story of della Valle's cats. Some accept it as the beginning of long-haired cats in Europe. Others point out that the evidence is thin, a passing mention rather than a careful record, and that long-haired cats may have reached Europe at about the same time from Turkey as well, through the trading ports of the eastern Mediterranean. The fairest view is probably that della Valle was at least one of the people who carried them home, and that Pishi would not care in the slightest either way.

Now, climb up onto the roof, just you, in the dark.

You go up the narrow stair, built into the thickness of the wall, with your rolled mattress in your arms. The steps are worn hollow in the middle. The walls of the stairway are still warm, and the air in the stair is close and dusty. You climb, turning once, turning again, and then your head comes up through the opening into the open air, and the whole sky is there above you.

It is enormous. It stretches from one edge of the world to the other, black and deep and absolutely clear, and it is so full of stars that it seems almost crowded. Across the middle of it, from one horizon to the other, runs the long pale river of the Milky Way, faint and soft, like a trail of spilled flour, or of straw. The Persians called it the Kahkeshan, the path of the straw carrier, as though someone long ago had walked across the sky with an armful of straw and let some fall.

You step up onto the roof. The plaster under your bare feet is still warm, but only just. It has been giving up its heat to that clear black sky since sunset, and it is cooling steadily. You lay your mattress down along the parapet and unroll it, and smooth it flat with your hands.

All around you, across the city, you can see the other roofs, dark shapes at different heights, with the wind towers rising among them. And on many of those roofs, there are people. You can see the tiny glow of a lamp here and there, shaded by a hand. You can hear voices, low and murmuring, carrying through the still air. A mother hushing a child. Two men talking quietly about something, perhaps the price of barley. Someone laughing. Someone else saying the night prayer. A whole city, lying down to sleep under the stars.

Up on the roof, the family arranges itself. It is done the same way every night, and nobody has to say where anyone goes. The grandparents sleep at the north end of the roof, nearest the stair, with their mattresses side by side and the quilt folded at their feet. Ali Akbar and Fatemeh sleep along the eastern parapet. The children sleep in a row in the middle, Mahmud at one end, then Maryam, then Reza, closest to his mother.

There is a rule on the roofs of Yazd, unwritten but understood by everyone. When you are on your own roof at night, you do not look onto your neighbor's. The parapets are low, and the roofs run together, and on the hottest nights, half the city is sleeping within sight of the other half. So you keep your eyes on your own family, and on the sky, and your neighbors do the same, and everyone has as much privacy as if there were walls between them.

Bibi Khadijeh shakes out the quilt and lays it across her husband's feet, though he says he does not need it. She says he will need it by morning. They have had this conversation on this roof every summer night for forty-five years, and they both know how it ends. By the hour before dawn, when the roof has given up the last of its warmth to the sky and a faint breath of air comes down off the mountains, he will reach down in his sleep and pull the quilt up to his chin, and she will see him do it, and say nothing, and smile.

The voices on the other roofs are growing fewer and softer. A lamp goes out, two roofs away. Then another, farther off.

Mahmud has carried his grandfather's mattress up as well as his own, and he unrolls it for the old man and smooths it flat, and helps him lower himself onto it, knee by knee. Ostad Hossein lies back with a long breath and looks up at the stars.

"There," he says. "That is better."

Reza lies down beside him for a moment, on the warm plaster, and the old man lifts one arm and points straight up.

"That one," he says. "The bright one, almost over our heads. Do you see it."

Reza sees it. It is a blue-white star, the brightest in that part of the sky, very nearly at the top of the world.

The stars over Persia had been watched and named and drawn for a very long time. Seven hundred years before this night, in the year 964, a Persian astronomer named Abd al-Rahman al-Sufi, who worked for the rulers of Isfahan and Shiraz, finished a great illustrated work called The Book of the Fixed Stars. In it he drew every constellation, twice, once as it looked in the sky and once as it would look on the surface of a globe, and he listed the stars in each one, with their brightness and their positions and their names. Many of the star names we still use today, Vega, Altair, Deneb, came to Europe through books like his. The bright blue-white star above Ostad Hossein's finger is Vega, which in summer passes almost directly over the roofs of Yazd.

And al-Sufi noticed something else. In the constellation we call Andromeda, he wrote of a small, faint patch of light, which he called a little cloud. It was the first written description we have of what we now know is another galaxy, a whole vast island of stars, so far away that its light has been traveling for millions of years. Later tonight, after midnight, it will rise high enough in the east to see, a soft smudge of light, if you know where to look. Ostad Hossein does not know about galaxies. But his own grandfather showed him that little cloud, on this same roof, when he was a boy.

"Later," he tells Reza, "I will show you a cloud that is not a cloud. But you must stay awake."

Reza swears he will stay awake.

Maryam unrolls her mattress next to Reza's. She has been very quiet this evening, and every so often she looks at the colored thread on her wrist, though it is too dark now to see its colors.

Fatemeh has brought up one of the sweating clay jars from the cool room, filled with water, and a single cup, and set them in the corner of the roof, where anyone who wakes thirsty in the night can find them. The outside of the jar is already damp, and cool to the touch.

Reza has put Pishi down on his mattress, and Pishi has turned around three times, considered the situation, and then walked off along the top of the parapet, very slowly, tail high, to begin her nightly inspection of the neighborhood's roofs. She will be back. She always is.

Somewhere below, in the lanes, the night watchman is beginning his round. In the towns of Safavid Persia, the bazaars and streets at night belonged to the watch, men who walked the dark lanes with a lamp and a staff, calling out at intervals so that honest people would know they were there, and others would know it too. You can hear him now, faint and far off, a single long call, and the knock of his staff on a shuttered door. It is a comforting sound. It means the city is being looked after while it sleeps.

Ali Akbar stands for a while at the edge of the roof, looking out over the dark city, with his hands on the warm parapet. The day's work is done. The taffeta is almost finished. The air is cool on his face. Far away, on the edge of the city, a dog barks once and falls silent.

And Bibi Khadijeh, last of all, does what she does every night of the year. Before she climbs the stair to the roof, she goes down the other stair, the forty steps into the earth, with a lamp in her hand. At the bottom, the cool room is dark and still and smells of water. She looks around it once. The clay jars stand on their ledges. The empty ice jar sits in its bed of sand. And the stone pool lies there in the circle of her lamplight, with the water sliding through it, smooth and dark and silent, in from the mouth in the wall and out again, as it has done all day, and all the days before.

She blows out the lamp. In the darkness, the water goes on, and she climbs back up toward the stars.

## Part Eleven — Toward Midnight: Sleeping Under the Desert Stars

By eleven o'clock the plaster of the roof has lost the last of its warmth, and when you lay your palm flat on it beside your mattress, it feels cool and smooth, like the skin of a melon taken from the pool.

Here is a small question for this part, and it is very nearly our last. Why is it so much cooler up here than in the rooms below?

Think back over the whole long day. All morning and all afternoon, the sun beat on the thick earth walls of the house, and the heat crept into them slowly, inch by inch. Now, hours later, that stored heat has finally worked its way through to the inside, and the rooms below are full of it. The walls are giving back the noon. That is exactly what they were built to do. But it means that, for the first half of the night, the inside of the house is the warmest place in it.

The roof is different. Up here, there is nothing between you and the sky. And the clear, dry desert sky, the same sky that froze the water in the ice pools last January, is drawing the warmth out of everything beneath it, the plaster, the mattresses, the sleepers themselves, and sending it away into the dark. Up here, too, any breath of moving air reaches you, passing over the tops of the walls, where in the lanes below it would be blocked. And in a city as dry as Yazd, there are very few biting insects to trouble a sleeper in the open.

Historians do not all agree about how universal this was. Some travelers wrote as if all of Persia slept on its roofs in summer. Others noted that in the grand houses and gardens, families often slept instead on raised wooden platforms beside a pool, under the trees, and that in the cooler mountain towns many people stayed indoors. The fairest view is probably that, in the hot plain towns like Yazd, the roof was where most ordinary families went, on most of the summer nights, for as long as anyone could remember.

So here, at last, is the answer to our third question. Why does the whole family sleep on the roof? Because after a desert day, the rooms below are full of the heat that the walls soaked up at noon, while the roof lies open to a clear night sky that pulls the warmth away, and to whatever breeze is moving over the city. The house keeps them cool by day, and the sky keeps them cool by night. And between the two, they have found a way to live comfortably through the hottest summer in Persia.

And now there is nothing left to wonder about. There is only the night.

The voices on the other roofs have fallen silent. Across the whole city, the lamps have gone out, one by one, until there is no light anywhere except the stars. And there are so many of them. They fill the sky from the black line of the parapet all the way up and over and down to the parapet on the other side. The Milky Way lies across the middle of them like a soft scarf, pale and faint and endless.

You are lying on your back on a thin cotton mattress, on a cool roof, under all of it.

The sheet is light across your legs. The pillow under your head is round and firm and smells faintly of the cupboard where it lay all day. The air on your face is cool now, truly cool, for the first time since this morning, and it is moving, very gently, a slow soft breath coming down off the far mountains and passing over the roof and away.

You can hear the city breathing around you. Somewhere, very far off, a donkey shifts in its stall. A dog sighs. Someone on a roof two houses away turns over in their sleep, and their mattress rustles, and then it is quiet again.

And if you listen carefully, beneath all of it, you can hear something else. Not with your ears, perhaps. More with the stillness of your body. Far below you, down through the roof and the rooms and the floor of the courtyard, down the forty steps into the earth, the water is still moving. It is moving through the stone pool in the dark, cool and quiet and steady, as it has moved all day, as it will move all night.

Let the whole long day come back to you now, slowly, the way a day comes back when you are almost asleep.

The gray light on the roof before dawn, and the cool plaster under your hand. The water turning in the lane, glinting, pouring into the courtyard pool. The warm bread wrapped in a cloth. The old man's voice in the summer room, telling of the tunnels, and the little lamp in the dark. The knock at the door, and the long call of the ice seller, and the cold shard of winter melting in your palm.

The forty steps down into the earth, and the cool rising to meet you. The melon turning slowly in the pool. The fan moving, and the water murmuring, and the long afternoon passing over the house like weather passing over a ship. The tower breathing. The sprinkled lane, and the smell of wet earth. The bright thread on a girl's wrist. The red sherbet, clinking with ice, in a clay cup.

All of it is behind you now. All of it is done. There is nothing more you need to carry, and nothing more you need to remember. You can let it go, the way the roof lets go of its warmth, a little at a time, up into the dark.

Your arms are heavy. Your legs are heavy. The mattress holds you. The air moves over you, cool and soft, and you breathe it in, slowly, and let it out again, slowly, the way the house breathes through its tower.

Reza is the first to fall asleep. He lasted a little longer than anyone thought he would. He lay on his back with his eyes wide open, staring at the sky, determined to see the cloud that is not a cloud. He asked twice whether it was time yet, and twice his grandfather said, "Not yet. Soon." And somewhere between the second asking and the third, his eyes closed, and his mouth fell slightly open, and his breathing slowed. Pishi, back from her rounds, stepped delicately over him and curled herself into the crook of his arm, exactly where she was at dawn this morning, as if the whole long day had been only a circle, coming back to where it began.

His face is soft and peaceful in the starlight, and one hand lies open on the pillow beside him.

Fatemeh reaches over and draws the sheet up over his shoulders. He does not stir.

Maryam is next. She lies on her side, facing her little brother, with her hand tucked under her cheek, the hand with the bright thread around its wrist. Her eyes are half closed. She is thinking about what she will wish for, when it is time to give the thread to the wind. She has not decided yet. She thinks perhaps she will wish for a loom of her own. Or for a cat like Pishi. Or for nothing at all, just for the summer to go on being exactly like this. She is still deciding when she falls asleep.

Mahmud lies with his arms behind his head, as his father did in the cool room at noon, looking up at the stars. He is thinking about Isfahan. About the great square, and the palace, and the bazaar that goes on for a mile under its roofs, and the crimson taffeta folded in a merchant's pack, traveling there by night under these same stars. And he is thinking, too, though he would never say so, about the tunnels. About the cold water running in the dark, and the little flame of the lamp, and his grandfather walking beside it at fourteen. His thoughts grow slower, and softer, and turn into something else, and then he too is asleep.

Ali Akbar and Fatemeh lie side by side along the eastern parapet, not talking. They do not need to. They have had a long day, and a good one, and the night is cool, and the children are asleep. After a while Fatemeh's hand finds her husband's on the mattress between them, and rests there. That is all. And soon her breathing deepens, and so does his.

Far away, down in the lanes, the night watchman calls once more, a long, low, drawn-out call, faint with distance. Then the knock of his staff on a door, and the soft fall of his footsteps, moving away. And then nothing. Only the stillness, and the stars.

A small breath of wind comes over the parapet, and stirs the edge of a sheet, and lifts a strand of Fatemeh's hair, and moves on across the roof, and over the next roof, and the next, across the whole sleeping city, touching every sleeper, one by one, the way a mother checks on her children in the night.

Ali Akbar is the last of the younger ones to sleep. For a while he lies with his eyes open, looking up, and in the stars above him he sees, without meaning to, the pattern of a cloth, a dark blue silk scattered with small gold flowers, the kind his wife embroiders in the cool room at noon. He thinks he might weave a pattern like that one day, when the crimson is done. He thinks of the shuttle passing through the threads, back and forth, back and forth, steady and slow. And the thought of it rocks him, gently, the way the loom has always rocked him, until his eyes close and he is gone.

The roof is very quiet now. Five sleepers now, breathing slow and soft, each in their own rhythm, and the sound of their breathing so gentle that you can only hear it if you lie very still. A sheet rustles. A small hand moves on a pillow. The cat's tail twitches once, and settles.

Only the old people are still awake. They lie side by side at the north end of the roof, near the head of the stair, looking up.

"There," whispers Ostad Hossein after a while. "There it is."

Low in the east, above the dark line of the mountains, a faint, soft patch of light has risen into view. It is so faint that you can only see it if you look a little to one side of it, and then it is there, a pale smudge, like a breath on a cold window. It is the little cloud that al-Sufi wrote of, more than seven hundred years ago, in his Book of the Fixed Stars.

"He missed it," says Bibi Khadijeh softly, looking at Reza, asleep.

"Tomorrow," says the old man. "It will be there tomorrow. It has been there a long time."

They watch it for a while together, the old qanat digger and the keeper of the water. Then Bibi Khadijeh reaches down and pulls the quilt a little higher over her husband's knees, though he does not need it yet, and he lets her.

The little cloud hangs there, soft and patient, above the mountains. Below it, the dark line of the hills. Below that, the plain, and the gardens, and the long rows of shafts running out toward the hills, and beneath them, in the dark, the water, coming home.

For a long time they say nothing at all. Then Bibi Khadijeh speaks, very softly, so as not to wake the children.

"It was a good day," she says.

"It was a hot day," says her husband.

"It was a good day," she says again. "Everybody ate. Everybody drank. Nobody was ill. The water came on time. The silk is almost done. Maryam has a new thread, and Reza learned where the water comes from." She pauses. "And you told your story."

The old man is quiet for a while.

"Yes," he says at last. "I told my story."

She can hear, in his voice, that he is smiling in the dark. She closes her eyes.

Bibi Khadijeh lies awake a little longer, as she always does, listening. She listens to the children breathing, and to her son and his wife, and to her husband beside her, until she is sure that every one of them is asleep. It is the last task of her day, and in a way the most important. Then she lets her own breath slow to match theirs, and the stars blur softly above her, and the roof seems to rise and fall, very gently, like a boat on still water.

The breeze from the mountains is cooler now. The plaster under the mattresses is cool. The stars turn slowly overhead, so slowly that you cannot see them move, only notice, after a long while, that they are not quite where they were.

And far beneath it all, at the bottom of the forty steps, the stone pool lies in perfect darkness, and the water slips through it, cool and clear and quiet, carrying the last of the day's heat gently away under the sleeping city.

## Part Twelve — The Small Hours Toward First Light: The Water Under the House

Midnight passes over Yazd without a sound, and then one o'clock, and then two, and the stars wheel slowly westward over the sleeping roofs.

Here is one last small question, a gentle one. In the deepest part of the night, who is still awake in this house?

The answer is almost nobody. Not the children, curled in a row in the middle of the roof. Not their parents along the eastern parapet. Not the old people at the head of the stair. Even Pishi has given up her rounds and lies tucked against Reza's arm, her eyes closed, her ears turning now and then toward some faint sound that only a cat could hear, and then going still again.

The only thing awake in this house is the water.

Down at the bottom of the forty steps, in the dark of the cool room, it is doing exactly what it did this morning, and this afternoon, and all the evening. It comes in from the black mouth in the wall, crosses the stone pool, and slips out again on the far side, on its long, slow way through the city to the gardens beyond. It does not hurry. It does not rest. It came from the snow on the mountains, through the gravel at their feet, into the mother well, and along the dark tunnels that men like Ostad Hossein dug and cleaned for generations, and it will go on coming, all night, while everyone sleeps.

Somewhere in the city, the mirab's night turn is passing too. A copper bowl is slowly filling in a basin, and sinking, and being lifted out, and set floating again. A pebble drops into a jar. The water turns from one channel to the next, from one garden to another, measured out in the dark, so that every share is honored, even at this hour, even with nobody watching but the stars.

And far out beyond the gardens, at the edge of the city, the great dome of the ice house stands silent and dark against the sky. Deep inside it, under its thick walls, the last of last winter's ice lies waiting in the cool, packed in its straw. In the morning, before dawn, the ice seller and his patient donkey will go out to it again, and the long call of yakh will drift down the lanes once more. But not yet. For now, the ice sleeps too.

Below the roof, the house lies quiet in the dark, room by room.

The summer room is empty, its rugs swept, its curtains hanging still. The little pool in its floor holds a single star, reflected in the water from the square of sky at the top of the tower. The tower itself stands silent above it. There is almost no wind now, and the air inside it hangs at rest, cool and dark, the brick fins dim, the pigeons asleep on their ledge.

The weaving room smells of silk and wood. The loom stands in the dark with the crimson taffeta stretched across it, nearly finished, the shuttle resting where Ali Akbar laid it down. Tomorrow, in the cool of the early morning, the knock and swish will begin again. But tonight the threads are still.

The kitchen is dark, its little charcoal fire long since dead and cold. The rice from supper waits under its cover. The jar of sour cherry syrup stands on its shelf, a little lower than it was this morning. The bottle of rosewater stands beside it.

In the courtyard, the long pool lies perfectly still, and the whole sky is in it. Every star above the roof is down there too, floating on the dark water, so clear that a small frog sitting on the rim might think it could jump into the heavens. The pomegranate tree stands at the end of the pool with its leaves hanging quiet, its small green fruit growing, very slowly, in the dark, toward the autumn.

And the street door is shut and barred, with its two knockers hanging silent, the heavy one and the light one, waiting for the morning.

Marco Polo, nearly four hundred years ago, called this a good and noble city. Tonight, under its stars, it is something simpler than that. It is a quiet one.

You are lying on your mattress on the roof, in the deepest, coolest hour of the night.

The plaster beneath you is cool now, all the way through. The sheet over you is cool. The air on your face is soft, and faintly fresh, with the smell of the far mountains in it, and of dust, and of the fig leaves in the next courtyard.

You do not need to do anything. You do not need to think about anything. The day is done, and the next day has not begun, and in between there is only this, the cool, and the quiet, and the stars.

Your breathing is slow. Your body is heavy and loose and warm, the right kind of warm, the kind that comes from being under a light sheet on a cool night. Your thoughts drift apart like the last voices on the rooftops, and fade, one by one, and fall silent.

Let yourself sink a little deeper into the mattress. Let your hands rest open, palms up, on the cool sheet. Feel how heavy they are. Feel how quiet the night is, all around you, above you and below you, out to the walls of the city and the gardens and the dark desert beyond.

The stars are there. The cool is there. The water is there, far below. Everything that needs to be kept is being kept. Everything that needs to be cool is cool.

You can rest.

Around three o'clock, the breeze off the mountains grows a little cooler, just as Bibi Khadijeh said it would. And on his mattress at the head of the stair, Ostad Hossein, fast asleep, reaches down without waking and draws the quilt up to his chin. Beside him, his wife opens her eyes just long enough to see him do it. She smiles. She says nothing. She closes her eyes again.

Fatemeh turns over in her sleep and puts out a hand toward the children, and finds Reza's foot, and rests her hand on it, and goes on sleeping.

Mahmud mutters something about bobbins, very indistinctly, and is silent. His arm slides off his eyes and comes to rest on the mattress beside him, open and loose, and he sleeps on.

Maryam sleeps with her wrist under her cheek, and the thread still on it.

The stars move on. The Milky Way has swung slowly across the sky. The little cloud that al-Sufi saw is high in the east now, far above the mountains, a soft faint smudge among the stars, and nobody on the roof is awake to see it, and that is all right, because it will be there tomorrow, and the night after, and long after that.

Four o'clock comes. And then, very slowly, the sky over the eastern mountains begins to change. It does not grow light. Not yet. It simply grows a little less dark, the black softening into the deepest blue, the faint stars near the horizon fading out one by one, so gently that you could not say when any of them went.

On the other roofs, across the city, the other families sleep on too. The ice seller, asleep on his own roof beside his donkey's stall. The mirab's runner, curled at the foot of his master's bench, his spade beside him. Banu, next door, asleep among her grandchildren, the one with the cough sleeping easily now in the cool. The old man who sat on his stool at the corner, the baker by his cold oven, the coppersmiths, the weavers, the water sellers with their leather bags hung up on a peg. Thousands of them, on thousands of roofs, all under the same sky, all breathing the same cool air, all waiting, without knowing it, for the same gray light.

The mountains to the south are only a dark shape now, a little darker than the sky above them. Up there, on the high slopes, the last of the spring snow has long since melted and sunk into the gravel, and is moving, slowly, downhill, through the stones, toward the mother wells. Some of it will reach this city next summer, or the summer after. Some of it, perhaps, will reach the stone pool under this very house, and a child not yet born will put her hand into it and feel how cold it is.

The breeze has almost stopped. The air lies still and cool over the roofs. Somewhere, a single bird begins to sing, very quietly, a few notes, and stops, as if it had spoken too early and was embarrassed.

This is the hour when the whole long circle began, yesterday morning. In a little while, Bibi Khadijeh will wake, the first of them, as she always does, and fold her mattress, and go down the forty steps with her lamp to look at the water. And then the day will begin again, with its heat and its work and its long bright hours, and the family will meet it with all their old tricks, the thick walls and the deep room, the tower and the pool and the ice.

But not yet. Not quite yet. For now, there is only the cool of the last hour of the night, and the soft gray coming into the sky, and everyone asleep.

The gray grows a little paler. The tops of the wind towers begin to show against it, faint and soft, square shapes rising out of the dark city like the masts of ships in a quiet harbor. The parapet of the roof takes shape. The rolled quilt at the old man's feet. The round pillows. The jar of water in the corner, its sides still damp and cool.

There is no sound at all now. Not a voice, not a footstep, not a breath of wind. Only the deep, soft quiet of the very end of the night, when the whole world seems to be holding still, resting, gathering itself for the day.

And in that quiet, if you lie very still, you can almost hear it once more. Far below. Down through the roof, and the rooms, and the courtyard floor, and the forty steps into the cool earth. The water, moving.

Here they all are, then, one last time, asleep on the roof under the fading stars.

Ostad Hossein, the old qanat digger, sixty-nine years old, asleep with the quilt drawn up to his white beard, dreaming, perhaps, of a little flame in a long dark tunnel, and cold water running past his feet.

Bibi Khadijeh, the keeper of the water, asleep beside him, close to the head of the stair, where she will be the first to wake.

Ali Akbar, the silk weaver, asleep on his back, his clever hands quiet at last, with a length of crimson taffeta waiting on the loom below.

Fatemeh, who carried and swept and sprinkled and cooked and kept them all cool, asleep with her hand resting on her youngest son's foot.

Mahmud, sixteen, asleep with one arm flung over his eyes, dreaming of Isfahan, or of the tunnels, or of both.

Maryam, eleven, asleep with her cheek on her wrist, and a thread of four bright colors waiting there for the wind.

Reza, six, asleep in a tangle of sheet, with his mouth a little open, and the cat curled warm in the crook of his arm.

And Pishi, the long-haired gray cat, asleep with her tail across her nose, her ears twitching now and then, dreaming of sparrows and frogs.

Seven people and one gray cat, on one flat roof, in one desert city, on one summer night, all of them sleeping deeply and well.

All around them, across the whole sleeping city, the wind towers stand quiet against the paling sky. The roofs are cool. The lanes are still. The pools lie dark in every courtyard.

And far below them, at the bottom of the forty steps, in the cool room under the house, the stone pool lies in the dark. The water from the mountains comes in from the black mouth in the wall, smooth and cold and clear, crosses the pool with a soft, endless murmur, and slips away again, under the city, toward the gardens. It has flowed all through the day and all through the night. It will be flowing when Bibi Khadijeh comes down with her lamp. It is cool, and it is quiet, and it is keeping the whole house safe.
