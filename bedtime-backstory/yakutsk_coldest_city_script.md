The sun has barely lifted itself above the larch trees all day, and now it hangs low and red, as if it is tired. The snow squeaks under every step. Your breath leaves your mouth as a white cloud and falls, almost at once, as a glitter of frost on your collar. And from one low log house at the edge of a frozen meadow, a single thread of smoke rises perfectly straight into the still air, because inside, an open clay fireplace is burning, and it has not been allowed to go out since the autumn.

Now let your head grow heavy on the pillow. Let your shoulders loosen and drop, and let your eyes close whenever they like, because there is nothing you need to watch. We will go back to that low red sun and that thread of smoke together. So let us begin.

The house stands on a slight rise above a meadow, with a lake beyond it that froze solid back in October. The forest begins just past the lake, larch and pine, dark against the snow. There is no road, only a packed track worn by sledges and hooves, leading off toward the town. Yakutsk itself is out of sight, behind the trees, a few hours away by ox sledge.

The family who live here are Sakha. That is their own name for themselves. The Russians, who arrived in this valley in the sixteen hundreds and built a fort on the bank of the great Lena River, called them Yakuts, and the town took that name. But in their own language, around their own fire, they are Sakha, and the land is their land.

The town, in 1885, is not large. It is a place of wooden houses and wooden sidewalks, of onion-domed churches, government offices, and the warehouses of fur merchants, home to a few thousand people. Along its streets you would hear Russian and Sakha, and very often a mixture of both, because many Russians who settled here learned to speak Sakha at home. In winter the town sits under a low haze of its own smoke and frozen breath, so that from the hills around it, it seems to be sinking slowly into a white cloud. Out in the countryside, though, where most Sakha families lived, there was no town at all. There were scattered farmsteads, one or two or three houses at the edge of a meadow, each one far enough from its neighbors that the cattle had room to graze in summer and the hay had room to grow.

This family never quite existed under one name, but every detail of their night is real.

There are seven of them under this roof. Kuolai is the grandfather, seventy years old, with a thin white beard and hands that still carve harness pieces from birch. Everyone in the district knows him as a teller of the old sung stories. His wife Akulina is sixty-four. She is the keeper of the fire, and in this house that is not a small title. Their son Uibaan is forty-one, a cattle man and a hay cutter, broad in the shoulders, quiet, and slow to smile. His wife Maaria is thirty-six. She milks, she makes the butter, and she sews the furs that keep all seven of them alive. Their eldest, Byötür, is sixteen, and he looks after the horses. Daaria is twelve, and she has taken charge of the calves this winter, which she is very proud of. And little Miitere is seven, and his main job, as far as he is concerned, is to ask questions.

Like most Sakha families by this time, they were baptized in the Orthodox Church, so their names are Russian church names, Nikolai, Ivan, Maria, Pyotr, Daria, Dmitri, said the Sakha way.

Here is a small question to begin with, and we will answer it before this part is over. Just how cold is it outside that door, on an ordinary January afternoon?

Let us start with what everyone in this valley knew. The middle Lena valley, where Yakutsk sits, is one of the coldest inhabited places on the planet. It is a long way from any ocean, so no sea air softens the winter. In January the great high-pressure air of inner Siberia settles over the land like a lid, and the sky goes clear and pale, and the heat of the earth leaks away into space night after night. There is very little wind. The cold simply sinks and pools in the valley, the way water pools in the bottom of a bowl.

The days are short. Today the sun rose at a little before nine in the morning, and it will set at about twenty-five minutes past three in the afternoon. In between, it never climbs high. It slides along just above the southern trees, throwing long blue shadows across the snow, so that even at noon the light looks like evening light anywhere else.

And yet people here had been living with this for centuries. They were not visitors to the cold. They were farmers of it. The Sakha are a Turkic-speaking people whose ancestors, long ago, came north down the Lena from lands farther south, bringing with them cattle and horses. In a place where the reindeer herders and hunters of the forest lived by moving, the Sakha settled down with cows. That is the strange and wonderful thing about this house. It is a cattle farm, very near the coldest corner of the Northern Hemisphere.

So, the answer to our small question. In the decades around our night, the average temperature in Yakutsk in January was about fifty degrees below zero on the Fahrenheit scale. That is not the coldest night of the year. That is the ordinary middle of the month, day and night together.

Here is a surprising detail. At these temperatures, the two scales most of the world uses for measuring cold come together. Forty below zero Fahrenheit is exactly the same as forty below zero Celsius. Below that line, the numbers drift apart again, but by then everyone in this valley has stopped caring which scale they use. They measure the cold in other ways. By how the snow sounds underfoot. By how fast spit freezes. By whether the smoke from the chimney stands up straight or lies down.

And there is a record. Six winters from now, on the fifth of February in the year 1891, the thermometer at Yakutsk will read nearly eighty-four degrees below zero Fahrenheit, about minus sixty-four Celsius. It remains the coldest temperature ever measured in the city. Nobody in our house knows that yet, of course. To them, February is simply the month when the trees crack in the forest at night like rifle shots, and the old people tell the children to stay close to the fire.

Our first real witness tonight is a young Polish man named Wacław Sieroszewski. He was a political prisoner. He had been arrested in Warsaw in the late eighteen seventies for his part in the socialist movement, and in 1880 the Russian government sent him into exile in the Yakutsk region. He would stay twelve years. He learned the Sakha language. He married a Sakha woman. He lived in houses very much like this one, through winter after winter, and he wrote down everything he saw.

In 1896 his great book was published in Saint Petersburg, in Russian, under the plain title Yakuty, which simply means The Yakuts. It was a thick, careful study of how the Sakha lived, their houses, their cattle, their food, their songs, their beliefs. It won a gold medal from the Imperial Russian Geographical Society. Much of what we know about a night like this comes from that book, and from the people who told him their lives.

Now let us go outside for a moment, just you, in the last of the afternoon.

You step out of the low doorway and the cold meets you like a hand pressed flat against your face. It does not feel like the cold you know. It feels dry, and enormous, and very still. The inside of your nose prickles as the moisture there begins to freeze. Each breath you take is sharp at the back of your throat, so you learn quickly to breathe through a fold of fur, slowly, through your nose.

Around you the world is blue and gold. The low sun lays a strip of copper light across the meadow, and every crystal on the snow catches it. Behind you, the house is a long, low shape, almost like a part of the hillside, its walls thick and gray with plaster, its flat roof heaped with snow. Smoke climbs from its chimney in one unbroken column, straight up, higher than the trees, before it thins out and disappears.

You listen. There is almost nothing to hear. No wind in the trees. No birds. Far off, a dog barks once, and the sound carries across the lake so clearly that it might be standing beside you. Your boots squeak and crunch on the hard snow, and that squeak is the loudest sound in the world.

You pull off one mitten to touch the door frame, and you feel the warmth leave your fingers almost at once. You put the mitten back on quickly. Your eyelashes have begun to feel heavy, and when you blink, they stick together for a moment, gently, before they part.

Today, in the same city of Yakutsk, people still live through these winters, but they do it with central heating, insulated apartment blocks raised on piles above the frozen ground, and cars left running in parking lots so their engines will not freeze. Our family has none of that. They have wood, clay, fur, butter, cattle, and an old, deep knowledge of how heat behaves.

Historians and geographers do not all agree on what to call this place. Some say that Yakutsk is fairly called the coldest city on earth, because no other city of its size has winters this harsh. Others point out that smaller settlements farther north and east, such as Verkhoyansk and Oymyakon, have recorded colder temperatures still. Both views are fair. Verkhoyansk and Oymyakon are colder, but they are villages and small towns. Yakutsk is often described as the largest city in the world built on ground that stays frozen all year.

Inside the house, Akulina is crouching in front of the fireplace, feeding it. She does not throw the wood in carelessly. She stands each split length of larch upright against the back of the hearth, on its end, so that the flames climb it from below. That is how a Sakha fire is built. The logs stand like people warming themselves, leaning together.

Miitere is sitting on the low bench beside her with his chin on his knees, watching.

"Grandmother," he says, "why does the fire never go to sleep?"

"Because if it went to sleep," she says, "we would all go to sleep with it, and we would not wake up."

She says it lightly, the way old women say serious things to small boys, and she reaches over and pinches his nose, which he hates and loves.

At the other end of the room, Maaria is sitting cross-legged on a bench with a coat of foal skin across her lap, stitching a new strip of fur along the hem with a steel needle and a thread of twisted sinew. Daaria is beside her, sorting the fur scraps by color, the dark ones in one pile, the pale ones in another. Kuolai is in the place of honor, on the bench along the far wall, opposite the door, with a piece of birch in one hand and a short knife in the other, shaving off thin curls of wood that drop into his lap.

Through the wall at the back, behind a low door, there is a soft heavy shuffling, and a long, contented breath, the sound of a cow shifting her weight in her stall. Nobody looks up. It is the most ordinary sound in the world to them.

Outside, the sun touches the tops of the trees. Uibaan and Byötür will be back soon from the hay stack in the far meadow with a load on the ox sledge, and then the evening will truly begin.

Akulina sets one more log on its end in the mouth of the fireplace, and the flames lean into it, gold and steady.

## Part Two — Around Twenty-Five Past Three: The Sun Goes Down

The sun slips below the trees at about twenty-five minutes past three, and for a while afterward the whole sky in the south glows a soft rose color, fading upward into a deep and spotless blue.

Across the meadow, a dark shape is moving slowly toward the house. It is an ox, white with frost from its horns to its tail, pulling a low wooden sledge piled high with hay. Walking beside it, wrapped in furs so thick that they look like two small haystacks themselves, are Uibaan and his son Byötür.

Here is a small question for this part. Why would a family haul their hay home in the middle of winter, in the dark and the cold, instead of bringing it all in at harvest time when the days were long and warm?

To answer that, we need to understand what hay meant to a Sakha family. Everything in this household depended on cattle, and cattle in this country cannot graze for most of the year. From the first heavy snows in October until the grass comes back in late May, the cows must be fed by hand, every single day, from what the family cut and dried the summer before. A Sakha year, in a sense, was a long race between two seasons. The short, hot summer was for growing and cutting hay. The long winter was for feeding it out, armful by armful, and hoping it would last.

Haymaking happened in July and August, when the days here are long and bright and surprisingly hot. The whole family went out to the meadows with scythes and wooden rakes. Men, women and older children cut and turned and gathered the grass, and they built it into great stacks, out in the meadows where it grew, fenced around with poles to keep the horses and the deer away. A good hay meadow was precious. Families knew their meadows the way farmers elsewhere knew their fields, and they argued over them, and passed them down.

Many of the best meadows in this country lie in round, shallow hollows called alaas. They are one of the most beautiful features of the middle Lena valley. Over thousands of years, patches of the frozen ground beneath the forest slowly thawed and sank, leaving wide grassy basins, often with a small lake at the center, ringed by a wall of dark larch trees. To a Sakha family, an alaas was home in the deepest sense. The grass in the basin fed the cattle, the lake gave fish and ice, and the forest around the rim gave wood. Our family's house stands at the edge of just such a hollow, which is why the lake lies so close and the forest stands all around.

The Sakha also moved house twice a year, though only a short distance. In summer, families lived at a summer place, with a lighter dwelling near the summer pastures. In autumn, when the hay was in and the frost came, they moved back to the winter house, nearer the hay stacks and the woodpile, and plastered it fresh for the cold. This house, then, is a winter house. It was built for exactly this.

So the answer to our small question is this. In summer, the meadows are often soft, wet, cut through with streams and marshy ground. Hauling hay across them would be slow and muddy work. But in winter, everything freezes hard, and the snow lays a smooth white road over every bog and stream. A sledge glides easily where a cart would sink. So the hay is left standing in the meadows until the cold comes, and then it is brought home one load at a time, all winter long, as it is needed.

Here is a surprising detail. The animal pulling that sledge is an ox, and not a horse. The Sakha were famous horse breeders. They loved their horses, sang about them, and drank their mares' fermented milk at the great summer festival. But in winter, the horses here are mostly left to look after themselves. They stay out in the open, in all weather, and dig down through the snow with their hooves to find the dry grass underneath. These small, shaggy Sakha horses can live outdoors in temperatures that would kill most horses on earth, around seventy-five degrees below zero Fahrenheit. The cows, the precious milk cows, are kept inside and fed. And it is the patient ox who does the heavy pulling of hay and ice and wood across the winter snow.

When Wacław Sieroszewski first arrived in this country in 1880, he was not sent to Yakutsk at all. He was sent much farther north, to the small town of Verkhoyansk, deep in the mountains near the Arctic Circle. It was a place so remote that the government used it as a kind of prison without walls. Two years later he tried to escape from there, and failed.

Verkhoyansk would soon become famous for a different reason. In February of the year 1892, the thermometer there fell to about minus ninety degrees Fahrenheit, roughly minus sixty-eight Celsius. For a long time, it was accepted as the lowest temperature ever recorded in the Northern Hemisphere. That reading is still shared, as a record, with the village of Oymyakon, which measured almost exactly the same cold in 1933. Our family's valley is not quite so harsh as that. But it is close enough that the people here would have understood, perfectly well, what life in Verkhoyansk was like.

Now, let us walk with the men for the last stretch home.

You are walking beside the ox, one hand resting on the side of the sledge. The hay smells faintly of summer, even now, a dry sweet smell that seems out of place in this frozen world. The ox breathes heavily through its nose, and each breath makes a great white plume that drifts back over its shoulders and settles as frost on its hide. Its eyelashes are white. Long icicles hang from the hair around its mouth.

Your own face is wrapped up to the eyes. Over your mouth you have a fold of fur, and where your breath passes through it, a little crust of ice has formed. When you speak, your voice sounds muffled and strange, so you do not speak much. Neither does anyone here, outdoors in winter. Words are for inside, by the fire.

The snow under your boots is so cold that it no longer feels like snow. It is dry and gritty, like sand, and it squeaks with every step. Your boots are high, soft boots of reindeer skin, with the fur turned outward, and inside them your feet are wrapped in layers of soft hair and felt. They are warm. Your hands, inside long fur mittens tied to your sleeves by a cord, are warm too. It is only your face that feels the cold, and so you keep your head down and watch the snow.

The house comes nearer. In the deepening dusk it is only a low dark shape, but there is that column of smoke rising from the roof, gray now against the darkening sky. And when the door opens for a moment, a block of orange light falls out across the snow, and a cloud of steam pours out of the doorway and rolls along the ground, as if the house has breathed out.

Behind you, the meadow has gone from gold to blue to gray in the space of a few minutes. The forest at the far edge is a solid black wall now. Above it, the first star has appeared, low in the east, steady and white. You look back once along the track you have come by, the two long grooves of the sledge runners and the dark prints of the ox, running away across the snow toward the hay stacks, which stand out there in the dusk like small, patient houses of their own.

The ox knows where it is going. It quickens its pace a little, without being asked, and lowers its head, and you hear it give a low grunt, deep in its chest. It can smell the barn. It can smell the warmth and the hay and the other animals. Like you, it is very ready to be home.

Today, in Yakutsk and the villages around it, many families still keep Sakha cattle and horses, and they still make hay in summer and haul it in winter. But most do it with tractors and trucks, and the hay may be pressed into bales. The ox and the wooden sledge have mostly gone into museum photographs.

Historians do not all agree about when the Sakha first came to the middle Lena valley. Some, reading the old legends and the evidence of language, think that the main migration north happened around the thirteenth or fourteenth century, perhaps as the great Mongol empire unsettled the lands around Lake Baikal. Others think the movement was slower and more gradual, a drift of families over several centuries, mixing with the forest peoples who were already here. Both sides agree on the result. By the time the Russians came, the Sakha had already made this cold valley a land of cattle and horses.

At the house, the men unload the hay into a stack against the side wall of the barn, where it will be close at hand for the evening feed. Uibaan works without hurry, forking the hay with long, even strokes, the way a man works when he has done the same thing ten thousand times. Byötür unhitches the ox and leads it into the barn, ducking under the low lintel, and the ox follows him gratefully into the warmth.

Then the two of them come into the house, and the door closes behind them, and the cold goes out of the room like a guest who has stayed too long.

They are white all over. Frost covers their fur hats, their collars, their shoulders. Uibaan's beard is a solid mat of ice, and his eyebrows are white, and little beads of ice cling to his eyelashes. Byötür, who has not grown much of a beard yet, has instead a delicate rim of frost all around the edge of his hood, like the lace on a church cloth.

"Stand by the fire," Akulina says. "Not too close. Let it come out slowly."

That is an old rule in this house. You do not rush to the flames when you come in from the deep cold. You let your face and hands warm a little at a time, or they will burn and ache.

The men stand a few paces back from the hearth, and slowly the ice in Uibaan's beard begins to soften, and then to drip, and little dark spots appear on the clay floor at his feet. Miitere watches this with enormous interest. He has seen it a hundred times, and it never stops being interesting.

"Father," he says, "you are melting."

"I am," says Uibaan, and for a moment, just a moment, he smiles.

Maaria has already put a kettle on the edge of the fire. She does not ask how the work went. She can see how the work went. The hay is home, the ox is in, and her husband and her son have come back with all their fingers and all their toes. That is enough.

Byötür pulls off his mittens, slowly, and flexes his fingers in front of the fire. He has been thinking about the horses all afternoon, out there in the forest pasture beyond the lake, digging for grass in the snow. He will check on them tomorrow, in the short light. For now, there is nothing to be done.

Kuolai looks up from his carving.

"Was the ice good on the lake?" he asks.

"Good and thick," says Uibaan. "Thick enough for anything."

Maaria pours the tea. It is strong brick tea, the kind that comes all the way from China by caravan and river boat and is sold in the Yakutsk market in hard, pressed slabs. She has broken a piece from the slab with the back of a knife and boiled it in the kettle, and now she adds a generous spoonful of milk to each bowl. The men drink standing, holding the wooden bowls in both hands, letting the steam wash over their faces. The ice in Uibaan's beard is almost gone now.

"There were fox tracks along the edge of the far meadow," says Byötür, between sips. "Fresh ones. Going toward the lake."

"A red one or a gray one?" says Daaria at once, from the bench.

"I did not see the fox," says Byötür patiently. "Only the tracks."

"Then how do you know it was going toward the lake, and not coming back?"

He opens his mouth, and closes it again, and their grandfather laughs out loud.

"She has you there," says Kuolai. "A fox that leaves tracks pointing one way can still be walking the other way, if he is a clever enough fox."

"Foxes cannot walk backward," says Byötür.

"Not the foxes you know," says the old man, and goes back to his carving, very pleased with himself.

Daaria, satisfied that she has won, slips off the bench and goes to the low door at the back of the room. She opens it a crack and looks through into the barn. The calf she has been caring for, a small black heifer born in the autumn, is lying in the straw near the partition, her legs folded under her and her eyes half closed. Daaria watches her for a long moment, then shuts the door softly and comes back to the fire.

"She is warm," she announces, to nobody in particular, and her mother nods without looking up.

The old man nods. It is a small conversation, but it means a great deal in this house. Ice is drinking water, and ice is light, and soon, very soon, we will see why.

In the hearth, the fire has burned down a little, and Akulina sets another log upright in its mouth, and the flames rise up around it, brightening the whole room.

## Part Three — Around Half Past Four: The House of Clay and Frozen Ground

By half past four the rose color has drained out of the southern sky, and the first stars are showing over the forest, small and hard and very bright.

Inside, the room is lit only by the fire. The light is warm and moving, and it reaches into the corners of the house unevenly, so that some parts of the room glow gold and others sink into soft brown shadow. Now is a good moment to look around us properly.

So here is a small question for this part. What, exactly, is this house made of, that it can stand against such cold?

The Sakha winter house was called a balagan. From the outside, it looked a little like a flattened pyramid with its top cut off, a long, low box whose walls leaned slightly inward as they rose. It was built around a frame of strong upright posts, with beams laid across them to hold the roof. The walls were made of straight, slender logs, usually larch, set upright side by side, and tilted gently inward so that they leaned against the frame. The slant was not decoration. It made the walls strong, and it helped them shed snow.

Then came the part that made the house warm. Every autumn, before the cold set in, the walls were plastered all over, outside and in, with a thick coat of clay mixed with cow dung. The dung was not a sign of poverty. It was a building material, and a good one. Mixed with clay, it dried into a hard, tough skin that sealed every crack between the logs, shut out every draft, and held the warmth inside. Women did most of this plastering, slapping the mixture on with their hands and smoothing it, and they renewed it each year, so that by midwinter the house was wrapped in a seamless coat several inches thick.

The roof was nearly flat. Over the beams went poles, then bark, then a deep layer of earth, and over that, all winter, the snow. Snow is a fine insulator, so the heavier it lay, the better.

That is the answer to our small question. This house is made of larch, clay, earth, snow and dung, all working together. It is less like a building and more like a nest.

The door of a Sakha house traditionally faced east or south, toward the rising sun and away from the cold north. It was low, so that you stooped to enter, and it was often hung with a heavy curtain of hide or felt on the inside, a second barrier against the cold. Doors here are small for the same reason that windows are small. Every opening in a wall is a place where heat can escape, and so every opening is made only as large as it truly needs to be.

Inside, the floor is packed earth, swept clean. Around the walls run wide wooden benches, built in as part of the house, where people sit by day and sleep by night. The bench on the far wall, facing the door, is the place of honor, where Kuolai sits. And in the corner there is a small icon of a saint, its painted face catching the firelight.

Here is the surprising detail, and it lies under the floor. Beneath this house, beneath the meadow, beneath the whole town of Yakutsk, the ground is frozen. Not only on the surface in winter. It is frozen all year round, summer and winter alike, to a depth that is hard to imagine. In summer the top few feet thaw, and the grass grows in that thin, soft layer. But below it, the earth stays frozen solid, as it has been for many thousands of years. Today we call this permafrost.

Our source moment for this part is a well. In the year 1828, a merchant in Yakutsk named Fyodor Shergin decided to dig a well in his yard, so that his household would have water all year. His men dug down through frozen earth, and kept digging, and kept finding frozen earth, year after year.

By 1837 the shaft was about three hundred and eighty feet deep, and still the ground at the bottom was frozen. Shergin gave up. He never found water.

But his failed well became one of the most important holes in the history of science. In April 1829, while the digging was still going on, the German scientist Adolph Erman, traveling through Siberia, lowered a thermometer into it and took the first readings. In 1844 the naturalist Alexander von Middendorff, sent by the Russian Academy of Sciences, began careful measurements in the shaft, which local observers kept up until 1846. The world learned from that hole in a Yakutsk yard just how deep the frozen ground of Siberia could go. The well still exists today, protected as a historical monument.

Now, imagine yourself in the house as the dark comes down.

You are sitting on the wide bench along the wall, with a sheepskin under you and a fur coat over your knees. The wall behind your back is smooth and slightly cool, the clay plaster hard and dry under your fingers. If you press your ear against it, you hear nothing at all. No wind. The house is so thick and so tightly sealed that the outside world might not exist.

The fire fills the room with a soft, steady sound. It hisses and murmurs and sometimes gives a small sharp crack as a pocket of resin bursts in the larch. The smell of the room is woodsmoke, and warm clay, and the faint sweetness of hay, and underneath it all, very gently, the smell of cattle, which you have already stopped noticing.

You look up. The ceiling is low and dark, the beams blackened by years of smoke. Above them lies the earth roof and the snow, a heavy, quiet blanket. You look down. The floor is earth, firm and swept, and you know now that beneath it, a few feet down, the ground is iron hard with ancient ice, and will be hard all summer long.

And yet you are warm. The fire is in front of you. The thick walls are around you. The frozen ground is far below, and you are safe above it, like a small boat on a still, cold sea.

You lean back and close your eyes for a moment. The warmth of the fire lies across your face and knees like a light blanket. Your back, against the wall, is cooler. Your feet, on the earth floor, are cooler still, and you draw them up onto the bench and tuck them under the fur. That is the shape of warmth in a house like this. It is strongest at the fire and at the height of a seated person, and it thins out toward the floor and the corners. Everyone in the family knows this map of warmth by heart, without ever having thought about it. The old sit nearest the fire. Babies sleep at the height of the benches, never on the floor. Wet boots go near the hearth, but not too near. Milk is set to cool by the door. Every object in the room has its place on the map.

In modern Yakutsk, the frozen ground is still the first thing every builder must think about. Large buildings are raised on concrete piles driven deep into the permafrost, with a gap of air underneath, so that the heat of the building does not thaw the ground and make it sink. Our family's house sits directly on the earth. It is low and light enough, and plastered thickly enough, that it has learned to live with what lies beneath it.

Historians of science do not all agree about how good those early well measurements were. Some argue that Middendorff's readings at the bottom of Shergin's shaft were slightly off, perhaps because the open shaft let cold air sink down into it in winter and distort the numbers. Others defend him, pointing out that he worked with the best instruments of his time, under hard conditions, and got the main fact exactly right. Both views can be held at once. His numbers may have been imperfect, but his conclusion, that the ground here is frozen far deeper than anyone had believed, has never been overturned.

Miitere is sitting on the floor near the fire, poking at a small pile of wood chips from his grandfather's carving. He has been listening to the men talk about the frozen ground near the river, where the bank has slumped.

"Grandfather," he says, "why does the earth stay cold underneath, even in summer?"

Kuolai lays down his knife and considers this, seriously, as he considers all of Miitere's questions.

"In the town," he says, "there was once a rich Russian merchant who wanted a well. He dug down, and down, for years. Deeper than the tallest larch is tall. Deeper than three tall larches standing one on top of another. And at the bottom, still, the earth was ice."

"Did he find water?"

"Never. He found only cold. The earth here keeps its winter inside it, the way a big pot of soup keeps its heat long after you take it off the fire. Summer is only a thin skin on top."

Miitere thinks about this. "So we live on top of the winter."

"We live on top of the winter," says Kuolai, "and we keep a fire burning so that the winter does not come up into the house."

Akulina, who has been listening, gives a small dry laugh. "You make it sound like a bear," she says.

"It is a bear," says Kuolai, with dignity, "a very slow one," and he picks up his knife again.

Maaria has finished her sewing for now. She folds the foal skin coat and sets it aside, and goes to the door that leads through to the barn. Behind it, the cows are shifting and breathing. She listens for a moment, as a mother listens at the door of a sleeping child, and then she comes back to the fire.

Daaria is sitting next to her grandmother, warming her hands. She has a small, round face, very serious, and a thick plait of black hair that reaches almost to her waist. Her grandmother is showing her, quietly, how to stand a new log so that it catches from the back.

"Not in the middle," says Akulina. "Against the wall of the hearth. Let the old fire climb into the new wood. Fire likes to climb."

Daaria sets the log carefully on its end. The flames lick at it, hesitate, and then catch, running up its side in a bright, eager line.

"Good," says her grandmother, and Daaria glows almost as brightly as the log.

The fireplace itself deserves a closer look, because we will be sitting beside it all night. Russians in Siberia called this kind of hearth a kamelek. In the Sakha language it has its own name, kömülüök. It stands against one wall of the room, a little out from it, and it is made of a framework of poles, thickly plastered with clay, rising from a wide, open mouth at the bottom into a chimney that goes straight up through the roof. There is no grate and no iron door. The fire burns in the open, on a floor of hardened clay, and its heat pours straight out into the room.

It is a very simple machine, and it does two jobs at once. It heats the room with its open flames, the way a campfire heats the people sitting around it. And its thick clay body soaks up heat all day and slowly gives it back, the way a warm stone gives back the sun. When the fire is burning high, the clay of the hearth is too hot to touch. When it burns low, the clay stays warm for hours.

There is one weakness. A chimney that goes straight up is a fine road for smoke, but it is also a fine road for heat to escape. So at night, once the flames have died down to a bed of glowing embers, the chimney can be closed over, partly or wholly, to keep the warmth inside the house. Akulina knows exactly when to do this. Too soon, and the room fills with smoke. Too late, and the heat of the whole evening goes up into the stars.

Uibaan has gone to the woodpile against the outside wall and come back with an armful of split larch, which he stacks beside the hearth, close enough that the wood will warm and dry, but not close enough to catch. Then he sits down heavily on the bench beside his father and stretches out his legs toward the fire.

"The ice we cut for the windows is stacked by the door," he says. "Clear ones, from the deep end of the lake."

Kuolai nods. "The east window has a crack in it," he says. "I saw it this morning."

"Then we change it tonight, before we eat," says Uibaan, and closes his eyes for a moment, and lets the warmth soak into him.

The kamelek, the open clay fireplace, stands in the room like a small and patient guardian, and the new log burns in its mouth, and the light of it spills out across the floor toward our feet.

## Part Four — Around Half Past Five: The Window Made of Ice

It is half past five now, and full dark outside, the kind of dark that comes early and stays long, with stars thick over the alaas and the lake lying gray and silent under its snow.

Uibaan has rested long enough. He stands, stretches, and reaches for his fur coat again. Byötür, without being asked, reaches for his.

Here is a small question for this part, before we come to the larger one. Where does a family get its drinking water, in a land where every lake and stream is frozen solid for seven months of the year?

The answer is stacked beside the door. Each autumn, in October or November, when the lake ice was still fairly thin and easy to cut, the men of the household went out onto the lake with axes, saws and iron bars. They cut the ice into large, square blocks, pried them loose, and dragged them home on sledges behind the ox. The blocks were stacked outside the house, often on a raised platform of logs, so that the dogs could not reach them and the snow would not bury them. And there they sat all winter, a glittering wall of frozen lake water, slowly gathering a cap of frost.

Whenever water was needed, someone went out with an axe, chipped off a piece, and carried it inside. It went into a large pot or a copper kettle beside the fire, and slowly, with small, cracking sounds, it melted. The water was clean and sweet. Lake ice is purer than lake water, because as water freezes, much of what floats in it is pushed aside. Sakha families in this country still harvest ice in the same way today, and many say that melted lake ice makes the finest tea there is.

It took a great deal of ice to see a household through the winter. A family of seven, with cattle to water as well as tea to boil and porridge to cook, might need a stack of blocks taller than a man and as long as the side of the house. Cows drink a great deal, even in winter, and every drop of their water had to be melted first, by the fire, in the house. So the ice stack by the door was watched as closely as the hay stacks in the meadow. If it ran low before spring, there would be another cold day on the lake with axes and saws, chopping through ice that by then might be five or six feet thick.

And some of the ice was saved for summer. Many Sakha households had a cellar dug down into the frozen ground near the house, a small, dark chamber where the walls were permafrost and the air stayed below freezing even in July. Into it went the last blocks of spring ice, along with meat and fish and butter that needed to stay cold. When the summer heat came, and the lake was warm and green with weeds, the family could still go down into the cellar and bring up a block of clean winter ice for the tea. The frozen ground that defeated the merchant Shergin was, to a Sakha family, a free and endless refrigerator.

There is something else about ice that every person in this house understands without ever putting it into words. Ice is not all alike. Ice from shallow water, near the reeds, is cloudy and full of tiny bubbles and bits of plant. Ice from fast water is rough. Ice that formed under falling snow is white and grainy and weak. But ice that formed slowly, in still, deep water, on calm nights early in the winter, is hard and dense and almost perfectly clear. Children learned to tell the difference by looking, and by the sound the ice made when they tapped it with a stick.

So that is our small answer. The family drinks the lake, one chip at a time. And now we come to the first of our three carry-questions, because the same lake that gives this house its water also gives it light.

How do you make a window out of ice?

Here is how. In late autumn, when the men went out to cut the drinking ice, they also looked for something special. They wanted the clearest ice they could find, from a deep, still part of the lake where the water had frozen slowly and evenly, with no bubbles, no snow mixed in, no cracks. From this clear ice they cut thin slabs, a few inches thick, shaped to fit the window openings of the house.

The windows of a balagan were small, and in winter, the summer coverings came out of them. In their place went the slabs of ice. Each slab was set into the window opening from the outside, pressed against the frame. Then came the clever part. Someone took a pot of water and poured it, or splashed it, around the edges of the slab, where the ice met the wood and the clay. In that cold, the water froze almost at once, and welded the slab into the wall as firmly as if it had grown there. Snow could be packed around the edges too, and wetted, to seal it further.

And that was the window. It let in light, a soft, milky, silvery light, by day. It kept out the wind and the cold. It did not melt, because the outer face of the slab was in contact with air far below freezing, and the slab was thick enough that the warmth of the room could only make its inner face a little damp. And it lasted all winter long, until the spring sun came back strong enough to soften it.

If a slab cracked, as the east window in our house has cracked, it was simply replaced. There was always more ice.

So the answer to our first carry-question is this. You make a window out of ice by choosing the clearest lake ice, cutting it to fit, setting it in the frame from outside, and then using the cold itself as your glue.

Here is the surprising detail. Glass, in this country in the eighteen eighties, was rare and expensive. It had to be carried thousands of miles from European Russia, by cart, by sledge, and by river boat, and it broke easily on the way. Wealthy merchants in the town of Yakutsk might have glass windows. But in the countryside, and in many town houses too, people made do with what the land gave them. In summer, they covered the windows with thin, stretched membranes, from the bladders or stomachs of cattle, or with sheets of mica, a mineral that splits into thin, clear layers. In winter, they used ice.

Our witness for this part is a Swedish traveler named Jonas Stadling, who came through this country in 1898, traveling down the Lena River in search of news of a lost balloon expedition to the North Pole. He passed through Yakutsk, and he stayed in Sakha homes, and he wrote about them in a book published in English in 1901 under the title Through Siberia.

He described the Sakha dwellings as wooden huts with sloping walls covered with a thick layer of turf, and then he wrote this: "In the place of window-glass ox-bladders are used in summer-time and ice in winter." He added that the window openings were usually not more than a foot square in size. It is a plain sentence, written by a tired traveler. But it tells us that the ice windows of our night in 1885 were still in use more than a decade later, as an ordinary part of life.

Now, let us go out with the men and change the window.

You step outside behind Uibaan into the dark. The cold closes around you at once, and the inside of your nose freezes with the first breath, a tight, crackling feeling, so you pull the fur up over your face. Overhead, the sky is crowded with stars, more than you have ever seen, so bright and so close that they seem to be hanging just above the roof.

Byötür carries a burning stick from the fire, wrapped at one end, and its small orange light jumps across the snow. He holds it up to the east window. The old slab is there, gray and frosted on its outside face, and you can see the crack running through it from one corner, a thin bright line in the torchlight.

Uibaan taps around the edges with the back of an axe, carefully, and the old ice comes loose in two pieces. Warm air from inside, which has found the gap, breathes out at you through the empty window, smelling of smoke and tea. Quickly, Byötür lifts the new slab from the stack by the door. It is clear as water, as clear as a pane of glass, and in the torchlight you can see the stars through it.

They set it into the opening together. It fits. Uibaan holds it in place with both mittened hands while Byötür fetches a pot of water from inside, still a little warm, and pours it slowly around the edges.

And you hear it freeze. There is a faint, thin crackling, like someone crumpling a very fine paper, and the water turns white and hard along the seam even as it runs. In less than a minute, the slab is part of the wall. Uibaan lets go. The window holds.

You look through it. Inside, the room is a warm gold blur, the fire a bright moving shape, and the figures of the family soft and wavering, as if you were looking at them from under water. Akulina's face appears, close to the other side of the ice, and she nods once, and disappears.

Today, the apartment blocks of Yakutsk have windows of double or even triple glass, with sealed air gaps between the panes, and in the coldest weather the inside of the glass still frosts over into feathers and ferns. Our family's window is a single slab of frozen lake, and it does almost the same job.

Historians do not entirely agree on how widespread ice windows really were. Some accounts give the impression that every Sakha house had them, every winter. Others suggest the picture was more mixed, with mica, oiled skin, or bladder used in some houses through the winter as well, and with ice used most in the poorer or more remote households, or in the very coldest months. Both views are probably true in different places and different families. What all the accounts agree on is that ice windows were real, common, and perfectly sensible.

Back inside, the men stand once more at a distance from the fire and let the cold leave them slowly. Miitere has gone to the east window and is standing on his toes, peering at the new slab.

"It is clearer than the old one," he says.

"It came from the deep end of the lake," says Uibaan. "Where the water is still."

"Can I see the stars through it?"

Byötür lifts him up so that he can press his face close to the ice. It is cold against the tip of his nose, and he pulls back, laughing, and then leans in again more carefully. Outside, past the faint shimmer of the slab, the stars are there, wavering a little, as if they are under water too.

"I can see them," he says, in a whisper, as if they might hear.

"The stars can see you too," says his grandfather from the bench. "So be good."

Miitere is put down, and runs to the fire, and sits beside his grandmother, and holds out his cold nose toward the flames. Maaria shakes her head at the drips of melted ice on the floor by the window, and fetches a cloth.

"It will run a little tonight," she says, "until it settles."

That is true. A new slab, freshly set, will weep a little on its inner face as the warmth of the room touches it. By morning it will have found its balance, a thin skin of damp on the inside, hard ice on the outside, and it will stay that way, quietly, until April.

Kuolai watches the window with an expression of satisfaction. He has set more ice windows in his life than he could count, and he has never yet grown tired of the moment when the water freezes and the slab holds.

"My father," he says, "used to say that a house in winter needs three things from the lake. Water to drink, fish to eat, and ice to see by."

"And what does the lake need from us?" asks Daaria.

The old man smiles. "Only that we take no more than we need," he says, "and say thank you."

Daaria considers this, and seems to find it reasonable.

"When I was a boy," the old man goes on, "my mother would not let anyone in the house break a window slab on purpose, even when it was spring and the slab was half melted. She said the ice had kept us all winter, and it should be allowed to go back to the lake in its own time. So in May we would stand there and watch it drip, drip, drip, slower than anything, and we were not allowed to touch it."

"That sounds very boring," says Miitere.

"It was very boring," agrees his grandfather. "And then one morning you would wake up and it would be gone, and the sun would be pouring in, and the swallows would be back, and it was not boring anymore."

Maaria has hung the kettle back over the fire. Uibaan sits down again. Byötür goes through the back door into the barn to check the ox, and comes back saying that it is lying down, chewing, perfectly content. And the household settles again, the way water settles after a stone is dropped into it.

In the hearth, the fire has burned down while the door stood open, and Akulina rebuilds it now, log by log, until the flames stand tall again in the open clay mouth of the kamelek.

## Part Five — Around Half Past Six: Supper, and a Gift for the Fire

By half past six the window has settled, the kettle is singing again, and the smell of warm butter is beginning to fill the room.

Supper in a Sakha house is not hurried. The light outside has been gone for hours, and there are many hours of darkness still ahead, so there is no reason to rush anything. Maaria has laid out the low table near the hearth, and the family gathers around it on the benches and on the floor, the old people nearest the fire.

Here is a small question for this part. What does a family eat, in the depth of a Siberian winter, when nothing at all is growing for hundreds of miles?

The plain answer is milk, in a dozen different forms. The Sakha were, before anything else, a dairy people. Their cattle gave milk in the long light of summer, and the family spent the summer turning that milk into foods that would keep. They churned butter, and stored it in birch-bark vessels. They made a thick, rich sour cream. They made a kind of whipped cream, beaten with a wooden whisk until it rose into a light, cold foam, sometimes with berries folded in. And they made a sour, fermented milk called tar, which was perhaps the most important food of all.

Tar was made by letting milk sour and thicken, then adding more milk, and more, all through the summer and autumn, in a large vessel. Into it went whatever else the family could spare. Berries. The cleaned bones of fish, which softened in the sourness. Edible roots, gathered from the meadows. In winter the whole mass froze solid, and pieces were cut from it and thawed, and boiled with water and a little flour into a sour, filling soup. A nineteenth-century observer of the Vilyui country, to the west of here, wrote that sour milk played the main role in the people's nutrition. That was true here too.

Then there was fish. Every lake in this country was full of small, golden carp, and in late autumn, when the ice was firm enough to walk on, whole neighborhoods gathered for a great communal net-fishing under the ice. They cut holes in a long line across the lake and passed a net from hole to hole beneath the ice, and drew it out full of fish, which froze on the ice almost as soon as they landed. The family stored them outside like stacks of firewood, frozen hard, and brought them in as needed.

And there was meat, though less of it than you might think. In late autumn, when the first real frost came, the family slaughtered one or two animals, a cow that was too old to calve, or a young bull. The meat froze, and was stored outside or in the ice cellar, and lasted the winter.

So that is the answer. In winter, a Sakha family ate the summer, carefully preserved in milk and butter, in frozen fish, and in a little meat, all of it kept fresh by the cold itself.

Tonight there are fish. Maaria has brought in four frozen carp from the stack outside, and set them by the fire to soften just slightly. And there will be salamat.

Here is our surprising detail, and it comes from a Baltic German naturalist. Alexander von Middendorff, whom we met beside Shergin's well, traveled through this country between 1842 and 1845 on his great expedition for the Russian Academy of Sciences. He wrote about Sakha food, and he described salamat with real affection.

Salamat is a porridge, made by heating butter in a pot until it is very hot, and stirring flour into it, and then thinning it with milk or cream until it is smooth and thick and golden. It is enormously rich. It is warming in a way that is hard to describe until you have eaten it on a night like this one. Middendorff described it, in a phrase that has come down to us in translation, as a food fit for powerful spirits, for rich people, for the sick, and for women in labor. In other words, it was the food you gave to those who most needed strength, and to the gods.

And that tells us something about the Sakha table. Butter was not simply food. It was wealth, and medicine, and an offering. A family's well-being could be measured by its butter.

The flour in the salamat tells its own story. The Sakha had not traditionally grown grain, because the summers here are short and the frost can come in any month. But by the eighteen eighties, rye and wheat flour were coming into the country in large amounts, carried up the Lena by Russian merchants and sold in Yakutsk, and some Sakha farmers near the town had begun to sow small fields of barley and rye themselves. So bread, flat cakes and flour porridges were becoming part of ordinary meals, especially in the households closest to the town. In the remote districts to the north, flour was still a luxury, bought a little at a time and saved for guests.

And there was the frozen butter itself. Butter churned in summer was packed into containers of folded birch bark, sewn with strips of root or sinew, and stored in the cold. In winter it froze as hard as wood. Sakha children were given curls of it, shaved off with a knife, as a treat, and travelers on the winter roads carried lumps of frozen butter in their bags, to be eaten as they went, because there was no better fuel for a body moving through the cold.

Now, come and sit at the table.

You are sitting on the bench near the fire, with your fur coat loosened now, and the warmth of the room pressing gently against your face and hands. In front of you there is a wooden bowl, carved from a single piece of birch, smooth and dark with years of use. Maaria ladles salamat into it, thick and steaming, a pale gold color with little pools of melted butter gathering on top.

You lift it in both hands. It is heavy and hot. You take a spoonful, and the taste is rich and nutty and slightly sweet, the butter deep and browned, the flour soft. It goes down slowly, and you feel it spread inside you, a warmth that starts in your chest and moves outward into your arms.

Beside the bowl is a plank with a frozen fish on it. Uibaan takes a sharp knife and shaves thin curls from the fish, long translucent ribbons of flesh that curl as they fall. You pick one up. It is still frozen, cold and firm, and when you put it in your mouth, it melts slowly on your tongue, clean and sweet and slightly salty, like the taste of the lake itself. Russian visitors called this way of eating fish stroganina. To the family, it is simply supper.

The fire crackles. The tea is poured again, dark and milky. Somebody laughs. The windows are black squares of ice, gleaming faintly.

Today, in Yakutsk, you can buy stroganina in restaurants, served on a cold plate with salt and pepper and a little dish of sauce, and many families in the city still keep frozen fish on their balconies all winter, exactly as their great-grandparents did outside their houses.

Historians do not all agree about how well fed the Sakha countryside really was in the late nineteenth century. Some writers, especially the exiles who lived among poorer families, described long months of hunger in late winter and early spring, when the stores ran low and the cows gave little milk, and families mixed the soft inner bark of pine trees into their food to stretch it. Others, describing richer households with large herds, wrote of tables heavy with butter and cream. Both pictures are true. It depended on the family, and on the year. Our family, tonight, is neither very rich nor very poor, and it is still early enough in the winter that the stores are good.

Before anyone eats, though, there is something to be done.

Akulina rises from her place, slowly, one hand on her knee. She takes a small spoonful of the salamat, the first spoonful from the pot, and a little piece of butter on the tip of her knife, and she goes to the fire.

The family falls quiet.

She holds the spoon out over the flames, and speaks to the fire in a low voice, in Sakha. She is not praying in church words. She is speaking to the fire the way you would speak to an old member of the household, someone respected, someone you depend on. And then she tips the salamat and the butter gently into the flames.

The fire hisses, and flares up yellow for a moment, and the smell of browning butter fills the room.

This is called feeding the fire. The Sakha believed, and many still believe, that the fire has a spirit, a master or keeper, who lives in the hearth. You do not spit in the fire. You do not poke it carelessly with a knife or throw rubbish into it. And at important moments, and in many families at every meal, you give it the first and best part of the food, because the fire warms you and feeds you, and it is only fair to feed it in return.

Akulina comes back to the table and sits down, and the family begins to eat.

Miitere, who has been very patient, falls on his salamat. Daaria eats neatly, small spoonfuls, the way her mother does. Byötür eats a great deal very quickly, because he is sixteen. Kuolai eats slowly, and dips curls of frozen fish into a little dish of salt, and chews thoughtfully, and says nothing at all for a long time. Then he says, to no one in particular, "Good salamat," and Maaria, who made it, looks quietly pleased.

"Grandmother," says Miitere, with his mouth full, "does the fire like salamat best, or butter?"

"Butter," says Akulina, without hesitation. "Everybody likes butter best."

"Then why give it salamat at all?"

"Because the fire should eat what we eat," she says. "That is good manners. You do not give a guest one thing and eat another thing yourself."

Miitere thinks about this, and then, very carefully, saves the last small spoonful in his bowl, and looks at his grandmother.

She nods. He gets up and goes to the hearth, and holds his spoon over the flames as he has seen her do, and says something under his breath, and tips it in. The fire hisses. He comes back looking solemn and important.

"What did you say to it?" asks Daaria.

"I told it thank you," says Miitere, "and to keep my grandfather warm in the night."

The old man reaches over and puts a hand on the boy's head, and leaves it there for a moment, and says nothing.

Uibaan, who has said almost nothing all through the meal, sets his bowl down and wipes his beard.

"The merchant Popov was asking after our butter again," he says. "When I was in the town at the start of the month. He says the Russians in the town will pay well for it before the fast."

"They always pay well before the fast," says Maaria, "and then they pay nothing after." But she is smiling a little. Butter that can be sold means tea and flour and needles and thread, and perhaps a length of bright cotton cloth for Daaria, who is growing out of everything.

"We will see how the cows do through the cold," says Akulina. "Cows first. Merchants after."

Nobody argues with that. It is the oldest rule in the house.

Kuolai pushes his bowl away at last, and leans back against the wall, and folds his hands across his stomach.

"When I was young," he says, "there was a winter when the hay ran short. It was a wet summer, and the grass rotted in the stacks. By the end of the winter we were feeding the cows on willow twigs and the bark of trees, and we ourselves were eating pine bark mixed into the tar, and saying it was delicious."

"Was it delicious?" asks Miitere.

"It was terrible," says his grandfather. "But we all lived, and the cows lived, and in the spring the grass came. And ever since then, I have never once complained about salamat."

Byötür, who has finished first, as always, is looking hopefully at the pot.

"There is more," says his mother, without turning her head, and he gets up and helps himself, and his grandmother clicks her tongue at the size of the helping, and he pretends not to hear.

Daaria has been quiet. She is looking at the fire, where the butter her grandmother offered has long since burned away.

"Does the fire spirit live in every fire," she asks, "or only in ours?"

Akulina takes her time answering. "Every hearth has its own," she says at last. "When a family moves to a new house, they carry fire from the old hearth to light the new one, so that the fire knows them. When a girl marries and goes to her husband's house, she bows to the new fire, and gives it butter, so that it will know her too. A fire is like a person. It has to be introduced."

Daaria thinks about this for a long while. "When I marry," she says, "I will give the new fire a great deal of butter."

"Not too much," says her mother. "Or it will think you are showing off."

The meal goes on. The kettle is filled again with a chip from the ice by the door, and the ice cracks and settles as it melts. There is more tea. There is a little more fish. Uibaan cuts a curl of frozen butter and eats it as it is, like a sweet. Outside, the cold deepens, the stars turn very slowly over the roof, and the new window by the east wall shines faintly, a small silver square.

The fire, fed and content, burns tall and bright in the kamelek, and its light moves across every face around the table.

## Part Six — Around Half Past Seven: Fur, Frost and the Evening Milking

Supper is cleared by half past seven, the bowls wiped and stacked on the shelf, and the kettle pushed to the edge of the hearth where it will stay warm for the rest of the night.

Now the women's evening work begins. Maaria takes down two wooden pails from their peg. Daaria is already on her feet, pulling on a short fur jacket. The cows must be milked, fed and watered before the night closes in, and the hay the men brought home must be carried through.

But before we follow them, here is a small question for this part. How do you dress for a day out in the open, when the air itself can freeze your skin in minutes?

The common answer, all across northern Siberia, was fur, and the Sakha were masters of it. A Sakha winter outfit was built in layers, from the skin outward, and almost every layer came from an animal the family had raised or hunted.

Next to the body went soft underclothes, in older times of fine leather or fur, and by the eighteen eighties, more and more often, of cotton bought from Russian merchants. Over those went trousers of fur or leather, and over the trousers, long leggings of fur, reaching above the knee. The boots were the most important piece of all. They were tall and soft, made from the skin of reindeer legs or from horse hide, with the fur sometimes turned outward. Inside, the feet were wrapped in socks of fur or felt, and the space was often padded with soft dry grass, which absorbed the moisture from the feet and could be changed each evening. A dry foot is a warm foot. A damp foot, at fifty below, is a foot in danger.

Then came the coat. It was long, often reaching below the knees, made of fur, and it was cut generously, with room inside to trap a layer of warm air. Over the head went a fur hat with flaps that came down over the ears and could be tied under the chin, and often a separate fur collar or scarf for the neck and face. The mittens were long and thick and lined, tied to each other by a cord that ran through the sleeves, so that a mitten pulled off in a hurry would never be dropped and lost in the snow.

The furs themselves came from many animals. Horse hide and the soft, short fur of foals made warm coats and boots. Cattle hides made tough outer leather. From the forest came the furs of hares, foxes and squirrels, and from the north, by trade, reindeer skins. For centuries, too, fur had been the way the Sakha paid their tribute to the Russian state. From the sixteen hundreds onward, every adult man was expected to hand over a set number of good pelts each year, sable and fox above all, as a kind of tax. By the eighteen eighties, the tribute was mostly paid in money, but the old knowledge of fur, how to trap it, scrape it, soften it and sew it, was still in every Sakha household.

The sewing was women's work, and it was skilled work. A coat that was badly sewn, with gaps at the seams, could let in a thread of cold air that would chill its wearer to the bone on a long journey. So the seams were tight and close, sewn with thread made of twisted sinew, which swells slightly when damp and fills its own needle holes. A good fur coat lasted many winters. It was mended, and patched, and handed down, and when at last it wore out, its best pieces were cut out and used again in smaller things, mittens, hats and the tops of children's boots.

That is the answer to our small question. You dress in layers of fur, from your boots to your hat, with dry grass in your boots, air trapped inside your coat, and your mittens on a string.

Here is the surprising detail. Even with all this, the one part of you that cannot be fully covered is your face, and the face is where the cold always tries to get in. So people traveling together in the deep cold kept an eye on each other. Every so often, one would glance at the other's nose, cheeks and chin, looking for a small, waxy white patch, the first sign of frostbite. You often cannot feel it yourself. But your companion can see it. The patch would be gently warmed with a bare hand held against it, and then the hand would go quickly back into its mitten, and the journey would go on.

Our witness for this part is an English nurse named Kate Marsden. In 1891, she set out from Moscow on an extraordinary journey across Siberia, by sledge, by river boat and on horseback, seeking out the people in remote Sakha villages who were suffering from leprosy, and hoping to help them. She went on to Yakutsk and beyond, and she wrote about it all in a book called On Sledge and Horseback to Outcast Siberian Lepers, published in the early eighteen nineties.

She described being so wrapped in layers of fur and wool that she could hardly move, and had to be lifted into her sledge. And she wrote of the cold, in one small, plain line: "Even one's eyelashes freeze, so that it is almost impossible to see." That is true here too, as you have already felt. The breath rises past the eyes, and the moisture in it freezes onto the lashes, and they grow heavy and white, and stick together when you blink.

Now, follow Maaria and Daaria to the barn.

You are standing by the low door at the back of the room, the one that leads through into the barn. Maaria has opened it, and a warm, thick, animal smell rolls out to meet you, the smell of cattle and hay and manure, and a damp, living heat. You duck under the lintel and step through.

It is darker in here. The only light comes from a burning splinter of resinous pine that Daaria carries in her hand, and from the glow of the fire through the open door behind you. As your eyes adjust, you see the cows. There are five of them, standing in a row along the wall, each in her own stall, with a calf or two in a pen at the end. They are small, smaller than you expected, with thick shaggy coats, dark brown and black and white-faced, and they turn their heads to look at you with large, calm, wet eyes.

The air in here is warm, not as warm as the house, but far warmer than outside, and full of the sound of breathing, heavy and slow. A cow shifts her weight, and the straw rustles under her feet. Another lets out a long sigh through her nose. Somewhere a calf gives a small, impatient bleat.

Maaria crouches beside the first cow with her pail. She does not hurry. She speaks to the cow softly, in Sakha, the way you would speak to a friend, and lays one hand flat on her warm flank, and then begins to milk. The milk rings into the empty pail with a thin, high sound, and then, as the pail fills, the sound grows softer and deeper. A little steam rises from it in the light of the splinter.

You put out your own hand and rest it on the cow's side. It is warm, wonderfully warm, through the thick hair, and you can feel her breathing, and somewhere deep inside her, very faintly, the slow working of her great stomach, turning summer grass into milk.

Today, people in Yakutsk dress for winter in a mixture of the old and the new, with down jackets and modern boots for some, and for many others, still, long fur coats and tall fur boots made in the old Sakha style, because in the very deepest cold nothing yet invented works quite so well.

Historians and anthropologists sometimes disagree about how far we can trust the travelers and exiles who wrote about the Sakha in this period. Some point out that most of them were outsiders, Russians, Poles, Englishwomen, who did not always understand what they saw, and who sometimes described Sakha life as harder or stranger than it seemed to the Sakha themselves. Others reply that writers like Sieroszewski lived for years in Sakha homes, learned the language, married into Sakha families, and recorded things that would otherwise have been lost. The fairest view is probably to listen to them carefully, and to remember always whose eyes we are borrowing.

In the barn, Daaria has wedged the pine splinter into a crack in the wall, where it burns with a small, smoky, steady flame. She goes to the calves' pen. Her own calf, the small black heifer, stands up as she approaches, and pushes her wet nose into Daaria's hands, looking for milk.

"Not yet," Daaria tells her. "Wait for your mother to finish."

Maaria moves along the row, one cow after another. When each pail is full, she carries it back through to the house and pours it into a wide, shallow wooden vessel, where it will stand and cool, and the cream will rise. A little of it will be drunk fresh, tonight, warm with tea. The rest will become butter, or sour milk, or tar for the stores.

The cows give much less milk in winter than in summer. Two of them, tonight, give almost nothing at all, because they are close to calving, and Maaria does not try to take more than they want to give. She runs her hand along their bellies instead, and feels the calves moving inside them, and is satisfied.

Then comes the watering. Byötür has come through from the house carrying a big pot of water melted by the fire, still a little warm. Water too cold would chill a cow from the inside on a night like this. He pours it into a wooden trough, and the cows crowd to it and drink in long, noisy gulps, and he goes back for more, and then more again. It takes many trips. Every one of those drinks of water, you will remember, began as a block of lake ice by the door, and was melted a pot at a time over the kamelek.

Last comes the hay. Daaria and her brother carry it through in great armfuls from the stack by the outer barn door, scattering loose wisps across the floor, and spread it in front of each cow. The cows bury their noses in it and begin to eat, steadily, with a soft tearing and chewing that fills the whole barn.

"There," says Maaria at last, standing in the middle of the barn with her hands on her hips, looking along the row. "Everyone is fed."

Daaria lets her calf suckle the last of what her mother left. The heifer drinks greedily, her tail flicking, and then lies down again in the straw, full, and closes her eyes.

"Look," Daaria whispers to her brother. "She is going to sleep."

"So should you," says Byötür. But he is smiling, and he stands beside her for a while, watching the calf breathe.

Before they go, Maaria stands for a moment at the end of the row, simply looking. She is counting, though she does not count aloud. Five cows. Two calves. One ox, lying down at the far end, his great head resting on the straw. Every evening she does this, and every evening the number is the same, and every evening she is quietly glad. A family in this valley could be rich one autumn and poor the next spring, if sickness came to the barn or the hay ran out. The count at the end of the evening milking is a small prayer of its own.

Back in the house, meanwhile, Akulina has made Miitere sit on the bench and take off his boots. He went out to the woodpile with his father after supper, and his grandmother wants to see his feet.

She pulls out the old grass from inside his boots, damp and flattened, and drops it into the fire, where it flares and is gone. Then she reaches into a bundle hanging by the hearth and takes out a handful of fresh dry grass, golden and fragrant, gathered from the meadows in summer for exactly this purpose. She rubs it between her palms to soften it, and packs it into the boots, and sets them near the hearth to warm.

She takes his bare feet in her hands. They are pink and warm.

"Good," she says. "Ten toes. I counted."

"You always count," says Miitere.

"And I will keep counting until you are old and gray," says his grandmother, "and then you can count your own."

When they come back into the house, carrying the last of the milk, the warmth of the fire washes over them, and they stand for a moment, blinking, in the gold light of the kamelek, where the fire is still burning tall.

## Part Seven — Around Half Past Eight: The Barn Behind the Wall

It is half past eight. The milk has been set to cool, the barn door has been closed, and through the wall, very faintly, comes the sound of five cows chewing their hay in the dark.

The whole family is in the main room now, and the evening has entered its quiet, working middle. Maaria has her sewing again. Kuolai has his carving. Uibaan is mending a leather strap from the ox harness, working a bone awl through the stiff hide. Byötür is sharpening an axe, slowly, on a whetstone. Daaria and Miitere are on the floor by the hearth, playing a game with small pieces of carved bone.

Here is a small question for this part. Five cows, eating and drinking all winter, make a great deal of dung. Where does it all go?

The answer is that nothing in this house is wasted, and dung least of all. Every day, sometimes twice a day, someone in the household cleaned out the barn with a wooden shovel. The dung was carried outside and piled in a heap beside the barn, where it froze at once, hard as stone, and stayed frozen until spring. It did not smell. It did not rot. It simply waited.

In spring, when the heap thawed, it became one of the most useful things the family owned. Mixed with clay, it became the plaster for the walls of the house and the barn, as we have seen. Spread on the meadows, it fed next summer's hay. And in the summer, when the mosquitoes and biting flies of the Siberian forest rose up from the marshes in clouds so thick they could drive cattle half mad, the family lit slow, smoky fires of dried dung near the herds, and the cattle gathered gratefully in the smoke to escape the insects. So the dung of winter protected the cattle in summer. It was all one circle.

Now, let us look at the barn itself, the khoton, because it is the key to the second of our three questions.

The khoton was built in the same way as the house, with posts and slanting log walls, plastered thick with clay and dung, and roofed with earth and snow. It was lower than the house, and darker, with fewer openings, because cows do not need windows. Its floor was packed earth, deep in straw and old hay. Along the walls stood the stalls, and at one end there was a pen for the calves.

And it was joined directly to the house. In many Sakha homesteads of this period, the khoton stood right against the back wall of the dwelling, under a continuous roof, with a doorway between them, so that you could pass from the family's fire to the cows' stalls without ever stepping out into the open air. That is the arrangement in our house tonight.

Inside the khoton, the work of the winter has its own rhythm. In the morning, before the late dawn, the cows are milked and fed. Around midday, in the few hours of light, they are given water and more hay, and the barn is cleaned. In the evening, as we have seen, they are milked and fed and watered again. In the early part of the winter, on the milder days, the cows might be let out for an hour or two into a fenced yard beside the barn, to stretch their legs and stand in the low sun. But as the cold deepens toward January, they stay inside almost all the time, and the barn becomes their whole world until spring.

The calves are the most fragile of all. Most Sakha calves are born in late winter and early spring, so that they can grow strong on the first green grass of May. A calf born in the depths of January is at real risk, and the family watches the cows that are close to calving with special care. The calf pen in the corner of the khoton is closest to the house wall, nearest the warmth, and it is deep in the softest hay.

And the ox has his place too, at the far end, nearest the outer door, because he is the one who goes out to work and comes back in, and he is the least bothered by the cold of the doorway. Every animal in the barn, like every person in the house, has its own place on the map of warmth.

Here is our surprising detail. The cows in that barn belong to one of the hardiest breeds of cattle on earth. Sakha cattle are small, much smaller than the dairy cows you might know, and they grow a thick, shaggy winter coat. Their udders are small and covered with hair, which protects them from frostbite, and their bodies are very good at holding heat and using little energy in the cold. They can graze outdoors in early winter at temperatures far below zero, and they can live on hay that larger cattle would find too poor. They are, in a sense, the cattle the Sakha brought with them on their long journey north centuries ago, slowly shaped by this valley into animals that could survive it.

They are also rare now. In the twentieth century, they were largely replaced by larger breeds that gave more milk, and only a little over a thousand purebred Sakha cattle are left today, almost all of them in Yakutia, cared for by farmers who are determined not to let them disappear.

Our source moment for this part returns to Wacław Sieroszewski and his great book of 1896. In its long chapters on Sakha houses and Sakha herds, he described the cattle barn standing against the dwelling, and the women of the household passing back and forth between the fire and the stalls through the day, to milk and feed and water. He was not writing as a passing traveler. He had lived through twelve winters in this country, and for much of that time he had slept in Sakha houses himself, with the cattle breathing on the other side of the wall. When he wrote about the khoton, he was writing about a place he knew by its sounds and its smells, in the dark.

Now, let me ask you to sit very still for a moment.

You are sitting on the bench near the hearth, with your back against the warm clay wall. In front of you, the fire burns steadily, throwing its moving light across the floor. And behind you, on the other side of the back wall, a few feet away, the cows are breathing.

You can hear them, if you listen. A long, slow sigh. The soft tearing of hay. The creak of a stall post as a cow leans against it. A calf shuffling in the straw. It is a deep, low, peaceful sound, like the sound of the sea heard from very far away.

And you can feel them, too, if you lay your hand flat against the back wall. The clay is warmer here than on the other walls. On the far side, five large bodies are standing close together, each one giving off heat like a small stove, all night long. Their warmth soaks into the wall from one side, and the fire's warmth soaks into it from the other, and the whole house sits wrapped between them, the fire and the cattle, like a child between two sleeping parents.

You realize something then. On a night like this, the cows are not visitors in the house. They are part of how the house stays warm.

You open your eyes and look around the room. Every person in it is doing something with their hands. The awl going through the leather. The knife curling the birch. The whetstone sliding along the axe. The needle drawing sinew through fur. Even the children's hands are busy with their pieces of bone. And all of it, every bit of it, is happening in this pool of firelight, with the cold pressed up against the walls on every side and the cattle breathing at your back. It feels less like a house than like a small ship, sailing through the winter with all its crew and all its cargo aboard.

There is one more thing the cattle are being kept safe from, out there in the dark, and it is not only the cold. The forests of this country were home to wolves. In the hungry months of late winter, wolf packs would sometimes come down to the edges of the meadows, drawn by the scent of cattle and horses. The horses, out in their winter pastures, had to take their chances, gathering together in a tight group with the foals in the middle. But the cows in the khoton were safe. No wolf could get through a wall of logs and frozen clay, and no wolf would come close to a house where a fire burned and dogs slept by the door. Tonight, the forest is silent. There are no wolves near. But the walls would keep them out if there were.

Historians and doctors in later years did not all agree about the wisdom of this way of living. Some, especially Russian officials and physicians in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, worried about it. They argued that living so close to cattle, in damp, smoky, crowded rooms, spread disease and harmed people's health, and they urged the Sakha to build separate barns and Russian-style houses with stoves and glass windows. Others, including later ethnographers who looked more closely, argued that the old arrangement was a careful, sensible response to one of the harshest climates anywhere that people farm, and that its benefits, in warmth, in safety, and in the survival of the herd, were very great. Over the following decades, many families did move their barns a little apart, but the old reasons were never forgotten.

And so, here is the answer to the second of our three carry-questions. Why do the cows live under the same roof?

They live under the same roof because, here, everything depends on them, and the cold would take them otherwise. A cow left out in the open, on a night of fifty or sixty below, without shelter, would suffer badly, stop giving milk, and might die. So the khoton keeps them sheltered.

But it is joined to the house, and not built off by itself across the yard, for several reasons that all work together. The first is shared warmth. The fire warms the cattle, and the cattle warm the house, and neither has to stand alone against the cold. The second is work. A Sakha woman might go to her cows many times in a single winter day, to milk them, feed them, water them, and clean them out. Every pot of their drinking water must be melted at the house fire and carried to them. If she had to step outside into the deadly air each time, crossing an open yard in the dark, the work would be slower and much more dangerous. Through the connecting door, it is a few steps. The third is watchfulness. Through that thin wall, the family can hear at once if a cow is sick, or restless, or beginning to calve in the night, and someone can go to her. And the fourth is the simplest of all. The cows are the family's life. Their milk is the family's food, their dung is the family's walls, their calves are the family's future. You keep what is most precious close beside you.

That is why the cows live under the same roof. Not because the family has no choice, but because, in this valley, it is the wisest thing to do.

On the floor by the hearth, Miitere has just lost the bone game for the third time, and is beginning to look stormy. Daaria, who is twelve and therefore very wise, lets him win the fourth.

"Daaria," he says, triumphant, "do the cows know we are here?"

"Of course they know," says Daaria. "They can hear us, the same as we hear them. That is why they are so calm."

"Do they like us?"

Daaria thinks about her black calf pushing its nose into her hands. "Yes," she says firmly. "They like us very much."

Akulina, listening, smiles a little to herself. She remembers a winter, long ago, when the cold was so deep that a cow gave birth in the night and the calf would not stand. Her own mother had carried the newborn calf into the house, wrapped it in an old coat, and laid it by the fire, and it slept there all night among the family, and in the morning it stood on its wobbly legs beside the hearth and bawled for its mother. Sakha families often did this, on the bitterest nights, with their newest calves. She has done it herself, more than once.

"When the white-faced cow calves," she says to Daaria, "if it is a hard night, you will bring the calf in here, by the fire."

Daaria looks up at her, startled and delighted. "Me?"

"You. You are the one who looks after the calves this winter. So it will be your calf to warm."

Daaria turns to look at the back wall as if she could see straight through it to the white-faced cow, standing heavy and patient in her stall.

Uibaan glances up from his harness and catches his mother's eye, and something passes between them, a look of quiet agreement. The girl is ready. That is how a household passes on what it knows, one small trust at a time.

Through the wall, a cow sighs, long and deep, and the fire in the kamelek sighs with her, settling into its bed of embers with a soft fall of sparks.

## Part Eight — Around Half Past Nine: A Guest Comes in from the Cold

At about half past nine, the dogs outside begin to bark, and then go quiet again, and there is the sound of someone stamping snow from his boots by the door.

Uibaan looks up. Byötür puts down the axe. The door opens, the curtain is pushed aside, a great cloud of frozen air rolls in across the floor, and a figure ducks into the room, so wrapped in fur and so white with frost that for a moment nobody can tell who it is.

Then he pulls the fur away from his face, and Miitere shouts, "It is the student."

The student is a young man of about twenty-five, thin, with spectacles that have instantly fogged over in the warmth, so that he stands blind in the doorway, laughing, wiping them on his sleeve. His name is Józef. He is a Pole, a political exile, and he has been living this winter with a Sakha family on the far side of the alaas, about a mile away. He has come across the snow on skis, in the dark, by starlight, because he heard that old Kuolai might sing tonight.

Here is a small question for this part. How did a young man from Warsaw come to be living in a Sakha house in the middle of Siberia, a mile from our fire?

The answer lies in the long, hard history of Russian exile. For centuries, the government of the Russian Empire had used Siberia as a place to send people it did not want at home. Criminals were sent there, and religious dissenters, and rebels. And in the eighteen seventies and eighteen eighties, as revolutionary movements grew in Russia and in Russian-ruled Poland, more and more young political prisoners, students, writers, teachers and workers were sent east into exile without trial, by order of the police.

The farthest and harshest place of exile was the Yakutsk region. Exiles sent here were scattered across the countryside, often one or two to a district, and they were assigned to live among the Sakha communities. The local Sakha were expected to provide them with a place to live, and land or hay or food to support themselves. Some exiles lived alone in small huts. Many lodged in Sakha households, sharing the family's fire, eating the family's food, and learning, slowly, the family's language.

So that is the answer to our small question. Józef is here because the Russian state sent him here, as far from Warsaw as it could. And the Sakha family across the alaas took him in, because that was what was asked of them, and then, little by little, because they grew fond of him.

The journey itself was a kind of punishment. An exile bound for Yakutsk in the eighteen eighties might travel for months. He went by rail and by river steamer as far as the roads allowed, and then on by cart, under guard, along the great post road to Irkutsk, near Lake Baikal. From there, the last stretch ran north, down the Lena River, for nearly two thousand miles, by boat in summer or by sledge along the frozen river in winter, stopping at lonely post stations where the horses were changed. Many exiles arrived in Yakutsk with no warm clothes worth the name, and the first thing they had to learn, from the Sakha, was how to dress.

Here is the surprising detail. Many of these exiles became, without meaning to, some of the most important early students of Sakha life. They had little else to do. They were educated, curious, and lonely, and they found themselves living inside a culture that almost no one in Europe knew anything about. So they began to write things down. Sieroszewski, as we know, wrote his great book. Another Polish exile, Edward Piekarski, sent here in 1881, spent decades compiling a huge dictionary of the Sakha language, which is still used today. Others collected songs, stories, words for cattle and hay and ice, and the names of spirits.

Our source moment for this part comes from a Russian exile, the writer Vladimir Korolenko. He was sent to this region in 1881, after refusing to swear an oath of loyalty to the new tsar, Alexander the Third, and he was settled in the village of Amga, about a hundred and eighty miles from Yakutsk. There he lived for about three years, farming, cutting hay, working with his hands, and listening.

In 1885, the very year of our night, he published a story he had written there, called Makar's Dream, and he gave it the subtitle A Christmas Story. It is about a poor, worn-out settler named Makar, who lives among the Sakha in this cold country and has become, in his habits and his speech, half Sakha himself. On a freezing Christmas Eve, Makar dreams that he has died, and that he must stand before the great Lord of the next world and give an account of his hard life. The story made Korolenko famous almost overnight. And it carried a little of this valley, its cold, its hay, its fires, into the warm drawing rooms of Saint Petersburg and Moscow.

Now, let us be in the room as the guest comes in.

You are sitting by the fire as the young man stands steaming in the doorway. Cold pours off him like water. You can feel it from where you sit, a breath of winter flowing across the floor and around your ankles, and then the curtain falls back over the door and the warmth closes in again.

He is unwrapping himself, layer by layer. A fur scarf, white with the frost of his breathing. A fur hat, with ice in its ear flaps. Long mittens, which he slaps together, and a cloud of frost crystals falls from them and glitters for a moment in the firelight before it melts. Under it all, a thin, pale face, red at the cheeks and the tip of the nose, and a pair of steamed-up spectacles, which he wipes again and again.

Akulina looks hard at his face, as everyone in this country looks at a face that has just come in from the cold. She is checking his nose and his cheeks for the white patch. There is none. She nods, satisfied, and points him to a place on the bench, not too close to the fire.

He sits. Maaria puts a bowl of hot tea with milk into his hands, and he holds it under his face and breathes in the steam, and closes his eyes. The fire crackles. Through the wall, the cows shift and sigh. And slowly, slowly, the young man's shoulders come down from around his ears.

You watch the young man's face as the warmth works into it. Out there, a few minutes ago, he was alone on the snow, under a sky full of hard bright stars, following a narrow ski track through the larches by the faint light reflected off the snow. You can imagine it. The hiss of the skis. The squeak of the poles. The silence, enormous on every side. The breath freezing on the scarf. And then, ahead, through the trees, the faint orange glow from the ice window of this house, and the straight column of smoke against the stars, and the dogs beginning to bark.

You have only been in this house a few hours yourself, but already you understand what that glow would mean, after a mile of dark. It would mean that there were people. It would mean fire, and tea, and a place on a bench. It would mean, quite simply, that you would live.

Historians have not always agreed about how to read the work of the exiles, including Korolenko's. Some admire it deeply, as a rare and humane picture of the Sakha by people who truly lived among them, and Korolenko was praised in his own time for his sympathy. Others point out that the exiles were still outsiders, carried here against their will, often homesick and unhappy, and that their writing sometimes reflects their own loneliness and their own politics as much as Sakha reality. Both are fair. The best of the exiles knew it themselves, and tried hard to let the Sakha speak.

The family gathers around the guest, in the way that families everywhere gather around a visitor on a long winter night. A guest is news. A guest is a story.

"How is the family across the alaas?" asks Maaria.

"All well," says Józef, in careful Sakha. He has been here two winters now, and he speaks it slowly but correctly, with an accent that makes Daaria hide a smile behind her hand. "The old man has a cough, but he says it is nothing. The new calf is strong. And the grandmother sent this."

He reaches into his coat and takes out a small bundle wrapped in birch bark, and hands it to Akulina. She unwraps it. Inside is a lump of frozen butter, pale yellow, carefully shaped.

"For the fire," says Józef. "She said it is from her fire to your fire."

Akulina holds it for a moment, and something softens in her face. The two old women have known each other for fifty years. She rises, and goes to the hearth, and breaks off a small piece of the butter, and gives it to the flames with a few murmured words. The fire hisses and flares. The rest she puts away on the shelf.

"Tell her," she says, "the fire says thank you."

Józef nods gravely. He understands, now, what that means.

Miitere has edged along the bench until he is sitting right beside the guest, staring at his spectacles.

"Can you see through them?" he asks.

"Only when they are not covered in frost," says Józef, and takes them off and gives them to him. Miitere holds them up to his eyes, and the fire swims and blurs and doubles, and he gasps and gives them back very fast. Everybody laughs, including Miitere.

Daaria, who has been longing to ask something since he came in, finally does.

"Is it cold in Warsaw?"

Józef laughs. "It is cold," he says. "But not like this. In Warsaw, in winter, the river freezes, and the snow falls, and people go skating, and there are lamps along the streets that burn gas, so that the whole city glows at night. But the cold there is a soft cold. Here," and he gestures toward the door, toward the black night and the stars, "here the cold is a wall. In Warsaw you walk through the cold. Here you must be clever to get around it."

"And are we clever?" asks Daaria.

"You are the cleverest people I have ever met," says Józef, and he means it, and Daaria, a little embarrassed, looks at the fire.

Uibaan, who rarely asks anything of anyone, has a question of his own.

"When you came down the river," he says, "the first time. What did you think, when you saw our country?"

Józef is quiet for a moment. "I thought I would die here," he says honestly. "It was the end of autumn. The river was full of ice, and the boat could barely move, and the land on both banks was black forest and gray sky, and nothing else, for days. I thought nobody could live in such a place."

"And then?"

"And then the boat stopped at a little landing, and a Sakha woman came down to the water with a pail of milk and a lump of butter, and sold them to us for a few coins, and smiled at me, and said something I did not understand. And I thought, well. Somebody lives here. And they have butter." He smiles. "After that, I was not so afraid."

Uibaan nods slowly, as if that were exactly the right answer.

Kuolai has been watching all this from his place of honor on the far bench. He has not said much. But his eyes are bright.

"You came across the snow at night," he says to Józef, "for an old man's singing."

"I did," says Józef. "Everyone says no one sings the old stories the way you do."

"Everyone says a great many things," says Kuolai. But he is pleased, very pleased, and he does not try to hide it. He sets his carving aside, and brushes the shavings from his knees, and looks at the fire for a long, considering moment.

"Put on more wood," he says to his wife. "If I am going to sing, I want to see your faces."

Akulina chooses two good logs of larch from the pile by the hearth, and sets them on their ends in the mouth of the kamelek, leaning together, and the flames rise up between them, tall and bright, and the whole room fills with gold.

## Part Nine — Around Half Past Ten: The Old Man Sings

By half past ten the new logs have burned down to a deep, steady glow, and Kuolai has been singing for almost an hour.

He sits very upright on the bench of honor, with his eyes half closed and his hands resting on his knees. His voice rises and falls. Sometimes it is a chant, rhythmic and rolling, like a horse at a long, easy trot. Sometimes it is speech, quick and dramatic, as one character answers another. And sometimes it soars up into a true song, high and strange and wavering, as a hero calls out across the three worlds.

Everyone in the room is still. Even the cows, through the wall, seem to be listening.

Here is a small question for this part. How long does it take to tell one of these old stories from beginning to end?

The answer may surprise you. What Kuolai is singing is called an olonkho, and it is the great heroic epic tradition of the Sakha people. An olonkho is not a short tale. A single olonkho might run to ten thousand lines, or fifteen thousand, or more, and a skilled teller, an olonkhosut, could keep it going through a whole long night, and then pick it up again the next night, and the next, for several nights in a row. Kuolai will not finish tonight. He will stop at a good place, when the hero has crossed some great obstacle, and the family will wait, impatiently, for him to go on another evening.

So that is the answer. One olonkho can take many hours, and the longest could fill a week of winter nights.

The olonkho tells of a universe in three layers. Above is the Upper World, the home of the bright spirits and the high gods, the aiyy, who are kind to people. Below is the Lower World, a dark, cold, frightening place, home to the abaasy, monstrous beings who cause sickness and misfortune. And in between is the Middle World, our world, the world of people and cattle and horses and meadows. In the olonkho, a great hero of the Middle World, a warrior born of the bright spirits, rides out on his marvelous horse to protect his people from the creatures of the Lower World, to win a bride, to cross mountains and seas, and at last to bring peace back to the land.

These stories were sung for weddings and summer festivals, and on long nights when the work was done. In a land of short days and very long nights, the olonkhosut was one of the most valued people in any district. A good one could fill a house with neighbors from miles around.

Here is the surprising detail. In the olonkho, the hero's homeland, the beautiful Middle World, is very often described as a land of endless summer. Its meadows are always green. Its lakes are always full of fish. Its cows give rivers of milk, and its horses are fat and shining. Its trees never lose their leaves. It is, in other words, the opposite of a January night in the middle Lena valley. And it is sung, most often, by tired people on dark winter evenings, gathered around a fire, with the cold pressing at the walls. The olonkho gave them summer to sit in, for as long as the singer could keep it going.

A skilled olonkhosut did not simply recite. He performed. He gave each character its own voice, deep and growling for a monster of the Lower World, light and clear for a heroine, proud and ringing for the warrior. He sang the speeches of the gods in a high, solemn chant. He described the hero's horse, its saddle, its bridle, its every hair, in long, rolling passages full of repetition, so that listeners could sink into the rhythm and see it all. Some of these descriptions ran for hundreds of lines. Nobody minded. That was the point. On a long winter night, the more slowly the story went, the longer the warmth of it lasted.

Our source moment for this part comes from two very different places. When Sieroszewski was gathering material for his book of 1896, he collected material on the olonkho and on how it was performed, understanding that they were among the greatest treasures of Sakha culture. Other exiles and Sakha scholars did the same, writing down by firelight whole epics sung by old men like Kuolai.

And more than a century later, in 2005, the United Nations cultural organization, UNESCO, proclaimed the olonkho a masterpiece of the oral and intangible heritage of humanity. In 2008, it was formally inscribed on the world list of living cultural heritage. The old men singing by their hearths had made something the whole world would one day recognize as precious.

Now, let yourself listen.

You are sitting on the bench in the half-dark, your knees drawn up, your back against the warm wall. The fire has burned low, and the room has grown dim and gold, and the old man's face is lit from below, all hollows and shining eyes. His voice fills the house. You do not understand the words, but you do not need to. You hear the gallop in them. You hear the long, ringing call of a hero standing on a mountain. You hear the hush when something terrible stirs in the Lower World, and then the swelling and lifting when the hero rides out to meet it.

You glance around. Miitere is sitting with his mouth slightly open, absolutely motionless. Daaria has her arms wrapped around her knees and her chin resting on them, and her eyes are fixed on her grandfather's face. Byötür, who is sixteen and far too old to be impressed by such things, is leaning forward on his bench, and his hands are clenched. Józef, the guest, has taken off his spectacles and is holding them forgotten in his lap.

And Akulina, beside the fire, has her eyes closed. She has heard this olonkho perhaps fifty times in her life, from her husband and from his father before him. She knows every turn of it. She is not listening for what happens next. She is listening the way you listen to rain on a roof, or to the sea, for the pleasure of the sound itself, and for all the winters it carries with it.

The fire murmurs underneath the voice. The cows breathe through the wall. And you are warm, and still, and very far from anywhere you have ever been.

It is late now, well past ten, and the house has settled into that particular quiet that comes over a room after a long story. You feel it settle over you too. The edges of everything have gone soft. The firelight is lower, redder. Your eyelids are heavy. Somewhere a log shifts in the hearth with a small, sighing fall, and a few sparks rise up into the dark of the chimney and are gone.

Historians and scholars of the olonkho do not all agree about how old it really is. Some believe its roots go back a thousand years or more, to the time before the Sakha came north, when their ancestors lived among other Turkic and Mongolic peoples on the steppes and in the mountains to the south, and they find echoes of those older lands in the epic's horses, its warriors and its gods. Others point out that oral epics are always changing, that every singer reshapes them, and that the olonkho as it was sung in the eighteen eighties had been formed by centuries of life in this cold valley. Both views can stand together. It is an old song, sung in a new place, again and again.

At last, Kuolai's voice slows, and softens, and comes to rest. The hero has made camp on the shore of a great sea, under a sky full of stars, and his marvelous horse is grazing beside him, and the danger is far away for now.

The old man opens his eyes. He smiles, a little tired, and reaches for his tea.

For a moment, nobody moves. Then Miitere lets out a long breath that he seems to have been holding for an hour.

"Is that all?" he says. "What happens next?"

"Next time," says his grandfather.

"Tomorrow?"

"Perhaps tomorrow. Perhaps the day after. An olonkho cannot be hurried. If you hurry it, the hero gets cross."

Józef is wiping his spectacles again, though they are not fogged at all. "Thank you," he says, simply, in Sakha. Then, after a moment, "I would like to write some of it down. Another night. If you will let me."

Kuolai considers him. "You may write it," he says. "But the words on paper will be only half of it. The other half is the fire, and the night, and the faces. You cannot write those."

"No," says Józef quietly. "I cannot."

In the twentieth century, one of the longest olonkho of all, the tale of Nyurgun Bootur the Swift, would be written down in full by the Sakha writer Platon Oyunsky, and printed, so that children all over Yakutia could read it. But Kuolai has never seen a printed olonkho, and would not much care for one. To him the story lives in the voice, in the breath, in the fire, and in the faces turned toward him.

Byötür has gone to the shelf and taken down a small iron instrument, shaped like a horseshoe with a thin steel tongue between its arms. It is a khomus, a Sakha jaw harp. He holds it to his lips and plucks the tongue with one finger, and a strange, twanging, humming sound fills the room, rising and falling as he changes the shape of his mouth. He plays the sound of a horse galloping, then a stream running, then the wind in the larches. It is a soft, buzzing, otherworldly music, and Miitere, who has heard it every day of his life, listens to it as if he had never heard it before.

Akulina has opened her eyes. She is looking at her husband across the fire, and there is something in her face that the children rarely see.

"The first time I heard you sing," she says to him, quietly, "I was fifteen. It was at the summer festival, by the river. You sang all night, and I sat at the back, behind my mother, and I did not sleep at all."

"You fell asleep halfway through," says Kuolai.

"I did not."

"You did. Your head was on your mother's shoulder. I saw it. I sang the best part of the whole story, the battle on the iron mountain, and you were snoring."

"I was resting my eyes," says Akulina, with great dignity, and the children shriek with laughter, and the old man grins at her, and for a moment, across the low fire, they are fifteen and twenty-one again, in the long white night of a northern summer, with the whole of their lives in front of them.

You close your eyes to listen to the khomus. With your eyes shut, the sound seems to come from inside your own head, a soft metallic hum that buzzes in your teeth and your cheekbones. It rises, and you hear hooves on frozen ground. It wavers, and you hear water running under ice. It sinks very low, and you hear the wind moving through the tops of the larches far away. When you open your eyes again, the room is exactly as it was, the low fire, the faces, the shadows, but for a moment it seems to you that you have been somewhere else entirely, riding fast across a summer meadow with the whole sky open above you.

Maaria, without being asked, has brought out a little of the evening's fresh cream, whipped with a few frozen berries saved from the autumn, and passes it around in small bowls. It is cold and sweet and sharp, and tastes, a little, of summer.

Józef eats his slowly, looking at the fire.

"It is very late to cross the alaas," says Uibaan. "There is room on the bench by the door. Stay."

"I would be glad to," says Józef.

That is settled, then. In this country, nobody sends a guest out into the night if the night is cold, and the night is always cold. Maaria fetches an extra sheepskin and a fur coat and lays them on the bench near the door.

Akulina rises, and goes to the hearth, and looks at the fire carefully, the way a doctor looks at a patient. It has burned down to a deep bed of glowing embers, with small, low flames running over them. She sets one more log upright at the back of the kamelek, not to blaze, but to burn slowly through the next hour, and the embers glow red and gold around it, steady and warm.

## Part Ten — Toward Midnight: Making the House Ready for Sleep

It is nearly midnight now. The khomus has been put back on its shelf, the cream bowls are empty, and a slow, contented heaviness has settled over everyone in the room.

Miitere has already fallen asleep once, sitting up, with his head against Daaria's shoulder, and been woken, and protested that he was not asleep at all. It is time.

Here is a small question for this part. In a house of one room, with eight people in it tonight, where does everyone sleep?

The answer runs all around the walls. The wide wooden benches we have been sitting on all evening are not only seats. They are beds. In a Sakha house, these sleeping benches were built into the walls on every side, broad enough for a person to lie down at full length, and they were often divided into separate places by low wooden partitions, like a row of open stalls, so that each sleeper or each couple had a little space of their own. Some households hung curtains of hide or cloth across the front of each place, to make a small, dark, private room within the room.

Each place had its meaning. The places along the wall opposite the door, farthest from the cold and nearest the honored corner, belonged to the eldest. The married couple of the house slept near them. Children slept with their parents, or close beside them. And the place nearest the door, which was the coldest, was given to a guest, or to a young man, or to whoever was strongest and least likely to mind a draft.

So that is the answer. Everyone sleeps on the benches along the walls, each in their own place, with the eldest farthest from the cold and the guest by the door.

Here is a gentle detail. The bedding was made from the animals and the meadows. There were cattle hides and horse hides laid on the planks of the bench. There were mattresses stuffed with dry hay or soft grass. There were pillows filled with feathers, or with hair. And for covering, there were blankets of fur, and above all, blankets made from the skins of hares, sewn together in a patchwork, light and soft and wonderfully warm. Hare fur is delicate, and tears easily, but it is so light that a sleeper can heap on layer after layer without feeling crushed. In many Sakha houses, the hare-skin blanket was the softest thing the family owned.

Our source moment for this part goes back sixty-five years before our night. In October of 1820, a restless young officer of the British navy named John Dundas Cochrane rode into Yakutsk. He had set out to travel right around the world by land, as far as he could, mostly on foot and on horseback, and with very little money. He had crossed most of Russia and Siberia to get here. In his book, Narrative of a Pedestrian Journey through Russia and Siberian Tartary, published in London in 1824, he wrote of his time in this country with real feeling, and with open admiration for the toughness and the hospitality of the Sakha guides and households who carried him on through the winter. On the roads beyond Yakutsk he would suffer badly from cold and hunger himself. He knew what a fire and a bench and a fur blanket could mean.

Now, let us make the house ready.

You are standing near the hearth, watching Akulina at work. This is the most careful thing she does all day. She kneels before the open mouth of the kamelek, and with a short iron poker she gently draws the remaining embers together into a deep, glowing heap at the back of the hearth. She does not stir them hard. She coaxes them, the way you would tuck a blanket around a sleeping child. The embers glow red and orange, and pulse softly as the air moves over them, as if they were breathing.

Then she lays a single good log of larch alongside the heap, not upright this time, but resting low, half buried in the embers, where it will not blaze, but will smolder and glow, slowly, for hours. You feel the heat on your face, steady and quiet now, not the leaping heat of the evening fire, but a deep, settled warmth, like the warmth of a stone that has lain all day in the sun.

Uibaan goes through to the barn with a burning splinter for a last look. You can hear him in there, moving softly from stall to stall, murmuring to the cows. A cow answers him with a low sound in her throat. He comes back, and shuts the connecting door gently, but not completely, leaving a small gap so that the warmth of the house can still reach the animals, and so that any sound from them will reach the house.

Byötür brings in the last armful of wood from outside, and the cold that comes in with him is so intense that it seems to fall to the floor and lie there, a pool of chill around your ankles, before the fire slowly drinks it up. He stacks the wood by the hearth, ready for the morning. Then he goes to the door, and checks the latch, and pulls the heavy hide curtain across it, and tucks its lower edge in against the floor.

Maaria lays out the bedding. On each bench she spreads a hide, and over it a soft mattress of hay, and over that the furs. The hare-skin blankets come down from where they have hung all day near the roof, warm from the rising heat of the fire. She shakes each one out, and the fur ripples and glows in the low light.

She has made a place for you too, on the bench near the hearth, between the old people and the children. You sit down on it, and feel the give of the hay mattress beneath you, soft and slightly crackling, with a faint sweet smell of summer meadows that rises as you settle. You pull off your boots and set them by the fire, with the grass inside them, so that they will be warm and dry in the morning.

You lie down. The hide beneath the mattress is firm, the hay is soft, and the furs are heavy and warm from hanging near the roof. You draw the hare-skin blanket up over you, and it is so light that at first you hardly feel it, and then, slowly, you feel the warmth of it gathering around you, as your own heat is caught and held in its fur. You pull it up to your chin. Then, because your nose is a little cold, you pull it up over your nose as well, and breathe the warm air under the blanket.

From where you lie, you can see the hearth. The embers glow in the mouth of the kamelek, red and orange, with small blue flames running over them now and then. The clay of the hearth around them shines faintly, warm to look at. The light they give is very low, just enough to show the shapes of the room, the dark beams overhead, the edges of the benches, the soft humps of sleepers under their furs.

And you can hear the house. The faint tick and settle of the embers. Through the barn wall, the cows, slow and heavy, one breathing out as another breathes in. Somewhere, a calf shifting in the straw. And under it all, the silence of the winter night outside, pressing softly against the thick clay walls, so deep and so complete that it is almost a sound itself.

You are not cold. That is the thing you notice, lying there. With fifty below on the other side of the wall, you are not cold at all.

Historians of sleep do not all agree about how people in long-night countries like this one actually slept. Some argue that in many places before electric light, people often slept in two parts, a first sleep in the early night and a second sleep toward morning, with a quiet hour of waking in between, when they might tend the fire, check the animals, pray, or talk softly. Others caution that this pattern was not the same everywhere, and that in a house like this, with fire and cattle to tend, sleep was shaped more by the work of the night than by any fixed rule. Both views can be gently held. In this house, at least, someone always wakes in the night, for the fire.

Miitere, woken briefly by the moving about, sits up in a nest of fur and watches his grandmother at the hearth with sleepy, serious eyes.

"Is the fire going to sleep now?" he asks.

"Only resting," says Akulina. "Like you. It will keep one eye open."

He lies down again, satisfied, and pulls the blanket over his head.

Kuolai stands before the icon in the corner for a moment, and crosses himself slowly, the Orthodox way, and bows his head. Then he turns, and nods once toward the hearth as well, a small private bow to the fire. He sees no conflict between the two. Most Sakha of his generation did not.

He lowers himself onto his place on the bench of honor, with a long sigh, and Akulina tucks the furs around his feet.

"Your feet are cold," she says.

"My feet are always cold," says Kuolai. "That is why I married you."

She clicks her tongue at him, but she wraps an extra fold of fur around his ankles anyway.

Daaria and Miitere share a place beside their parents' bench, under one big hare-skin blanket. Miitere is asleep almost before he is lying down, curled into a small round shape, with only the top of his head showing. Daaria lies awake a little longer, her eyes open in the firelight, listening to the sounds through the barn wall, thinking about the white-faced cow, and the calf that may come.

Byötür has the place nearest the barn door. He lies on his back with his arms behind his head, and looks up at the dark beams, and thinks about the horses out in the forest pasture, and whether the fox was going to the lake or coming back. Then he stops thinking about anything at all.

Józef settles on the bench by the outer door, under the extra sheepskin and the borrowed coat. He takes off his spectacles and folds them carefully and puts them inside his boot, where they will not be stepped on. Then he lies still, and listens, as he has learned to listen in his two winters in this country. The fire. The cows. The breathing of the family. The great silence outside.

"Good night," he says softly, in Sakha.

"Good night," says Maaria, from the dark.

Uibaan lies down beside his wife, and pulls the furs up over both of them. His hand finds hers under the blanket. Neither of them says anything. They have been married eighteen years, and they do not need to.

Akulina is the last. She stands for a moment beside the hearth, looking at the embers, the low, glowing log, the faint wisps of smoke rising into the chimney. When the smoke has thinned almost to nothing, she reaches up with a long pole, and partly closes the chimney, leaving it open just a crack. Now the heat of the embers will stay in the room, instead of escaping into the sky.

She listens for a moment, her head tilted. The smoke is rising, slowly, through the crack. The embers are glowing. The house is sealed. Everything is as it should be.

She will wake once, perhaps twice, in the night, to look at the fire. She does not need to set any alarm for it. Her body has done this for fifty winters, and it will wake her when it is time, as surely as the cows will wake Maaria before dawn.

Then she lies down on her place beside her husband, close to the fire, where she can see the hearth from her pillow, as she has every night for most of her life.

The kamelek glows softly in the dark, a small red heart at the center of the sleeping house, and its warmth spreads out slowly across the room to every bench, and every sleeper.

## Part Eleven — Around Two in the Morning: The Whisper of the Stars

It is two in the morning, and the house is asleep, all but one.

Akulina has woken, as she knew she would. She lies still for a moment under the hare-skin blanket, listening. Her husband is breathing slowly beside her. The children are a small warm hill under their furs. From the bench by the door comes the faint, steady breathing of the guest, and through the barn wall, the long, slow sighs of the cows.

She sits up, and slips her feet into her warm boots by the hearth, and pulls a fur coat around her shoulders.

Here is a small question for this part. What is the coldest hour of a winter night?

It is not midnight, as many people imagine. In a still, clear winter like this one, the land goes on losing its warmth into the open sky all night long, hour after hour, with no sun to give any of it back. So the cold keeps deepening right through the small hours, and it is usually coldest just before dawn, or even a little after, before the low sun has had any time to work. Right now, outside these walls, the cold is still deepening, and it will go on deepening for hours yet.

So that is the answer, simply. The coldest hour is the last hour of the night, near dawn.

Akulina kneels by the hearth. The log she laid low in the embers has burned through its middle and broken into two glowing halves. The heap of embers has sunk and grayed at its edges. But at its heart it is still bright and deep. She takes a few small, split pieces of dry larch from the pile, and lays them carefully against the glowing core, and blows on them very gently, once, twice. A small flame appears, hesitates, and catches.

She adds a larger piece, and lets it settle. Then she reaches for the ice by the door, to fill the kettle for the morning, and finds that the pot is nearly empty. She will fetch a piece from the stack outside.

Let us go with her, for a moment, as far as the doorway.

You stand up from your bench, wrapping a fur around you, and follow her quietly to the door. She lifts the edge of the hide curtain, and unlatches the door, and opens it just a hand's width.

The cold comes in like a solid thing. It flows over the threshold and pours down across your feet. You pull the fur across your face and step to the gap beside her and look out.

The night is enormous, and absolutely clear. There is no moon tonight. And so the stars are everywhere, thicker than you have ever seen them, from the black line of the forest all the way up over the roof, crowded and glittering, some white, some faintly blue, some faintly gold. The snow of the alaas lies pale and gray beneath them, lit by nothing but starlight, and the lake is a smooth, dim shape beyond it. Nothing moves. There is no wind. There is no sound at all.

The air does not feel like air. It feels thin and hard and bright, like the inside of a bell. When you move your head, the fur at your collar crackles faintly with frost. The skin around your eyes tightens. Your eyelashes begin, already, to grow heavy and white. And yet, standing here in the doorway, with the warmth of the house at your back and the old woman beside you, you are not afraid of it. It is simply there, vast and still and beautiful, the way the sea is there when you stand on the shore.

Far off, across the lake, a tree in the forest gives a single sharp crack, as the sap inside it freezes and splits the wood. The sound rings out over the snow and dies away into the silence, and the silence closes over it again.

Akulina breathes out, slowly, through the fur at her mouth.

And then you hear it.

It is very faint. A soft, dry, rustling whisper, like the sound of sand poured slowly onto paper, or of silk being drawn across silk. It lasts only a moment, and then it fades. She breathes out again, and there it is again, that tiny hiss and murmur, close to her face, and then gone.

You breathe out too, carefully, into the starlit air. And you hear it from your own breath. A faint, crackling whisper, just in front of your lips, as the warm moisture of your breathing freezes instantly into a cloud of tiny ice crystals, and the crystals brush against each other as they form and fall.

The Sakha have a name for this sound. They call it the whisper of the stars.

It happens only on the very coldest nights, when the air falls to around fifty-eight degrees below zero Fahrenheit, which is fifty below on the Celsius scale, or lower still. At that temperature, the breath freezes so fast that it makes a sound, and the sound seems to come out of the sky itself, on a still night full of stars. It is easy to understand why the sound was given that name. On a night like this, with no wind and no moon, it truly seems to come down from the sky.

Akulina chips a piece of ice from the stack, quickly, with the little axe that hangs by the door, and brings it in. She closes the door, and latches it, and pulls the curtain back across, and tucks its edge against the floor. The cold that came in lies on the floor for a little while, and then the fire takes it, slowly, and it is gone.

So now we can answer the last of our three questions. How does anyone sleep, when the air outside can freeze your breath into a whisper you can hear?

They sleep the way this whole household has lived through this whole night, with everything working together. The thick walls of larch, plastered with clay and dung, hold the cold back. The earth roof and the deep snow on top of it hold the warmth in. The doorway is low and curtained, and the windows are small slabs of ice, sealed into the walls with frozen water. The fire is banked low, its embers deep and glowing, and the chimney is nearly closed, so that the heat of the embers stays in the room. On the other side of the back wall, five cows and an ox give off their gentle warmth all night long. On the benches, people lie on hay and hide, under furs and soft hare-skin, close together, sharing their warmth as well. And one person, the keeper of the fire, wakes in the deep of the night, as Akulina has just done, to feed the embers, so that the fire never sleeps, and the house never grows cold.

That is how they sleep. Not by fighting the cold, but by quietly arranging everything they have, walls and fire and cattle and fur and each other, so that the cold simply cannot reach them.

Sieroszewski, who lived through twelve such winters before he wrote his book of 1896, had stood at doorways like this one, on nights like this one. And he understood, as every Sakha family understood, that the warmest place in the world, on such a night, was a bench near a banked fire, under a hare-skin blanket, with the cattle breathing beyond the wall.

There are those who say the sound comes from the crystals rubbing together, and those who say it is the tiny crackle of each droplet freezing. Perhaps it is both. It does not matter very much, tonight. It is a small, beautiful sound, and it belongs to the stars.

Akulina sets the piece of ice in the kettle, at the edge of the hearth, where it will melt slowly through the rest of the night. She adds one more small log to the fire, and watches it catch, and then settles it down low, so that it will not blaze. The flames sink back into the embers. The glow deepens.

She looks around the room, once, the way she always does.

Kuolai is asleep on his back, his mouth a little open, his white beard rising and falling on the fur.

Uibaan is asleep on his side, one arm across his wife.

Maaria is asleep, her face peaceful, a strand of black hair across her cheek.

Byötür has kicked off half his blanket, as he always does. Akulina tucks it back around him. He does not wake.

Daaria and Miitere are asleep together, tangled up like two puppies, under the big hare-skin blanket.

And by the door, the guest is asleep too, one hand resting on his chest, his spectacles safe in his boot.

Through the barn wall, a cow sighs.

Akulina slips off her boots and sets them back beside the hearth. She lies down on her place, close to the fire, and draws the furs up over her shoulders. The warmth gathers around her slowly. Her eyes rest on the embers for a while, red and gold and very quiet.

Outside, the stars go on whispering to each other, but nobody in this house can hear them now.

Inside, it is warm.

The fire breathes softly in the mouth of the kamelek. The ice in the kettle ticks, very faintly, as it melts. The cows breathe. The family breathes.

And the old woman's eyes grow heavy, and close.

Through the ice of the east window, the stars shine faintly, blurred and soft, the way they looked to Miitere earlier in the evening, as if seen from under clear water. The window holds. It will hold all night, and all winter, until the swallows come back.

On the shelf, the milk from the evening has set in its wide wooden vessel, and a thick skin of cream has risen to its surface, pale and smooth, ready for the morning churn.

On the floor by the hearth, the boots stand in a row, each pair with its fresh dry grass inside, warming. Seven pairs, and one more for the guest. Small ones and large ones. Old ones, mended many times, and new ones, still stiff.

Along the walls, the sleepers lie in their places, each one exactly where they belong on the map of warmth that this house has drawn, without words, over all its winters. The eldest nearest the fire. The children between their parents. The guest by the door.

The night goes on, slow and dark and peaceful. Hours of it still lie ahead, long, gentle hours, with nothing in them at all but warmth, and breathing, and the soft red glow of the hearth.

The kettle at the edge of the hearth gives a tiny, almost silent click as the ice inside it shifts. A drop of meltwater runs down its side and hisses faintly on the warm clay, and is gone.

In the barn, the ox lets out a long, low breath, and lays his heavy head down on the straw.

In the calf pen, the black heifer twitches one ear in her sleep, and is still again.

The pine splinter that Daaria wedged into the barn wall burned out long ago. There is no light in there at all now, only warmth, and breathing, and the sweet dry smell of hay.

Out on the alaas, the hay stacks stand under their caps of snow, waiting for the ox and the sledge. The ice blocks stand in their glittering wall beside the door. The lake lies sleeping under its thick white roof. The forest is still. Everything out there is resting too, in its own way, holding its breath until the sun comes back.

You are lying on your own bench, under your own blanket. Your feet are warm. Your hands are warm. Your face, half hidden in the fur, is warm. You can see the embers from where you lie, glowing. You can hear the cows through the wall.

There is nothing you need to do. There is nowhere you need to be.

The fire is kept. The house is sealed. The cold is outside, and it cannot come in.

Let yourself sink a little deeper into the hay and the fur. Let your breathing slow. Let the soft sounds of the sleeping house carry you, the way a slow river carries a leaf.

In the hearth, the embers settle with a tiny, sighing sound, and glow a little brighter for a moment, and then a little softer, keeping their quiet watch over everyone asleep in the warm dark.

## Part Twelve — The Small Hours Toward First Light: The Fire That Never Sleeps

The small hours pass slowly over the sleeping house, four o'clock, then five, and the stars wheel very gently overhead, and nothing in the world is moving at all.

Here is one last small question, a soft one. Who will be the first to wake, when the morning comes?

It will be Maaria. It is always Maaria. Long before the light, her body will remember the cows, and she will open her eyes in the dark and lie still for a moment, listening to them through the wall. Then she will rise and go to them. But that is still a long way off. For now, she sleeps, and the cows sleep, and the house sleeps around them.

So that is our answer. The mother wakes first, for the cows. But not yet. Not for a long while yet.

In the hearth, the fire is very low now. It is not a blaze, and it is not a flame. It is a bed of deep red embers, glowing softly in the open mouth of the kamelek, with one dark log lying across them, glowing along its underside. Every so often, a breath of air moves through the nearly closed chimney, and the embers brighten, very slightly, and then fade again. It is like watching someone breathe in their sleep.

The clay around the hearth is warm. It has been soaking up heat since the autumn, and it gives it back now, slowly, quietly, into the room. The air near the benches is still and soft. The walls hold it in. The snow on the roof holds it in. The furs hold it in.

We have spent a whole night in this house, you and I. We came in from the low red sun of the afternoon. We watched the hay brought home on the ox sledge. We saw the ice set into the window, and heard the water freeze around its edges. We ate salamat, and gave the first spoonful to the fire. We followed the women into the warm, breathing barn. We sat by the hearth while the old man sang of summer. And we stood in the doorway, under the stars, and heard our own breath whisper.

Now there is nothing left to do but rest.

A long time ago, travelers and exiles came to this valley from far away, like the Polish exile Sieroszewski, whose book about the Sakha was printed in 1896, and the Swede Stadling, who wrote of ice windows in 1901. They wrote down what they saw, so that people who would never come here could imagine it. But the people who truly knew these nights were the families who lived them, winter after winter, around fires like this one, keeping the warmth alive for each other.

Some who write about the past see a life like this as hard, and some see it as good. Perhaps the fairest thing to say, tonight, is that it was both, and that on this particular night, in this particular house, it is simply warm.

Let us lie here a little longer, you and I.

You are lying on your bench, near the hearth, under the soft hare-skin blanket. The hay beneath you has shaped itself to you, and you have sunk into it, and it holds you gently. The fur over you is light, and warm, and smells faintly of smoke and of summer meadows. Your feet are warm. Your hands are tucked against your chest, and they are warm too.

You can see the embers from where you lie, a low red glow in the dark, steady and quiet. You watch them for a while. They do not flicker much now. They simply glow, the way a coal glows, the way a lantern glows in a distant window.

You can hear the house. The faint, slow ticking of the clay as it cools and warms. The tiny sounds of the ice melting in the kettle, a drop, and then, after a long while, another drop. Through the barn wall, the cows, breathing slow and deep, and now and then a small rustle of straw. On the benches around you, the family breathing, each in their own rhythm, some slow, some slower, all of them soft.

And beyond the walls, the silence. The great white silence of the winter night, lying over the alaas and the lake and the forest, all the way to the end of the world. It cannot come in. It only lies outside, quiet, keeping its distance, the way the sea keeps its distance from a warm house on the shore.

There is nothing you need to do. Nothing you need to remember. Nothing you need to watch for. The fire is kept. The cows are fed. The door is latched. The window holds.

Let your breathing slow, until it matches the breathing of the house. In, and out. Slow, and slower. The way the cows breathe. The way the embers breathe.

Around five o'clock, Akulina stirs once more. She does not fully wake. She lifts her head a little from the pillow, and looks at the hearth, and sees the red glow there, steady and deep, and that is enough. Her head sinks back down. Her eyes close. The fire does not need her yet. It will keep until the morning.

You think back, drowsily, over the long evening, the way you might turn the pages of a picture book you know very well, without reading the words.

There is the ox, white with frost, leaning into his load of hay. There is Uibaan's beard, melting, dripping onto the floor. There is the new window, clear as water, with the stars shining through it, and Miitere's nose pressed against the ice. There is the golden salamat in its wooden bowl, and the old woman's hand tipping the first spoonful into the flames. There is Maaria, crouched beside a cow in the dark barn, the milk ringing in the pail. There is the young guest at the door, blind in his fogged spectacles, laughing. There is the old man's face, lit from below, singing of summer. And there is the doorway, and the stars, and the soft whisper of a breath turning to ice.

All of it is gone quiet now. All of it is folded away, like the furs and the bowls and the needles, until another day.

Somewhere in the deep of the night, Miitere murmurs something in his sleep, a word or two, perhaps the name of the hero from the old man's story. Daaria, half asleep beside him, puts an arm over him without waking, and he quiets again.

Kuolai turns over on his bench, slowly, with a small creak of wood, and settles again, and sighs.

The cow with the white face shifts in her stall, and lies down in the straw, carefully, heavily, and lets out a long breath, and is still. Her calf is not coming tonight. Perhaps tomorrow. Perhaps the next night. There is no hurry.

The fire breathes.
Let the picture of this house settle in your mind, the way snow settles on a roof. The low walls, thick with clay. The flat roof, heaped with white. The small square windows of ice, glowing faintly from within. The column of smoke, rising straight into the stars. And inside, the warmth, and the sleepers, and the soft red heart of the fire.

You do not need to hold on to any of it. You can let it go, gently, and it will still be there. It will always be there, this one warm house in the long winter night.


You feel very heavy now, and very light at the same time. The bench holds you. The hay holds you. The fur lies over you like a warm, soft hand. Your thoughts are slowing, drifting apart, like the last wisps of smoke rising into the dark of the chimney.

The cows breathe. The family breathes. The embers glow.

Somewhere far away, across the alaas, in the house of the family on the other side, another old woman is sleeping beside another hearth, and another fire is glowing in the dark. And beyond that house, another, and another, scattered across the white valley under the stars, each one a small warm light in the great winter night, each one kept.

The hours pass. Five o'clock. Six. The stars are still bright over the roof. Morning, in this country, in January, comes late and slowly. The first gray light will not touch the eastern sky until after eight. The sun itself will not rise above the trees until nearly nine.

But it will come. It always comes. And when it does, the first faint light will fall on the ice of the east window, the new clear slab from the deep end of the lake, and the ice will glow, softly, silver and pale, like a small moon set into the wall of the house.

And when that light comes, the fire will still be burning. It will have burned all night, low and patient, kept alive by an old woman's hands. And it will burn all through the day that follows, and the night after that, and every day and every night, until the swallows come back to the alaas in May, and the ice in the windows melts back into the lake.

Slowly, the stars over the alaas begin, very faintly, to pale. Not yet light. Only a little less dark. The edge of the sky above the eastern forest turns from black to the deepest blue, the color of a winter lake under ice. The house sleeps on beneath it, quiet and low and white with snow, its single thread of smoke rising straight and thin into the stillness.

Here they all are, then, one last time, asleep in the warm dark.

Kuolai, the old singer, seventy years old, asleep on the bench of honor with his white beard on the fur, dreaming, perhaps, of a hero and a horse and a land of endless summer.

Akulina, the keeper of the fire, asleep beside him, close to the hearth, where she can see it the moment she opens her eyes.

Uibaan, the quiet cattle man, asleep on his side, one arm across his wife.

Maaria, who milks and churns and sews, asleep with a strand of black hair across her cheek, her body already, faintly, listening for the cows.

Byötür, sixteen, asleep with his blanket half kicked off again, and not cold at all.

Daaria, twelve, asleep with her arm around her brother, dreaming of a new calf by the fire.

Miitere, seven, asleep, curled like a small round stone under the big hare-skin blanket, with only the top of his head showing.

And Józef, the guest, a long way from home, asleep on the bench by the door, warm for the first time in a long while.

And the household dogs, curled nose to tail in the straw by the outer barn door, asleep and dreaming dog dreams, their paws twitching now and then.

Through the wall, the cows sleep in their stalls, and the ox sleeps at the far end of the barn, and the calf sleeps in her pen in the softest hay.

And in the middle of it all, at the heart of the house, the open clay fireplace glows. Its embers are deep and red and steady. Its warmth spreads out, slowly, across the floor and up the walls, over every bench and every sleeper, into every corner of the room. It has not gone out since the autumn. It will not go out tonight.

If you enjoyed this night with us, you are very welcome to like and subscribe, so that we can spend another night together soon. And if there is a time or place you would love to spend a night in, leave it in the comments, because your ideas truly help.

Next time, we will visit a very different kind of house, in a very different corner of the world, with another household keeping watch until morning.

But for now, the kamelek is burning, low and gold, and the house is warm, and everyone in it is safe.

Sleep well, friend. Good night.

### Verification notes

Checked through a review that quotes the book, not against the book's own page.

- **Stated from standard knowledge without a fresh search.** Balagan construction (sloping log walls, flat earth roof, east-facing door). Tar, butter in birch-bark vessels and communal autumn net-fishing for carp under the ice. Dung smudge fires against mosquitoes. Fur tribute (yasak). The exile route down the Lena from Irkutsk. Edward Piekarski's Sakha dictionary (exiled 1881). Platon Oyunsky's *Nyurgun Bootur the Swift*. The khomus. Calves brought indoors on bitter nights.
