# How Koreans Survived Winter on Fire-Heated Floors | History for Sleep

## Part One — Around Half Past Three in the Afternoon: The Fire Mouth Wakes

It is January in the year 1790, on a low hill above the Han River, just outside the walls of Hanyang, the royal capital of Korea. The river below is frozen from bank to bank, gray and silent. The wind comes down from the north and finds every gap in your sleeves. Behind a tiled roof, in a kitchen floored with packed earth, a woman kneels in front of a small black opening at the foot of a clay hearth. She strikes a spark, feeds it pine needles, and the fire mouth of the house begins, very softly, to breathe.

Not the Korea of the costume dramas and the picture books, all palace gates and silk robes and whispering ministers. Just one ordinary household, on one ordinary winter night, in one ordinary house of wood and clay and tile.

And without a few old tricks, you would not have lasted until morning, not in a house with paper doors, on a hill where the frost goes a foot into the ground.

So tonight I would like you to carry three small questions with you. The first is this. How can one fire, burning in a kitchen, warm the floor of the bedroom next door, so well that an old woman can sleep on it through a January night? The second. Why does the whole village make its winter kimchi on the same few days, every year, as if someone had rung a bell? And the third. Who gets the warmest place on the floor, and why?

These are not three separate questions. They are three doors into the same house, on the same night, and we will open each of them in its own time.

This family never quite existed under one name, but every detail of their night is real.

So let your head grow heavy now. Let your shoulders loosen and drop. Your eyes may close whenever they like, and the little fire in the kitchen will keep breathing without you. Let us go back, gently, to a cold, bright afternoon in the twelfth month of the old Korean calendar, and to a house on a hill above a frozen river. So let us begin.

The woman at the fire mouth is called Lady Park. She is forty-four years old, and she is the mistress of this house. In the records of her time she would not be written down with a given name at all. She would simply be Park-ssi, a woman of the Park family, married into the household of the Kim. That was the custom for women of her kind in Joseon Korea, and she would not have thought it strange.

She is wearing a short padded jacket of faded blue cotton, a long full skirt of dark gray, and over her hair a quilted winter cap with a band of black silk. Her hands are red with cold.

The opening she is kneeling in front of is called the agungi. It is the fire mouth of the floor. It is about as wide as a large loaf of bread and about as high, a dark arched hole in the front of a long clay platform. On top of that platform, set into round holes, sit two great iron cauldrons with heavy wooden lids. The platform is called the buttumak, and it runs along one wall of the kitchen like a low clay counter, smooth and pale and polished by years of hands.

Here is the first small thing to notice. The kitchen floor is lower than the rest of the house. You step down into it, two feet or more, from the yard. Why would anyone build a kitchen in a hole?

The answer is the whole secret of this house, and we will learn it slowly through the evening. But the simplest part of it is this. Fire and hot smoke want to rise. If the fire mouth sits low, well below the floor of the room beside the kitchen, then the flames and the heat will not only climb into the cauldron above. They will also be drawn sideways and upward, through a hidden throat in the back of the hearth, and under the floor of the room next door. The kitchen is sunk so that the bedroom can float, warm, above the fire.

The whole household is out and about at this hour, in the last of the light, so let us meet them, one by one.

On the warm floor of the main inner room, the room just beyond the kitchen wall, sits the eldest person in the house. She is Lady Yun, Yun-ssi, and she is seventy-two. She is the widowed mother of the master of the house. She came to this house as a bride of sixteen, more than half a century ago, and she has seen three kings on the throne. Her hair is white and pulled back in a low knot, held with a long silver pin. Just now she is sorting dried red peppers into a flat basket, very slowly, by touch as much as by sight.

Beside her, kneeling, with a bundle of white cotton in her lap, is a girl of nine with a round face and a long black braid tied with a red ribbon. She is Suni, the youngest child of the house. Suni is only a home name, a little name, the kind a child is called by her mother and her grandmother. She is supposed to be learning to sew a straight seam. She is mostly watching her grandmother's hands.

Across the yard, in the separate men's quarters near the gate, is the master of the house. He is Kim Myeong-hwan, and he is forty-seven. He comes from a modest family of scholars. Long ago, as a young man, he passed the lower civil examinations in the capital, and so he is entitled to a certain respect and a certain hat. But he has never held office. He teaches the boys of the village their characters in a little schoolroom, and he looks after the family's rice fields, which are farmed by tenants on the flat land by the river.

With him, at a low writing table, is his eldest son, Kim Hyeong-ju, who is nineteen. Hyeong-ju is studying for the same examinations his father once passed, and he hopes, as his father once hoped, to go further. He was married last spring.

His bride is in the kitchen now, behind Lady Park, filling a gourd ladle from a big water jar. She is Lady Jeong, Jeong-ssi, and she is eighteen. She came to this house from her own family's village, a day's walk to the south, in a red and green bridal robe, riding in a closed wooden sedan chair. This is her first winter in her husband's home. Every kitchen has its own ways, and she is still learning these ones.

And out in the lane, coming slowly up the hill with a huge load of brushwood and pine needles on his back, is the seventh member of the household. He is Dolsoe, and he is thirty-six. He is not a relative. He is the hired man, who works for the family by the year, for a wage paid mostly in rice, and he lives in a small room by the front gate, next to the ox. He has been out on the hillsides since noon.

So that is the whole household. Seven people, three generations, and one hired man, under one tiled roof.

We know a great deal about households like this one, because the Joseon state counted them. Every three years, in the years of the rat, the rabbit, the horse and the rooster, every household in the kingdom was supposed to hand in a written return to the local office. It named the head of the house, his age and his rank or occupation. It named his wife, by her family name, with her age. It named their children, and anyone else who lived under the roof, servants included. And it named the head's four ancestors, his father, grandfather and great-grandfather, and his mother's father, and the same four ancestors for his wife. The office copied all of this into great register books, and sent one copy of the return back to the household to keep.

The year just ended, 1789, was a rooster year. So only a few months ago, Kim Myeong-hwan sat at his writing table and set his household down in brush and ink, Lady Yun and Lady Park and the young couple and little Suni, and the hired man too. A good many of those old registers survive. The registers of one county in the south, called Danseong, run for long stretches of the late Joseon period, and historians have used them to follow families through generations, one household at a time, the way you might follow a stream from pool to pool.

But the fire mouth is older than any register.

Long before this house was built, long before there was a Joseon kingdom at all, people in the north of the Korean peninsula and the lands beyond it had learned to warm themselves by running smoke under the place where they sat and slept. The old history of the Tang dynasty in China, compiled in the year 945, includes a description of the kingdom of Goguryeo, which once ruled the north of Korea and much of what is now northeast China. It says that most of the common people there were poor, and that in the winter months they all built long pits, and lit a low fire beneath them to take their warmth.

Long pits, with a fire beneath. Archaeologists have found the remains of exactly that kind of heating in Goguryeo houses and forts: low clay channels running along one or two walls of a room, carrying smoke from a hearth at one end to a chimney at the other, with a raised bench of warm stone above. It was not yet a whole heated floor. It was a warm ledge along the wall. But it was the beginning.

You are standing in that kitchen now, just behind Lady Jeong, at the top of the three earth steps. The air down here is cold and smells of old smoke, wet clay and pine. Light comes in through the open door behind you and lies in a pale slant across the earth floor. In front of you the fire has caught. You can hear it, a soft fluttering, like a bird shaking its wings inside a box. The pine needles crackle and hiss, and a sweet, sharp scent of resin rises. Lady Park sits back on her heels and feeds in a handful of dry brushwood, then a split stick or two. A thread of smoke curls out of the fire mouth and is pulled back inside again, as if the house were breathing in. The iron lid of the great cauldron above it begins, very faintly, to tick as it warms. You hold out your hands. Already, from three feet away, you can feel the warmth on your palms and on your face, and your cold fingers begin to ache as the blood comes back into them.

In our own time, if you live in a Korean city, you very likely have a heated floor too. Most homes and apartments in South Korea today are warmed from below, by hot water running through pipes laid under the floor, and many families still sit, eat and sometimes sleep on that warm floor rather than on chairs and beds. The fire has gone. The idea is still there.

Historians do not entirely agree about where the idea first came from. Some see the heated floor as something that grew on the Korean peninsula itself, out of those long Goguryeo pits and the even older hearths before them, and became, over many centuries, a defining feature of Korean houses. Others point out that heated benches and heated platforms were also built across a wide belt of northeast Asia, in Manchuria and northern China, where the heated brick bed called the kang is still known, and they prefer to see a shared northern invention with many local forms. Both views agree on one thing. By the time of our night, the warm floor had become the center of the Korean home.

Lady Park closes the little wooden door of the cupboard where she keeps her kindling, and stands, and brushes the ash from her skirt.

"It has taken," she says to her daughter-in-law. "Now the rice."

Lady Jeong brings the washed rice in a wide brown bowl. Together they lift the heavy wooden lid of the big cauldron, tip the rice in, and add water from the gourd, Lady Park measuring by laying her flat hand on the rice and letting the water come up to the back of her knuckles. Her mother measured it that way, and so did Lady Yun, when Lady Yun was the mistress of this kitchen.

Through the wall, in the inner room, Lady Yun feels the first warmth come into the floor beneath her. It is very slight at first, just a softening of the cold under her knees. She smiles, and does not stop sorting her peppers.

"Your mother has lit the fire," she says to Suni.

"How do you know, Grandmother?"

"The floor tells me," says Lady Yun.

Suni puts her small palm flat on the yellow paper floor, near her grandmother's knee, and waits. And after a moment she feels it too, a faint, spreading warmth, like the warmth of a cat that has just gotten up and gone away.

Out by the gate, Dolsoe sets down his load with a grunt and a long breath of white steam. The gate creaks. The ox in its stall turns its great head to look at him.

And in the kitchen, under the ticking cauldron, the little fire in the fire mouth burns on, bright and orange in the dark of the clay.

## Part Two — Around a Quarter Past Four: Pine Needles on the Long Hill

The sun has dropped behind the western hills across the river, and the light on the ice below has turned from white to the color of weak tea. In the yard, Dolsoe is untying the ropes of his load.

What does a family burn under a floor, all winter, on a hill where every tree has an owner?

The common answer, in a village like this one, was almost anything that would catch. The best and most precious fuel was split wood, pine and oak, cut into lengths and stacked under the eaves to dry. But split wood cost money, and in the hills around Hanyang it was harder and harder to find. So most families burned what they could gather. They burned rice straw from their own harvest, twisted into tight bundles. They burned the dried stalks of beans and sesame and millet. They burned brushwood, the thin branches and shrubs cut from the lower slopes. And above all, in this part of Korea, they burned pine needles.

Fallen pine needles were raked up from under the trees with a wide bamboo rake, gathered into great soft heaps, and carried home on the back in loads taller than the man carrying them. They caught at the first spark and burned with a quick, hot, fragrant flame. They were perfect for starting a fire, and for the short, fierce burning that the floor of a Korean room needed twice a day.

Dolsoe carries all of this on a wooden frame called a jige. It is a kind of A-frame backpack, made from two forked branches joined with crosspieces, with straw shoulder straps padded with rags. With a jige on his back, a strong man could carry a load of brushwood that would fill a small cart. When he wanted to rest, he propped it up on a forked stick and simply stepped out of the straps, and the load stood on its own.

Here is a detail that may surprise you. In Joseon Korea, a man could be punished for cutting down a pine tree.

Pine was the timber of the kingdom. It built palaces and temples, and it built the ships of the navy and the boats that carried tax rice up the coast. So from the early years of the dynasty, the government set aside whole mountains as protected pine forests, where cutting living pines was forbidden, and it repeated and strengthened these rules again and again over the centuries. Officials and village heads were supposed to watch over the slopes. Fallen needles and dead branches might be gathered, and brush might be cut. But a man caught felling a living pine on protected ground could be beaten, fined or worse.

And still the hills around the capital grew bare. By the late eighteenth century, the slopes within a day's walk of Hanyang had been raked and cut so often that in many places only scrub and grass remained. Every morning, men and boys from the outer villages walked into the city through its great gates, carrying loads of firewood and brush on their backs, or driving oxen piled high with it, to sell in the markets. A city of many tens of thousands of people, every one of them sleeping on a warm floor, needed a mountain of fuel every winter.

That is why Dolsoe has been out on the hills since noon, and why his load is mostly needles and brush.

Here is the first foreigner of our night. In August of the year 1653, a Dutch merchant ship called the Sperwer, the Sparrowhawk, sailing for Japan, was wrecked in a storm on the coast of Jeju, the large island off the southern tip of Korea.

Of the sixty-four men on board, thirty-six came ashore alive. One of them was the ship's bookkeeper, a young man named Hendrick Hamel.

The Joseon court did not allow foreigners to leave. So Hamel and his shipmates were brought north to the capital, and later sent to live in provincial towns in the south, and they stayed in Korea for thirteen years. They learned the language. They lived in Korean houses. Some of them married. And in 1666, Hamel and seven others escaped in a small boat to Japan. There, while he waited for a ship home, Hamel wrote an account of his years in Korea, and it was printed in the Netherlands in 1668. It was the first detailed description of Korea ever published in Europe.

Hamel had spent his Korean winters on heated floors, and he described them. In one modern English translation of his account, he writes that under the floors of the rooms they heat continuously, so that they are always warm like a baker's oven. And he adds that the floors are covered with oiled paper.

Warm like a baker's oven. A Dutchman, who grew up in a country of cold stone floors and peat fires, sleeping in a box bed against a chilly wall, had found something in Korea that he thought worth writing home about.

You are on the hillside with Dolsoe, an hour or two ago, under the pines. The trees here are old and twisted, with red bark that flakes like paper, and their crowns are dark green against a pale blue sky. The ground beneath them is soft and brown with fallen needles, and when you step on it, it gives a little and crackles. The air is very cold and very still, and it smells of resin and frost. Dolsoe pulls his bamboo rake toward him in long, slow strokes, and the needles gather into a heap, whispering. His breath smokes. His cheeks are dark red. Below you, through the trees, you can see the whole frozen river spread out, with tiny black figures moving on the ice, and beyond it the gray walls and tiled roofs of the capital, and a hundred thin threads of smoke rising straight up from a hundred kitchens. Your fingers are numb inside a pair of padded cotton mittens. Your feet are cold in their straw sandals and quilted socks. But the work warms you. By the time the heap is as high as your waist, you are steaming gently under your jacket, and you have almost forgotten the wind.

In the middle of the twentieth century, long after our night, the fuel under Korean floors changed. In the 1950s and after, millions of Korean homes began burning round coal briquettes, black cylinders pierced with holes from top to bottom, which could be stacked in the fire mouth and would smolder for hours. Today, most Korean floors are heated by gas boilers instead, and a whole winter's fuel arrives silently, through a pipe.

Historians do not agree on how much the warm floor itself was to blame for the bare hills. Some argue that as the heated floor spread from the poor to the rich and from the north to the south, especially through the cold seventeenth century, the demand for fuel grew so fast that no forest law could keep up with it. Others think the deeper cause lay in who controlled the forests. Powerful families and officials often held the best slopes privately, cut them as they pleased, and replanted little, while common villagers had no lasting reason to protect hills that were not really theirs. Most would say that both were true at once.

Dolsoe swings the needles off his jige into the fuel shed behind the kitchen, a low lean-to of posts and straw, and stacks the brushwood beside them in a neat wall. Then he takes one small bundle out of the rest and carries it down the kitchen steps.

"Mistress," he says. "Some pine root, with the sap in it. For the morning."

Lady Park takes it and turns it in her hands. The root is dark and heavy and smells strongly of turpentine. A few small pieces of it, at dawn, will light the morning fire in a moment, even if the ash has gone cold.

"Good," she says. "Put it by the hearth, where it will stay dry."

From the doorway, a man's voice says, "You were gone a long time, Dolsoe."

It is the master, Kim Myeong-hwan, standing at the top of the kitchen steps with his hands tucked inside the wide sleeves of his padded coat. He has a horsehair cap on his head, a small black cap of the kind scholars wore at home, and over his ears a pair of fur-lined cotton flaps, tied under the chin. He does not, as a rule, come into the kitchen. The kitchen belongs to the women. But he has come to the doorway, which is close enough.

"The near slopes are raked clean, master," says Dolsoe. "I went over the second ridge."

Kim Myeong-hwan nods. He knows. Every winter Dolsoe goes a little farther.

Then Dolsoe goes out to the gate quarters, a low wing by the front gate where he has his room and where the ox has its stall. The ox is a big brown animal, the Korean kind, with short horns and a long, patient face, and on its back, against the cold, it wears a thick blanket of woven straw, tied under its belly with rope. Beside the stall, in its own small clay hearth, sits another iron cauldron. Dolsoe lights a fire of straw under it, and fills it with chopped straw, bean husks and a little bran, and water, to make the ox's warm evening porridge.

And here is a small, clever thing. That cauldron's fire mouth faces the stall, but its smoke runs under the floor of Dolsoe's own little room next door. So every evening, when Dolsoe cooks for the ox, he is also warming his own bed. The ox eats warm, and the hired man sleeps warm, from one armful of straw.

He crouches by the little fire for a moment, holding out his hands, before he goes back to the yard.

Suni has heard the gate. She comes running across the yard from the inner house in her padded jacket, her braid flying, with her grandmother's voice following her, telling her to walk like a young lady and not like a calf. She slows down for exactly three steps and then runs again.

"Dolsoe, did you see the ice men?"

"I saw them," says Dolsoe. "Down below the West Ice House. A whole crowd of them, with saws and ropes. The ice is thick enough now."

Suni's eyes go round. She has wanted all winter to see the men cut ice from the river, and her mother has told her, every time, that the river is no place for a girl of nine in the twelfth month. Dolsoe knows this. He also knows that from the top of the back garden, by the kimchi jars, if you stand on the flat stone and stretch, you can just see a corner of the river through the bare trees.

"Tomorrow, if your mother says yes," he says, "I will lift you up on the stone, and you can look."

Suni is satisfied. She puts out one finger and touches the ox's wet black nose, and the ox blows a warm breath over her hand that smells of hay, and she laughs and runs back to the house.

Inside the inner room, Lady Yun has finished with the peppers. She sets the basket aside and lays her two hands flat on the floor in front of her knees, the way an old woman warms her hands at a stove. The floor near the kitchen wall is warmer now, distinctly warm, and the warmth is beginning to spread out across the room toward the far wall, slowly, the way a stain of water spreads across paper.

A heated floor of this kind was not like a stove that you could turn up and down. It took time to wake and time to cool. A family like the Kims would usually light the kitchen fire twice a day, once at dawn for the morning meal, and once in the late afternoon for the evening meal. Each fire burned hot for an hour or so, under the cooking pots, and then died down to embers. And the floor, which had drunk in all that heat, gave it back slowly, all through the following hours. A well-built floor lit in the late afternoon might still be warm, near the kitchen wall, at dawn the next day.

The fuel it took depended on the house, the room and the winter. But a family's winter fuel, gathered and bought, was one of the largest things it had to think about all year, along with rice and salt. In the autumn, the fuel shed was the measure of how well a family was prepared. A tall stack meant a warm winter. A thin one meant worry.

Lady Yun remembers thin winters. She remembers one, when she was a young wife in this house, when the family burned the dried stalks of everything that had grown in the garden, down to the last sunflower, and she and her mother-in-law slept in the inner room with the children, all together, to share one warm floor and save the fuel for another. But every autumn she walks out to the fuel shed, slowly, with her stick, and looks at the stack, and comes back without a word, and Lady Park knows from her face whether it is enough.

This autumn, she came back smiling.

In the kitchen, Lady Jeong watches her mother-in-law feed the fire. In her own mother's house, to the south, the kitchen fire was fed mostly with rice straw, because her family's village was all paddy fields and few trees, and her mother would twist each bundle of straw into a tight knot before pushing it in, so that it would burn longer. Here, on this hill, they burn needles and brush, and the fire is quicker and hotter and has to be watched more closely. She is learning to judge it. Twice already this winter she has let it burn too fast and too high, and the rice has caught on the bottom of the cauldron. Lady Park said nothing either time. She simply scraped the scorched rice out and set it aside, as if that had been the plan all along.

And in a way, as we will see, it was.

Across the house, in the kitchen, Lady Park lays a split stick of oak on the main fire, and it begins to hiss and spit as it catches, and the fire mouth glows deeper, from orange toward red.

## Part Three — Around Ten Past Five: Sunset over the Frozen River

At about nine minutes past five, the sun slips below the hills on the far side of the Han, and for a few minutes the whole sky over the western ridges turns the color of a ripe persimmon. In the yard, everyone who is not busy stops, for a moment, to look.

Why should a family that never eats ice care so much about how thick the river freezes?

The answer lies a couple of miles upriver, on the bank at the foot of a low hill. There, half sunk in the earth, stands one of the two great royal ice houses of the capital. The people call it Seobinggo, the West Ice House. Its twin, Dongbinggo, the East Ice House, stood farther upriver still. They were long, low buildings with thick walls and deep floors, roofed against the sun and lined with straw and reeds, and every winter they were filled to the rafters with blocks of ice cut from the frozen Han.

The ice from the East Ice House was kept mainly for the royal ancestral rites, the solemn offerings to the spirits of the kings, where food had to be kept fresh in the heat of summer. The ice from the West Ice House, the larger of the two, went to the palace kitchens, to the government offices, and to officials, who received a ration of it in the hot months as a mark of the king's favor. Ice, in a Korean August, was something close to a treasure.

So the freezing of the river was a matter of state. It was not cut at the first skin of frost. It had to reach a certain thickness, four chi in the old Korean measure, which is about five inches, before the cutting could begin. Then, in the twelfth month of the lunar year, the month we are in tonight, crowds of men from the riverside villages went out on the ice with saws, picks and ropes. They often worked in the coldest hours, in the dark before dawn, when the ice was hardest, cutting it into great blocks and dragging them on sledges up the bank and into the ice houses, where they were packed in straw and the doors were sealed until spring.

Here is a detail that brings it closer. When a winter was too warm, and the ice on the Han stayed thin, the court did something that might seem curious to us. It held a special rite, an offering to the spirit who governed the cold, praying for the river to freeze harder. The rite was called sahanje, the offering to the Lord of Cold. And even in ordinary winters, a version of it was held every year in the twelfth month at the ice houses, before the cutting began, and again in spring, when the doors were first opened.

The great chronicle of the dynasty, the Annals of the Joseon Dynasty, written up king by king, day by day, from the 1390s onward, records this rite again and again across the centuries. In its plain official language, it notes that an officer was sent each year in the last month of winter to make the offering to the Lord of Cold. Year after year, the same small entry. A kingdom asking politely, every winter, for enough cold.

So when Dolsoe tells Suni that the ice is thick enough now, he means more than it looks. He means the cutting has begun, and that men from villages like this one are earning a little winter money, hard money, out on the ice in the dark, so that a minister in Hanyang may have a cool drink in August.

In our own time, ice comes from a small tray in a freezer, or from a machine in a shop, at any season, for almost nothing. In 1790, a block of ice in summer was a gift from the king.

Some historians, looking at the records of these ice houses, see mainly the burden they placed on ordinary people. The riverside villagers were often required to do the cutting as a kind of labor duty, in the bitterest weeks of the year, and the work was dangerous. Others point out that, especially by the later Joseon period, much of this work was paid, and that private ice houses also grew up along the Han, run by merchants who sold ice to fishmongers and butchers. In that view, the ice cutting was not only a duty but also, for poor families in a hungry season, a welcome source of winter wages. It was probably both, in different decades and for different men.

You are standing at the edge of the Kims' back garden now, on the flat stone by the kimchi jars, where Dolsoe promised to lift Suni tomorrow. The sun has just gone. The sky is orange above the far hills, then pale green, then a deep, clear blue overhead. Just above the orange, low in the west, there is a young moon, only a few days old, a thin silver curve like a fingernail paring, lying almost on its back. Below you the river is a long gray band, darkening. Out on the ice, very small and far away, a few black figures are walking home toward the bank in a line, their tools on their shoulders. A dog barks somewhere in the village and is answered by another. The cold is deepening by the minute. You can feel it on your face, tightening the skin, and in the inside of your nose when you breathe. Behind you, from the chimneys of the village, smoke is drifting low along the ground, not rising. It flows down the slope like slow water, gray and fragrant, and hangs over the river in a soft blue haze. Every house on the hill is cooking supper now. Every house on the hill is lighting its floor.

Those chimneys are worth a closer look, and we will come back to them. Korean chimneys of this time were often surprisingly short. Many were no taller than a man, a little column of stones and clay, or a hollow log, set a few feet away from the back or the side of the house. They did not tower over the roof. And that, as we will see, was not carelessness.

In the kitchen, the rice is almost done. You can tell by the sound. The great cauldron, which was rumbling and bubbling a few minutes ago, has gone quiet, and from under its heavy wooden lid comes a soft hissing, and little beads of starchy water are weeping out around the rim and running down the iron and sizzling on the hot hearth. Lady Park listens with her head on one side, the way a musician listens to an instrument. Then she pulls most of the burning wood out of the fire mouth with a long iron poker, and pushes the embers back, and leaves the rice to steam gently in its own heat.

"Now we wait," she tells her daughter-in-law. "If you lift the lid now, it will be hard in the middle and soft at the top. Ten breaths more."

Lady Jeong counts the breaths silently. She has been told this before, in her own mother's kitchen. It is comforting to be told it again.

On a narrow shelf above the hearth, at the back, stands a small white porcelain bowl, full of clear water. It is not for drinking and it is not for cooking. It belongs to the spirit of the kitchen hearth, called Jowang, who in the old household beliefs of Korea watched over the fire and the family's food. Every morning before dawn, the mistress of the house would draw the first water from the well, the cleanest water of the day, and pour it fresh into that bowl, and perhaps bow once, and say a few quiet words for the health of the family. In this house, Lady Park does it. One day, Lady Jeong will.

Kim Myeong-hwan, the master, would not have taken much interest in the kitchen spirit. He is a scholar of the Confucian classics, and his reverence is for his ancestors, whose names are written on wooden tablets in a small shrine at the back of the house. But he would never have dreamed of telling his mother or his wife to stop pouring the water. The household ran on both kinds of care, his and theirs, the written and the unwritten, side by side.

Out in the yard, the master and his son are standing together at the top of the garden, watching the last of the light. Hyeong-ju is taller than his father now, which still surprises them both. He holds a little bundle of books wrapped in a blue cloth under one arm.

"The moon is very thin tonight," he says.

"It is the fourth day of the twelfth month," says his father. "When it is full, we will be halfway to the New Year."

They stand a little longer. Then the son says, carefully, as if he has been rehearsing it, "Father, I would like to sit up late with the Book of Documents tonight. If the lamp oil can be spared."

Kim Myeong-hwan looks at his son for a moment. He remembers asking his own father the very same thing, in the very same yard, nearly thirty years ago.

"It can be spared," he says. "But you will eat first."

While they talk, Dolsoe is crouching at the side of the men's quarters, under the eaves, in front of another small fire mouth. The men's quarters have no kitchen of their own. So their floor has its own little agungi, set into the outside wall of the house, facing the yard, with a low sloping roof of tiles over it to keep off the snow. Dolsoe feeds it with straw and brush, a smaller fire than the kitchen fire and a quicker one, and fans it with a flat straw fan until it roars softly. Inside, under the scholar's writing table, the floor of the men's room begins to wake.

A big house of this kind might have four or five separate fire mouths, one for each heated room or pair of rooms. Some opened into the kitchen. Some opened into the gate quarters, like Dolsoe's ox fire. And some, like this one, simply opened to the outside air, with a little shelter over them. Each one had to be fed, and each one, on a cold evening, had someone crouched in front of it.

Inside the inner room, the light is going. Lady Yun calls Suni to bring the lamp.

The lamp is a small, shallow dish of white porcelain, about as big as the palm of your hand, set on top of a tall, slender wooden stand. In the dish there is a little pool of oil, pressed from the seeds of a plant called perilla, a cousin of mint and basil, and lying in the oil, with one end hanging over the lip of the dish, is a twisted wick of cotton thread or paper. Suni carries a lighted spill of pine from the kitchen across the yard, cupping it in her hand against the wind, and touches it to the end of the wick, and a small yellow flame stands up, no bigger than a grain of rice at first, and then grows to the size of a fingertip.

It is not a bright light. A lamp like this gives about as much light as a single small candle, and it smokes a little, and it smells faintly of warm oil. But in a room of paper and wood, at the end of a winter day, it is enough to sew by if you sit close, and to see a face by, and to make the paper doors glow from outside like a lantern.

Oil was not cheap. Sesame oil and perilla oil were also used in cooking, and a careful household measured out its lamp oil the way it measured its rice. Poorer families burned fish oil, which smoked and smelled, or went to bed with the sun. That is why Hyeong-ju asked his father before sitting up late with his book. A scholar's lamp, burning far into the night, was a small expense that a family had to agree to.

Now, as the light outside fades, the doors of the house are drawn shut, one after another. The doors and windows of a house like this are frames of thin wooden lattice, a grid of slender strips in squares and diamonds, and over the lattice is pasted a sheet of tough white paper made from the inner bark of the mulberry tree. The paper is translucent. By day it lets in a soft, even light, like light through a cloud. By night it holds the lamplight in.

Around the edges of the doors, where wood meets wood, there are narrow strips of extra paper pasted along the frame, with one edge left free. They are called munpungji, door wind paper. When the door is closed, the free edge of the strip lies over the crack and seals it. When the wind blows outside, the little paper flaps tremble against the gap, and make a thin, high humming sound. On a windy night in a Korean house, that faint humming of the door papers was one of the sounds of winter, as familiar as the wind itself.

Lady Yun listens to it now, and says, as she says every winter, "The wind paper is singing. It will be cold tonight."

Suni slides the door shut behind her and comes to sit by her grandmother, close to the kitchen wall, where the floor is warmest. Lady Yun puts out an arm and draws her in under the edge of her quilted jacket, and they sit together in the small circle of lamplight, listening to the rice being served next door.

In the kitchen, Lady Park lifts the lid of the cauldron at last, and a great white cloud of steam rolls up into the cold air, smelling of new rice, and the fire mouth below glows dull red under its bed of embers.

## Part Four — Around Six in the Evening: Supper, and the Road Beneath the Floor

It is full dark now outside, and the young moon is a bright thin curve over the western hills. One by one, small wooden tables come out of the kitchen, carried in both hands at the height of the chest, and cross the cold yard to the lighted rooms.

Why does each person in this house eat from a separate little table, rather than all together from one?

In a household of this kind, in the Joseon period, meals were usually served on small, low, individual tables called soban. Each one was round or many-sided, about knee height when you sat on the floor, and just large enough for one person's bowls. Partly this was a matter of order. The eldest and the most honored were served first, and their tables were set with the most care, and a guest or a grandparent might be given a table of their own in their own room. Partly it was simply practical. In a house of separate wings, with the men in one building and the women in another, and the kitchen down a flight of steps, a small light table was the easiest way to carry a hot meal from the fire to the person who was going to eat it. You lifted the whole meal, bowls and spoons and all, and carried it like a tray.

Tonight, the first table goes to Lady Yun, in the inner room, carried by Lady Park herself. The second and third go across the yard to the men's quarters, to the master and his son, carried by Lady Jeong, one after the other. Dolsoe's food goes out to the gate quarters in a covered bowl. The women of the house, Lady Park and Lady Jeong and Suni, will eat a little later, in the inner room, near the grandmother, from tables of their own.

And while they eat, let us go under the floor.

So here, at last, is the answer to our first question. How can one fire, burning in a kitchen, warm the floor of the bedroom next door?

Begin at the fire mouth, the agungi, low in the kitchen wall. The fire burns there, under the rice cauldron. Behind the fire, at the back of the hearth, the clay narrows into a kind of throat, a short dark passage that drops a little and then climbs. When the fire is burning well, the heat and the hot smoke rush through that throat and out, not into the kitchen, but into the space under the floor of the inner room.

That space is not empty. It has been built, very carefully, into a set of long, low channels, running side by side from the kitchen wall toward the far side of the room. They are called gorae. Their walls are made of stones and clay, and they are perhaps a foot deep. Over the top of them, like a lid, lie big flat slabs of stone, each one a couple of inches thick, fitted edge to edge across the whole room. Those stones are called gudeuljang, the floor stones. And over the stones, a thick layer of clay mixed with chopped straw is spread and smoothed, thicker at the end nearest the fire and thinner at the far end, so that the heat comes through more evenly.

So the hot smoke from the kitchen fire flows along the channels, under the stones, from one side of the room to the other, giving up its heat to the stone above it as it goes. The stones grow warm, then hot. They pass their heat to the clay, and the clay passes it to the floor, and the floor passes it to whoever is sitting or lying on it.

At the far end of the room, just before the chimney, there is one more clever thing. The builders dug a trench there, deeper than the channels, running across the whole width of the room. It is called the gaejari. When the smoke reaches it, it slows and swirls, and drops its soot and ash, and gives up a little more of its heat. The trench also works the other way, as a kind of trap. When a cold wind blows down the chimney, or rain runs in, it falls into the deep trench instead of pushing back along the channels and blowing smoke into the kitchen.

And from the trench, the smoke goes out through a short passage to the chimney, standing outside the house. That is why Korean chimneys could be so short. A tall chimney draws hard. It would pull the heat through the channels too quickly, before the stones could drink it in. A short chimney draws gently, and lets the heat linger under the floor where it is wanted. Builders judged the height of a chimney the way a cook judges the heat under a pot.

So the fire cooks the rice and warms the room, both at once, with one armful of fuel. And when the fire dies down, the stones and clay go on giving out their stored heat for hours, long into the night, the way a hot stone from the hearth stays warm in your hand long after you have taken it from the fire.

That is the whole answer, and it is older than the kingdom. Fire low, floor high, a road of stone beneath, and a quiet chimney at the end.

There is one more layer, the one you actually touch. Over the smooth clay, the floor was papered. First came several layers of ordinary thick paper, pasted down one over another with a paste of rice or wheat flour, to make a smooth, even skin. Then, on top, came a special heavy paper called jangpanji, floor paper, made from mulberry bark and soaked with oil. Families oiled it with perilla oil, or polished it with a thin liquid made from ground soybeans, and rubbed it again and again until it shone. As it aged, the oil turned it a deep, glowing yellow, like the color of a well-loved wooden spoon. That is the floor Hamel saw, and called oiled paper. It was waterproof, it was easy to wipe, and it kept the fine dust of the clay below from rising into the room. When you lay your hand on it, it feels like smooth leather, warm from inside.

A floor like this needed care. Clay cracks as it dries and as it heats and cools, winter after winter, and a crack in the clay could let a thin breath of smoke into the room, which no family wanted. So every autumn, before the cold came, a careful household would look over its floors. Cracks were filled with fresh clay. Worn patches of floor paper were cut out and replaced. Every few years, the whole floor might be stripped and papered and oiled again from the beginning, and for a week the house would smell of new oil and drying paste. The chimneys were cleared. The deep trench at the far end, the gaejari, was dug out and its soot carried away, sometimes to the fields. And the fire mouths were patched with clay where the heat had cracked them.

The Kims did all of this in the ninth month, before the first frost, with Dolsoe up to his elbows in clay and soot and Lady Park inspecting every inch of the inner room on her knees. Lady Yun, who had overseen the same work every autumn for forty years, sat on the porch and offered advice. Most of it was good.

The end of the room nearest the kitchen, where the fire's heat arrives first and strongest, is called the araenmok, the lower part. The far end, near the chimney, where the smoke has already given up much of its heat, is called the witmok, the upper part. The difference between them, on a winter night, could be very large. We will come back to that, near the end of our night.

Here is a surprising problem with all this cleverness. The same fire that warmed the floor in January also warmed it in August. In the heat of a Korean summer, nobody wanted a hot floor under them at supper time. So many households also had a second, simpler cooking stove, a clay hearth in the yard or under an open shed, which they used in the hot months, so that the kitchen fire, and the floor, could rest. And in summer the family lived and slept more in the wooden-floored hall and on the cool raised porches, where no fire ran at all. A Korean house of this kind was really two houses in one, a warm one for winter and a cool one for summer, under the same roof.

Ten years before our night, in the summer of 1780, a Korean scholar named Pak Ji-won traveled with a royal embassy to China, all the way to the Qing emperor's summer retreat beyond the Great Wall. He kept a long, lively record of the journey, which became famous as the Jehol Diary. Pak was fascinated by practical things, by carts and kilns and bricks and roads, and on the way he stopped to study how the Chinese built their heated beds, the raised brick platforms called kang. And he compared them, not very kindly, with the heated floors he knew at home. As he described it, Korean floors were too often built carelessly, with uneven stones and rough clay that cracked, so that smoke leaked into the rooms and fuel was wasted. He thought his countrymen had much to learn from the neat brickwork of their neighbors.

You are sitting on that floor now, in the inner room, at Lady Yun's elbow, with a little table of your own in front of you. Your legs are folded under you on a thin cushion. The floor is the color of old honey and as smooth as a polished table, and it is warm, really warm, through the cushion and through your padded trousers. On your table there is a bowl of rice, white and steaming, with a few grains of millet in it like flecks of gold. There is a bowl of soup, made with fermented soybean paste and dried radish greens, dark and savory. There is a small dish of salted fish, grilled until the skin crackles. There is a dish of seasoned greens, and a bowl of kimchi, and a little bowl of cold radish water kimchi, with thin slices of radish floating in a clear, sharp broth, so cold that tiny needles of ice are still melting at its edges. You eat with a long-handled brass spoon and a pair of slender brass chopsticks. The lamp flickers. The paper door glows. Through the wall, you can hear the fire mouth settling, and Lady Park's voice saying something low to her daughter-in-law.

In our own time, most families eat at one table, on chairs, all together, from dishes placed in the middle. The little individual tables of Joseon Korea are now mostly found in museums, or in restaurants that serve the old court food.

Historians who read Pak Ji-won's criticism do not all accept it at face value. Some agree that he saw real faults, and note that many Korean floors of his day were indeed smoky, wasteful and hard to repair. Others point out that he was making a case, as the reformers of his circle often did, for learning from China, and that the Korean floor had real advantages he did not stress. It could burn grass, straw and pine needles rather than coal or large wood. And its heavy stones held their warmth through the night, long after a lighter heated bed had gone cold.

When Lady Yun has finished her rice, and her table has been taken away, Lady Park comes back from the kitchen carrying one more bowl. It is a plain brown bowl, and in it there is a pale, cloudy, gently steaming liquid. This is sungnyung. It is made from the scorched rice left stuck to the bottom of the cauldron after the meal has been served. Water is poured into the hot empty cauldron over the brown crust, and it is left to simmer by the dying fire, and the scorched rice softens and gives the water a mild, toasted, nutty flavor. In Korean homes it was drunk at the end of the meal, warm, in place of tea.

Lady Yun holds the bowl in both hands and breathes in the steam.

"This is good sungnyung," she says. "Who scorched the rice?"

Lady Jeong, kneeling by the door, turns pink to the tips of her ears.

"I did, Grandmother. I let the fire run too high."

Lady Yun sips, and nods, and sips again. "Then run it a little high tomorrow too," she says. "My teeth like it."

Suni laughs into her sleeve. Lady Park smiles and says nothing at all.

Out in the gate quarters, Dolsoe sits cross-legged on his own small warm floor, with his covered bowl on his knees. The ox fire has done its work. His room is no bigger than a large closet, with a single paper door and a straw mat rolled in the corner, but the floor is as warm as any in the house tonight, warmer perhaps, because the ox porridge took a long time to boil. He eats his rice and soup slowly, and drinks his own bowl of sungnyung, which Suni carried out to him with both hands, very carefully, spilling only a little. Through the wall he can hear the ox chewing, slow and steady, like a clock.

And down in the kitchen, under the empty cauldron, the fire mouth holds its bed of red embers, and the heat goes on flowing quietly under the floor, along its road of stone.

## Part Five — Around Seven O'Clock: Ink and Lamplight in the Men's Room

The supper tables have been carried back to the kitchen, and the yard is dark and empty, and the cold has settled over the hill like a lid. Across the yard, the paper door of the men's quarters glows a soft, steady yellow.

Why does a scholar, on a winter night, keep his inkstone on the floor?

The men's quarters of a house like this one were called the sarangchae, and the main room in them was the sarangbang, the master's room. It was his study, his office, his parlor and, often, his bedroom. Here he read and wrote, received his friends and visitors, taught his sons, and settled the business of the household with tenants and neighbors. It was usually close to the front gate, so that men who came to the house need not pass through the women's part of it. And it was furnished, in a scholarly family, with very few things, chosen with care.

There is a low writing table of dark wood, long and narrow, with its ends curled up like a scroll. There is a shelf of books, wrapped in cloth covers, lying flat rather than standing. There is a brush stand, a water dropper shaped like a peach, a box for paper, and, in the middle of the table, an inkstone, a flat, smooth slab of dark stone with a shallow well at one end. To write, you dripped a little water into the well, and rubbed a stick of hard black ink on the stone, round and round, until the water turned thick and dark.

And there is the problem. On a cold winter night, in a room whose air might be little warmer than outdoors near the walls, the water in the dropper could freeze, and the ink on the stone could thicken and turn to slush. So a Korean scholar in winter often did a simple thing. He set his inkstone and his water dropper on the warm floor, near the araenmok, the warm end of the room, for a while before he began to write, the way you might set a cold plate near a stove. A warm stone gave a smooth, flowing ink. A cold stone gave a stiff one.

Tonight, Kim Myeong-hwan has put his son's inkstone down on the floor by the kitchen side of the room, next to his own. Two smooth dark stones, side by side, warming in the lamplight.

The examinations that Hyeong-ju is studying for were the great road to rank and office in Joseon Korea. They were held in the capital and the provinces, in regular rounds every three years, and very often in special rounds too, called to celebrate a royal event or simply at the king's wish. The lower examinations tested a young man's knowledge of the Confucian classics, or his skill at composing poetry and prose in classical Chinese. Passing them gave him a title and a place in the world of scholars. The higher examination, much harder, could open the door to a post in government.

To prepare, a young man had to know the Four Books and the classics almost by heart, and be able to write on any passage from them, in elegant classical Chinese, in a long day, in a great crowded examination ground, with a brush. Hyeong-ju has been studying since he was six years old. He is nineteen. This is not unusual.

His father teaches the first steps of that same long road every winter morning, to the small boys of the village. The village school, the seodang, is a single room at the end of the lane, with its own warm floor and its own fire mouth, and in it, on cold mornings, a dozen boys of seven or eight or ten sit in rows on the floor with their books open in front of them, swaying and chanting together. The first book most of them learn is the Thousand Character Classic, a long poem made of one thousand different Chinese characters, none of them repeated, which begins with heaven and earth, and the dark and the yellow, and runs on through the stars and seasons and the virtues of a good life. A boy who has learned all one thousand characters, their sounds and their meanings and how to write them, has a key to every other book.

Winter was the great season for study in the countryside. In spring, summer and autumn, the boys of farming families were needed in the fields, and the schoolroom emptied. In winter the fields lay frozen, and there was nothing to do outdoors but gather fuel. So the long dark months were the months of chanting and brushwork, on a warm floor, while the snow lay outside.

You can imagine it, if you like. Twelve small boys in padded jackets, cheeks red from the cold walk, knees tucked under them, all reciting different lines at once, so that the little room hums like a hive. Their teacher sits at the warm end of the room with a thin switch of wood across his knees, which he almost never uses, and listens to them all at once, and somehow hears the one boy who has skipped a line. Kim Myeong-hwan is very good at that.

The families paid him as families paid such teachers, a little in coin, more often in rice or grain, and sometimes in kind, in a bundle of dried fish, or a jar of soy sauce, or a load of brushwood for the schoolroom fire. On festival days, the mothers sent rice cakes. It was not a rich living. It was an honored one.

Here is a story about a young scholar and a cold room that the scholars of Hanyang still told one another in 1790.

In the winter of 1760, thirty years before our night, a poor young man of about twenty named Yi Deok-mu was living in a small, unheated room in the capital. He was a gifted scholar, but he came from a branch of a family that could not hope for high office, and he had almost no money. That winter was very cold. As he later wrote, the cold grew so bitter one night that he could not sleep under his one thin quilt. So he got up and took down his copy of the Book of Han, a long history in many volumes, and laid the volumes in rows over his quilt to make a second layer. And when the wind still came through the cracks of the wall, he stood up a volume of the Analects of Confucius, the most honored of all the books he owned, as a little screen to keep the draft off his head.

A quilt of history. A screen of Confucius. He wrote about it with humor, as a man who had survived it, and the story was remembered.

And here is the part that makes it belong to our night. Yi Deok-mu did not stay poor and unknown. In 1779, King Jeongjo, who loved books and scholars, appointed him to the new royal library inside the palace, the Gyujanggak, as one of a small group of talented scholars of humble rank. So tonight, in January 1790, Yi Deok-mu is about fifty years old, and he is working for the king, among more books than he could ever have piled on a quilt. Among his many writings is a careful little book of good manners for scholars, women and children, written in the 1770s, called Sasojeol, Small Rules for Scholars. A copy of it might well sit on a shelf in a room like this one.

You are in the men's room now, sitting against the wall, close to the warm end of the floor. The room is small, perhaps ten feet square, with a low ceiling of dark beams and white plaster. The light comes from one lamp, standing at the master's elbow, its small flame quite still in the windless room. The walls are papered white, and the shadows of the two men move on them, large and soft. At your back, the floor is hot, hot enough that you shift your weight from time to time. But the air around your face is cool, and when you breathe out slowly, you can almost see your breath near the paper door. There is a faint smell of ink, which is made of pine soot and glue, and smells a little like smoke and a little like old wood. There is a smell of lamp oil, and of dry paper, and of the charcoal in a small brazier by the master's knee. Hyeong-ju is reading aloud, very softly, almost under his breath, swaying a little as he reads, the way students did, to fix the rhythm of the words in his memory. It sounds like a low, gentle chant, rising and falling. You do not understand the words. You do not need to.

That cool air at head level, over a hot floor, had a name of its own in Korean houses. It was called utpung, the upper draft. However warm the floor, the thin walls and paper doors let the cold in around the upper part of the room, and on a windy night you could feel it moving across your face and the top of your head. That is why a scholar's room, like this one, often had a folding screen standing near the sleeping place, to keep the upper draft off the head. Yi Deok-mu's little screen of the Analects was a poor man's version of the same idea.

The brazier by the master's knee is the other way to fight the upper draft. It is a small, heavy bowl of iron or pottery, standing on short legs, called a hwaro, a fire bowl. Before supper, Dolsoe carried it out to the kitchen, and Lady Park filled it with glowing embers from the fire mouth, and covered them with a thin layer of ash to keep them from burning too fast. Now it sits at the master's elbow, giving off a gentle, dry heat. It warms his hands when he holds them over it. It keeps a small kettle of water warm for his tea. And, best of all, if you bury a few chestnuts in its ash, in half an hour they will be roasted.

In our own time, a student preparing for a great examination might study in a brightly lit library, or a quiet reading room, with heating and electric light and a laptop. In 1790, he studied by the light of one small flame, on a warm floor, with his ink warming at his side.

Historians do not agree on how open the examinations really were. By law, the examinations were open to a wide range of free men, and not only to the sons of the old scholarly families, and some men of quite humble birth did pass and rise. In that view, the examinations were a real ladder, and one of the fairest of their time anywhere in the world. Other historians point out that in practice a young man needed years of leisure, a good teacher and a shelf of expensive books, and that the higher examinations and the best offices were overwhelmingly won by the sons of a small number of established families. Both views are drawn from the same records, read from different ends.

Kim Myeong-hwan has finished his own small task, a letter to a cousin about the boundary of a field, and he lays down his brush and listens to his son's soft chanting for a while.

When Hyeong-ju stops to rub more ink, his father says, "Your voice drops at the end of every line. The examiners will not hear you, but you will hear yourself. Keep it level."

"Yes, Father."

"And sit up. You are leaning toward the warm floor like a cat."

Hyeong-ju sits up, a little guiltily. His father smiles, which he does not often do in front of his son, and reaches into the brazier with a pair of iron chopsticks and pulls out two chestnuts, black and split, and sets them on a scrap of paper on the table, one for each of them.

They peel them in silence. The shells crack, and the soft yellow flesh inside is hot and sweet and floury, and steams in the cool air.

"When I sat my examination," Kim Myeong-hwan says, "it was in the spring, and it rained. All day. We sat on the ground under oiled paper umbrellas and wrote with the rain dripping off the edges. A man next to me had his paper soaked through, and he wept." He shakes his head. "I passed. He did not. He was the better scholar."

Hyeong-ju does not know what to say to that, so he says nothing, and eats his chestnut.

"Read me the passage again," says his father at last. "From the beginning. Level, this time."

So Hyeong-ju reads it again, level, and his father closes his eyes and listens, nodding very slightly at the end of each line, as his own father once did in this same room, on this same warm floor. Then he takes from a peg on the wall a sleeveless vest of padded silk, very old and very soft, lined with a little rabbit fur, and drops it over his son's shoulders without a word.

Hyeong-ju knows that vest. It was his grandfather's.

Outside, Dolsoe crouches once more at the little fire mouth under the eaves of the men's quarters, and pushes the last of the embers to the back with a stick, and closes the mouth with a flat stone, leaving only a small gap, so that the fire will smolder slowly instead of roaring. And across the yard, in the kitchen, the main fire mouth glows quietly in the dark, its embers banked and red, keeping the inner room warm for the women.

## Part Six — Around Eight O'Clock: The Sound of Clubs on Cloth

Low over the western hills, the thin young moon is sinking, and at about ten minutes past eight it slips behind the ridge and is gone. The village is very dark now. And from somewhere down the lane, and then from another house, and then from the inner room of the Kims' own house, comes a sound like soft wooden drums.

Why does a Korean village, on a winter evening, sound as if it were full of drummers?

The sound is called dadeumi, and it is the sound of cloth being smoothed. In a house like this, everyday clothes were made mostly of cotton, and summer clothes of hemp and ramie, and the best clothes of silk. When cotton or ramie cloth had been washed and lightly starched with a thin paste of rice, it dried stiff and wrinkled. To make it smooth and soft, and to give it a gentle sheen, it was folded carefully while still a little damp, laid on a long, smooth stone block, and beaten, again and again, with a pair of wooden clubs.

The stone was called a dadeumitdol, and it was usually a slab of granite, perhaps two feet long, polished smooth on top by years of use. The clubs were called bangmangi. They were made of hard wood, about as long as your forearm, rounded and smooth, and heavier at the striking end. A woman held one in each hand, and beat the folded cloth in a quick, steady rhythm, left, right, left, right, turning and refolding it from time to time. Often two women knelt facing each other across the same stone, each with her pair of clubs, and beat together, four clubs falling in turn, so that the rhythm doubled and became a kind of music.

And because the work was done in the evening, after the meal, when the day's other tasks were finished, the sound rose from house after house at the same hour, all over the village. On a still winter night, you could hear it from one end of a valley to the other. For people who grew up in Korean villages, even far into the twentieth century, the sound of dadeumi in the dark was one of the oldest sounds of home.

Here is something that surprises most people who hear it for the first time. In Joseon Korea, many garments were not washed whole. They were taken apart. A woman would unpick the seams of a padded jacket or a long coat, take out the cotton wadding, and wash the separate panels of cloth flat, like sheets. Then the panels were starched, dried, beaten smooth on the stone, pressed, and sewn back together again, with the wadding laid back in between. A single good winter coat might be taken apart and remade like this several times in its life.

It sounds like an enormous amount of work, and it was. But it also meant that clothes could be kept clean and soft for years. Worn panels could be replaced. A coat could be let out, or turned, or made over for a younger child. In a household like the Kims', much of the women's evening work in winter was exactly this, the long, patient round of washing, starching, beating, pressing and sewing that kept seven people clothed.

The warmth of all those clothes came, in the end, from a single plant. Cotton had not always grown in Korea. The story every Korean child learned was that in the 1360s, a scholar and envoy named Mun Ik-jeom, returning from a mission to the Mongol court in China, brought home a few cotton seeds, hidden, it was said, in the hollow of his writing brush. His father-in-law planted them, and only one of them sprouted. From that one plant, so the story goes, came all the cotton fields of Korea. Whether or not the brush is true, cotton did spread across the southern provinces in the following century, and it changed winter for everyone. Before it, ordinary people had worn hemp, which is strong and cool but thin. With cotton, a family could make cloth that was soft and warm, and wadding to pad their jackets and quilts.

Cotton became so important that, for a long time in the Joseon period, bolts of plain cotton cloth were used as a kind of money. Taxes could be paid in cloth. Soldiers' service could be paid in cloth. Wages and prices were often counted in lengths of it. A woman weaving cotton on her loom in the evening was, in a quite real sense, making the family's savings with her hands.

Most of the warmth in this room tonight is cotton. Lady Park's padded jacket is cotton, quilted in long, close, parallel lines of stitching, so that the wadding inside cannot slip or bunch. That kind of quilting, called nubi, was slow, careful work, sometimes with the lines of stitches less than half an inch apart over a whole garment, and a fine nubi jacket was a thing to be proud of. Under her skirt she wears two or three layers of cotton trousers and petticoats, the inner ones padded. Lady Yun wears a sleeveless vest of padded cotton over her jacket, and a soft winter cap of black silk, open at the top, with flaps that come down over her ears and the back of her neck, edged with a band of fur. In the old women's quarters of Joseon houses, a cap like that, in winter, was almost never taken off before bed.

Nineteen years after our night, in 1809, a woman of a scholarly family who lived not far from Hanyang finished writing a book about all of this. Her name, as she is remembered, was Lady Yi of Bingheogak. She had married at fifteen, in 1773, and spent her life running a household, and in her fifties she gathered what she knew into a kind of encyclopedia for women. She called it Gyuhap Chongseo, which means something like the collected writings of the women's quarters. It covered the making of wines and sauces and dishes of every kind, sewing and weaving and dyeing, the care of the household and its stores, and a great deal more, set down with the plain confidence of someone who had done it all herself. It was copied by hand, again and again, and passed from mother to daughter for generations, and it is one of the treasures of Korean writing about daily life.

Tonight, in 1790, Lady Yi of Bingheogak is a woman of about thirty, living her own winter evenings in a house much like this one. Her book is still far in the future. But the knowledge in it is all around us now, in this room.

You are kneeling in the inner room now, a little to one side of the stone. The dadeumi stone stands on the warm floor in the middle of the room, in the lamplight. On one side of it kneels Lady Park, and on the other side kneels Lady Jeong, each with a club in each hand. Between them, on the stone, lies a long folded band of white cotton, still faintly damp. Lady Park begins. Tak, tak, tak, tak. Lady Jeong comes in, half a beat behind, filling the spaces. Tak-a-tak, tak-a-tak. The sound is not loud. It is crisp and wooden and very clear, and you can feel it, a little, through the floor under your knees. The two women's sleeves are tied back. Their faces are calm and intent. The lamp flame trembles slightly with each stroke. And from outside, through the paper doors, you can hear the same sound coming from the house down the lane, and from another farther off, and another, a whole village of clubs, beating in the dark, all at different speeds, like rain beginning on many different roofs.

In our own time, most clothes go into a washing machine whole and come out ready to wear, and an electric iron or a steamer does in a few minutes what took a Joseon household half an evening. Very few people today have ever heard dadeumi played in earnest, except at a folk festival or in a museum.

Historians and writers have looked at this work in two quite different ways. Some see in it mainly the hidden burden of women's labor in Joseon Korea, the countless unpaid hours of washing and beating and sewing, late into the night, that kept a household running and went almost entirely unrecorded. Others stress what the women themselves often remembered, that the evening hours of shared work were also hours of company and conversation, of songs and stories and private jokes, in a room where no man came. Neither picture is false. The work was hard. The room was also warm, and full of voices.

When the cloth is smooth, Lady Park lifts it from the stone and shakes it out, and it hangs from her hands, soft and white, with a faint silky shine. Next it must be pressed.

For that there are two irons. The first is called an indu. It is a small iron with a flat, pointed head shaped like a leaf, on a long handle, and it is heated by pushing its head into the embers of the fire bowl, the hwaro, that Lady Park has filled from the kitchen fire mouth. When it is hot, she uses it to press the seams and the narrow edges of collars and sleeves, quick and precise, like drawing with heat. The second is called a darimi, and it is a small, heavy iron pan with a long handle. Glowing embers are put inside the pan itself, and its flat bottom is pressed slowly over the cloth. For that, the cloth must be held taut, so Lady Jeong takes one end and Lady Park the other, and they draw it tight between them, while Lady Yun, who cannot kneel so long anymore, moves the darimi over it in long, slow strokes. A faint warm smell of clean cotton rises.

"Pull," says Lady Yun. "Pull harder, child. You are not holding a baby."

Lady Jeong pulls harder, and the cloth snaps tight. Lady Yun nods.

At Lady Park's waist, hanging from the ties of her skirt, there is a heavy ring of iron keys. They clink softly as she moves. They are the keys to the storerooms of the house, to the rice store and the jar terrace and the chests of cloth, and in a household of this kind, whoever held those keys was the true mistress of the house. They were not given lightly. For many years they hung from Lady Yun's own skirt. Then, when Lady Park had been a wife in this house long enough, and had borne a son, and had proved herself in the kitchen through many winters, Lady Yun one day simply handed them to her, and with them the running of the household, and stepped back from the work of it, though never from her place of honor.

That handing over was a quiet thing. There was no ceremony. But everyone in the house knew what it meant. Lady Park has held the keys now for nearly twenty years. One day, many winters from now, she will hand them to Lady Jeong. Lady Jeong knows this, and it is one reason she watches her mother-in-law's hands so closely, at the fire and at the stone.

Suni sits by the kitchen wall with her own small piece of sewing, a padded sock that she is supposed to be finishing for her brother. Korean socks of this time, called beoseon, were made of cotton cloth, cut and sewn to fit the foot closely, with a little upturned point at the toe, and in winter they were padded with a layer of cotton wadding. Suni's stitches wander. She knows it. She keeps glancing at her sister-in-law, who has the steadiest hands she has ever seen.

"Sister," she says at last, in a small voice, "will you show me again how to turn the toe?"

Lady Jeong looks up, surprised, and then smiles, the first real smile Suni has seen on her face this winter. She sets down the end of the cloth and moves over beside Suni and takes the sock in her hands.

"Like this," she says. "You fold it here, and you sew with small stitches, very small, smaller than you think, and you pull gently, so it does not pucker. Like that."

Suni sees. Lady Yun and Lady Park exchange a look over the girls' heads, and say nothing.

For a while there is no sound in the room but the small pull of thread through cotton, and the lamp flame ticking faintly in its dish, and the distant drumming of the clubs in the house down the lane. Then that stops too, and the house down the lane is quiet, and you can hear the wind paper on the door, humming its one thin note. Lady Yun leans back against the wall, with her hands folded in her lap, and closes her eyes, and listens to the two girls whispering over the sock, one voice quick and high and the other low and patient, and her face, in the lamplight, is very peaceful.

Later, when the irons are cooling and the sewing is put away, Lady Park carries the hwaro back down the kitchen steps, and tips its last embers into the fire mouth, where they glow among the others, and the fire under the inner room takes them in, and goes on breathing.

## Part Seven — Around Nine O'Clock: The Jars Under the Straw

The sewing is folded away, and Lady Park has taken a small lantern from its hook by the kitchen door. Tomorrow's kimchi must be brought in tonight, before the cold grows any deeper, and she calls her daughter-in-law to come with her into the dark of the back garden.

Why does a family keep its winter food buried in the ground?

Behind the inner house, on a raised terrace of earth and stones where the sun falls longest in the day, stand the family's great pottery jars. There are dozens of them, of every size, from little ones no bigger than a melon to great round-bellied jars that a grown woman could almost climb inside. They are glazed a deep, glossy brown, and they hold the most important things the household owns after rice. Soy sauce. Soybean paste. Red pepper paste. Salted fish. Vinegar. This is the jar terrace, the jangdokdae, and in many ways it is the true larder of a Korean house.

But the winter kimchi is not on the terrace. It is under the ground.

In the late autumn, when the kimchi for the winter was made, the big jars that would hold it were buried in pits dug in the garden, up to their shoulders or their necks, so that only the lids showed above the earth. Over them, a little hut of straw was built, a low peaked roof of thatch on a frame of poles, to keep off snow and rain and the worst of the frost. Inside the hut, in the dark, under the straw, the earth held the jars at a steady, cool temperature, just above freezing, all through the winter. Not so warm that the kimchi went sour too fast. Not so cold that it froze solid. Just cold enough for it to ferment slowly, week by week, so that it would last until spring.

The jars themselves helped. Korean storage jars of this kind, called onggi, were made of a coarse local clay and fired in long kilns built up the slope of a hill. Potters and cooks have long said that the walls of a good onggi jar breathe. The clay is full of tiny pores, too small to let the brine leak out, but enough, it is said, to let a little air pass slowly through the walls, so that the food inside ferments gently and evenly and does not spoil. Whether or not the jars truly breathe, families trusted them for everything that had to keep, and a cracked jar was mended with wire and clay rather than thrown away.

So the ground itself was the family's refrigerator. And the little straw hut over the jars was one of the most familiar shapes in a Korean winter garden.

Here is a detail that surprises nearly everyone. The kimchi in those jars tonight is not quite the kimchi you may know. The fiery red kimchi of modern Korea, made with napa cabbage and plenty of red pepper, is in many ways younger than our night.

The red chili pepper came to Korea from the Americas, by way of other lands, only about two centuries before our evening. A Korean scholar named Yi Su-gwang, in an encyclopedia he finished in 1614, wrote one of the earliest Korean descriptions of it. As later writers summarize it, he noted that this pepper had a strong poison in it, that it had come from Japan, and that people called it the Japanese mustard. For a long time after that, chili was a curiosity and a medicine more than a seasoning. It was only in the 1700s that Korean cookbooks began to describe putting red pepper into kimchi. A farming and household manual expanded and printed in 1766, called Jeungbo Sallim Gyeongje, is often cited as one of the first to do so. That is just twenty-four years before our night.

So in 1790, red pepper in kimchi was a newer custom, spreading but not yet everywhere. Many families still made much of their kimchi pale, seasoned with salt, garlic, ginger, salted fish and other things, and some made it both ways. And the cabbage itself was different. The cabbage grown in Korea in 1790 was a looser, leafier plant than the tight, heavy heads of napa cabbage that fill Korean markets today. Much winter kimchi was made from radish, sliced or whole, with its leaves.

A book of the yearly customs of Korea, finished in 1849 by a scholar named Hong Seok-mo and called Dongguk Sesigi, the Record of the Seasonal Customs of the Eastern Kingdom, describes how, in the tenth month, the people of the capital made their winter kimchi in jars, from radishes, cabbages, garlic, red pepper and salt. And it adds, in words that are often quoted, that the making of sauces earlier in the year and the making of kimchi in winter were the two great tasks of every household's year. Half a century after our night, the custom was the same, and the red pepper had become part of it.

You are in the straw hut with Lady Park and Lady Jeong now. You have to stoop to get in. The doorway is low and hung with a curtain of straw matting, and inside it is very dark, and very cold, and very still, and it smells strongly and wonderfully of fermenting radish, garlic and salt. Lady Park sets the lantern down on a flat stone, and its small light shows the round lids of the buried jars, five of them in a row, like the tops of great brown eggs set in the earth. She lifts one lid. A breath of cold, sour, salty air rises from the jar. Inside, packed close, are whole small radishes and leafy greens, glistening in a dark, cloudy brine, with a skin of thin ice on the surface that cracks when she presses it with the back of a ladle. She fills a bowl. Lady Jeong lifts a second lid, on the next jar, and that one holds the cold radish water kimchi, dongchimi, clear and pale, with slices of pear and threads of ginger floating in it, and a crust of ice around the rim. Your fingers ache with the cold just from watching them. Your breath hangs in the lantern light. The straw roof above you creaks softly in the wind, and somewhere a dog barks once and is quiet.

Now, as they carry the bowls back toward the kitchen in the lantern light, let us go back seven weeks in time, to the days when these jars were filled.

It was the tenth month of the lunar year, about the second half of November. The radishes and cabbages had been pulled from the fields after the first frosts, and lay in great heaps in the yards. In the Kims' house, and in every house along the lane, the women were preparing. First the cabbages and radishes were trimmed and cleaned and split, and soaked overnight in big tubs of salt water, so that they softened and the salt went into them. The next day they were rinsed, again and again, in cold water carried from the well, or at the edge of the stream, until the women's hands were red and numb. Then came the seasoning, mountains of it, garlic pounded in mortars, ginger shredded, green onions and mustard greens chopped, salted shrimp brought up from the boats at Mapo on the river below, and, in this house, a large basket of Lady Yun's dried red peppers, ground to a coarse red powder. All of it was mixed into a thick paste and rubbed and stuffed, leaf by leaf, into the softened cabbages, and packed with the radishes into the jars.

It took two days, sometimes three, and it took many hands.

If you had walked down the lane on one of those days, you would have known what was happening long before you reached the first gate. You would have smelled it first, garlic and ginger and brine and the sharp, warm smell of ground pepper, drifting over every wall. Then you would have heard it, the chatter and laughter of a dozen women in one yard, the knock of pestles in mortars, the splash of water in tubs, a baby crying somewhere and being hushed. And when you looked over the wall, you would have seen the yard white with cabbages and radishes, piled like snowdrifts, and the women squatting around big shallow tubs with their sleeves tied up past the elbow and their arms red to the wrist, and the children running between them, stealing the sweet yellow hearts of the cabbages to eat raw, and being scolded, and stealing more.

And that is the heart of our second question.

So here is the answer. Why does the whole village make its winter kimchi on the same few days?

First, because the weather chooses the days. Kimchi for the winter had to be made after the vegetables had been harvested and touched by the first frosts, which sweetened them, but before the ground froze hard, because the jars had to be buried while the earth could still be dug. And the air had to be cold enough that the new kimchi would not sour too quickly. That left a window of only a few weeks in late autumn, and every family in the village was watching the same sky.

Second, because no household could do it alone. A family of seven needed hundreds of radishes and cabbages to see it through to spring. So the women of the lane worked in turns, all together, house by house. On the first day they all came to one house, and the next day to another, and so on down the lane, each family giving its labor and receiving it back. Koreans called this kind of exchange of work pumasi, and it ran through village life, in the rice fields as much as in the kitchens. So the kimchi days of the different houses were not really separate. They were one long festival of work, moving from yard to yard.

And third, because the kimchi was shared. A family that made more than it needed sent bowls to the neighbors, to the old, to those who had less. A new bride's kimchi was tasted and judged by every woman on the lane. The salted shrimp came up from the river boats in the same weeks, to the same market, and everyone bought at once.

In 2013, the United Nations cultural organization, UNESCO, added this custom, in its present-day form, to its list of the intangible cultural heritage of humanity, under the name Kimjang, the making and sharing of kimchi. What it chose to honor was not so much a recipe as exactly this: the neighbors, the sharing and the shared days.

Historians of food do not all agree on how quickly red pepper took over the kimchi jar. Some argue that once it arrived in the 1700s, it spread fast, because it helped kimchi keep and made a little salt go further, at a time when salt was dear. Others think the change was slow and uneven, region by region and class by class, and that the deep red kimchi most people now imagine became typical only in the 1800s and after, along with the new kinds of cabbage.

The kimchi days of this winter were Lady Jeong's first in her husband's house. She was frightened of them for weeks beforehand. Every woman on the lane would be there, and every woman would taste.

She remembers now, walking back with the bowl of dongchimi held carefully against her chest, how on the second day old Grandmother Han from three doors down, a woman with a voice like a crow and the sharpest tongue in the village, had picked up a leaf that Lady Jeong had stuffed, and tasted it, and chewed, and said nothing at all for a long time. And then had said, to Lady Park, not to her, "Your daughter-in-law has a light hand. Good." And had gone on to the next tub.

Lady Jeong had nearly cried with relief. Lady Park had simply said, "Yes," and handed her another cabbage.

That night, after the last jar was buried, Lady Park had sent a bowl of the new kimchi down the lane to Grandmother Han's house, and another to the widow at the bottom of the hill who had no daughters to help her, and a third, carried by Suni with great ceremony, to the schoolroom for her father's small pupils. And bowls had come back up the lane to the Kims, from three other houses, so that for a week the family ate everyone's kimchi but its own, and Lady Yun tasted each one and gave her verdict, kindly, on all of them.

In the kitchen, Lady Park sets the bowls of kimchi on the cold end of the hearth, away from the fire, and covers them with a cloth for the morning. Then she kneels, for the last time this evening, in front of the fire mouth. The embers are low and red and shimmering. She lays on a few handfuls of dry pine needles, and they catch with a soft rush of flame, and she watches until the flame has settled, then sets a flat stone against the mouth, leaving a gap the width of two fingers, so that the fire will burn slowly through the night.

## Part Eight — Around Ten O'Clock: The Great Bell Closes the City

The lamps in the village are going out, one by one, and the dark on the hill is very deep. Out by the gate, Dolsoe stands still with a wooden bar in his hands, listening. Far away across the frozen fields, from the heart of the city, there is a sound so faint you could almost believe you had imagined it. A bell.

How did anyone in Hanyang know what time it was, in the middle of a winter night, with no clocks on the walls?

The answer was that the city told them. In Joseon Korea, the night was not divided into hours the way ours is. It was divided into five watches, called gyeong, running from dusk to dawn, and each watch was divided again into five smaller parts, called jeom. The length of the watches changed with the seasons, longer in winter and shorter in summer, because the night itself was longer or shorter. And the passing of the watches was measured by the king's water clocks, and announced to the city by drums and gongs and bells.

Twice in every night, the great bell of the capital spoke for everyone. It hung in a pavilion on the main street of the city, the street that ran east to west through its middle, the one still called Jongno, the Bell Street. Toward ten o'clock in the evening, as we would count it, the bell was struck twenty-eight times. That ringing was called injeong. When it sounded, the great gates of the city wall swung shut, and a curfew fell over the streets. And toward four o'clock in the morning, the bell was struck thirty-three times, and that ringing was called paru. Then the gates were opened again, and the city was allowed to wake.

The wall those gates belonged to was one of the great works of the dynasty. It was first raised in 1396, only a few years after the kingdom was founded, by tens of thousands of laborers called up from the provinces, and it was rebuilt and strengthened in stone in later reigns. It ran for about eleven and a half miles, up and over the four mountains that ring the old city, along their ridges and down through the valleys between them, and it was pierced by eight gates, four great ones and four small. The nearest of the great gates to the Kims' hill is the South Gate, Sungnyemun, with its double roof of tiles, where the road from the river ports of Mapo and Yongsan came up into the city, and where the carts of salt and fish and firewood queued every morning to go in.

A village like the Kims', just outside the wall, lived in two worlds. It was close enough to the capital to sell to its markets and hear its news, and in this period it even fell under the authority of the city's own government, which reached out about ten li, roughly three miles, beyond the wall. But when the bell rang and the gates closed, the villagers on the outside were simply shut out, with the river and the hills and the dark. For them the curfew was a bell heard from far off, and a reason to go to bed.

Between the two bells, the streets of Hanyang belonged to the night patrols. Men found walking abroad without good reason could be stopped, questioned and held until morning. There were exceptions for urgent errands, such as fetching a doctor or a midwife, or carrying news of a death. But for most people, the evening bell meant simply this. Go home. Bar the gate. The day is over.

Here is a detail that connects the bell to a marvel. The timing of these bells went back, in the end, to an extraordinary machine. In 1434, in the reign of King Sejong, the court engineer Jang Yeong-sil completed a great self-striking water clock for the palace, called the Jagyeongnu. Water flowed at a steady rate from one vessel into another, and as it rose, it set off a chain of metal balls rolling down channels inside a wooden tower, which in turn made little wooden figures strike a bell for the hours, a drum for the watches and a gong for the smaller divisions of the night. It told the time, aloud, all night long, with no one touching it. Its signals were passed on to the watchmen, and from them, in the end, to the great bell in the street.

The great bell of the city itself had a long life too. An old bronze bell, cast in 1468, hung in that pavilion and rang the city's gates open and closed for centuries. It was cracked in the end and retired, and it is kept today in the National Museum of Korea, in Seoul, where you can stand beside it, a great green-gray dome of bronze taller than a man, and imagine its voice rolling across the rooftops in the dark.

Historians are not entirely sure where the numbers twenty-eight and thirty-three came from, though there are two well-known explanations, and they may both be partly right. One says that the twenty-eight strokes of evening stood for the twenty-eight lunar mansions, the groups of stars by which East Asian astronomers divided the night sky, so that the bell told the heavens that the night had begun. The thirty-three strokes of morning stood, in this view, for the thirty-three heavens of Buddhist teaching, so that the bell asked for peace for the kingdom and its people for the coming day. Others are more cautious, and note that the exact hours and even the customs of ringing changed over the long centuries of the dynasty, so that what was true in one reign may not have been true in another.

You are standing by the gate with Dolsoe now. It is very cold, the coldest it has been all night, and very still. The stars are out, thick and sharp, in a sky so clear and black that they seem close enough to touch. The cold stings your ears and the tip of your nose. Beyond the wall of the yard, the hill drops away into darkness, and far below and to the east, beyond the frozen river and the black fields, you can see a faint, low glow on the horizon where the city lies, and a few pricks of light. And then, carried on the still air, so faint that you hold your breath to hear it, comes the bell. One deep, soft stroke. A long pause, while the sound dies away. Then another. You do not try to count them. You simply stand and let them come, slow and far and calm, like stones dropped one by one into a very deep well.

"They are closing the gates," says Dolsoe. He says it every night, at about this time, whether he hears the bell or not.

Then he lifts the heavy wooden bar and drops it into the iron brackets across the double gate of the yard, and checks it with his hand, and the gate is shut for the night.

His last round begins. He goes first to the ox, who is lying down now in its stall, a great warm brown shape in the dark, its breath steaming, its straw blanket rising and falling. He touches its neck, and it does not stir. He looks at the little fire mouth by the stall and sees that the embers under the fodder cauldron are low and safe. Then he crosses the yard to the men's quarters, and crouches by the fire mouth under the eaves, and looks in at the gap beside the stone. A dull red glow. Good.

He goes around the back of the inner house and looks at the short chimney, where a thin, almost invisible thread of warm smoke is still rising and drifting away down the hill. He lays his hand on the chimney's side. It is warm. He goes to the well and checks that the bucket is drawn up and turned over, so it will not freeze to the stones. He looks at the straw hut over the kimchi jars, and straightens a corner of its matting that the wind has lifted. And last of all, he stops at the top of the kitchen steps and looks down into the dark kitchen, at the faint red line of light around the stone at the fire mouth.

Dolsoe has been doing this every winter night for eleven years. He came to the Kims as a young man, after a bad harvest in his own village to the north, with nothing but the clothes he wore and a straw rain cape. He did not expect to stay. But the master was fair, and the mistress fed him well, and the old lady, Lady Yun, once sat up with him for two nights when he had a fever, giving him sips of barley tea and scolding him whenever he tried to get up. That was nine years ago. He has never forgotten it.

In the men's quarters, Kim Myeong-hwan is putting away his brushes. His son has gone on reading, a little longer, by the light of the lamp, the old fur vest around his shoulders. But before he goes to his own room tonight, Hyeong-ju will do one more thing.

He closes his book, and stands, and slides open the paper door, and crosses the dark, frozen yard to the inner house. He climbs the step to the wooden hall. He kneels outside the door of the inner room, and says, quietly, "Grandmother. Mother. I am going to rest now. Is the floor warm enough for you tonight?"

"It is warm," says Lady Yun's voice through the paper. "Go to bed, child. You will ruin your eyes."

"Yes, Grandmother."

He bows, there on the cold floor of the hall, to the closed door, though no one can see it. Then he goes across the hall to the small room on the other side, the room he shares with his wife, where she has already unrolled the quilts and is waiting, sitting on the warm floor, with her hands in her lap.

This small evening visit, a young person going to an elder at the end of the day to ask whether they are comfortable, was something every well-brought-up son and daughter in a family like the Kims' was taught to do. It had an old name, and an old book behind it. We will come to both, a little later in our night.

The little room across the hall is the smallest heated room in the house. It is called the geonneonbang, the room across the way, and its floor has its own small fire mouth, outside, at the back of the house, which Dolsoe lit before supper. The room has one paper door, a low chest for clothes, and a folding screen painted with plum blossoms that Lady Jeong brought with her from her mother's house, as part of the things a bride brought to her new home. She has set it up at the head of the quilts, against the upper draft.

Hyeong-ju sits down on the warm floor, and lets out a long breath, and for a while neither of them says anything. They were married only last spring. They had met for the first time on their wedding day, as was usual for couples of their families, and they are still, in many ways, learning each other, the way you learn a new house, room by room.

"Did you finish the passage?" she asks at last.

"Twice," he says. "Father says my voice drops at the end of every line."

"It does," she says. "I can hear it from the kitchen."

He looks at her, startled. She is trying very hard not to smile, and failing. And then he laughs, quietly, so as not to wake the house, and she laughs too, behind her hand, and something in the small room, which has been a little stiff and shy all winter, softens like the warm floor.

"Grandmother liked the sungnyung," she tells him.

"I heard," he says. "Suni told me. She told everyone."

In the inner room, Lady Park is making her own last round, by lamplight. She looks at the oil in the lamp dish and judges it. She takes the ring of keys from her waist and sets it in its place in the chest by the wall, where her hand can find it in the dark. She lays her hand flat on the floor near the kitchen wall, and then near the far wall, and then near her mother-in-law's place, and is satisfied. She goes out once more onto the wooden hall, into the cold, and looks up at the stars over the courtyard, and listens to the house. The ox is quiet. The gate is barred. The men's lamp is still burning across the yard, but it will not burn much longer. Somewhere down the hill, a dog barks twice and gives up. Then she comes back inside, slides the door shut behind her, and turns the lamp wick down low, so that the flame is no bigger than a seed, and the room is full of soft brown shadows.

Down in the kitchen, the fire mouth breathes softly behind its stone, a thin red line of light in the darkness, and the warmth goes on moving quietly under the floor.

## Part Nine — Around Eleven O'Clock: Quilts on the Warm Floor

The lamp across the yard has gone out at last. In the inner room, by the small seed of flame that Lady Park has left burning, the women of the house are making their beds.

Where does a bed go, in a house with no beds?

In a Korean house of this time, there was no piece of furniture for sleeping. The warm floor was the bed. Every evening, the bedding was taken out and laid on it, and every morning it was folded up and put away again, so that the same room could be a sitting room by day, a dining room at supper and a bedroom at night. The bedding lived in a tall wooden cabinet against the wall, or folded and stacked on top of a low chest, neatly, with the quilts on top and their bright edges showing, so that even when it was put away it made the room look comfortable.

There were three parts to a Korean bed. First, on the floor, came the yo, a long, flat mattress of cotton cloth padded with cotton wadding, perhaps an inch or two thick, just long enough and wide enough for one person. It was thin on purpose. A thick mattress would have kept the warmth of the floor away from the sleeper. A thin one let it through. Over the yo came the ibul, the quilt, also of padded cotton, or for the old and the well-to-do, of silk on the top side, often in deep reds and blues and greens, with a band of white cloth sewn along the edge where it touched the face, so that it could be taken off and washed. And at the head lay the pillow, the begae, a long, firm cylinder of cloth stuffed with the husks of buckwheat or rice, which shifted and settled under the head with a faint dry rustle, like sand.

The two round ends of the pillow were often the most beautiful thing in the room. They were covered with small round panels of embroidered silk, worked with flowers and butterflies and the characters for long life and many sons and good fortune. A bride brought pillows like these with her, made by her own hands and her mother's, and they were treasured for a lifetime.

Quilts were among the most precious things a family made. When a daughter was to be married, her mother and the women of her house would spend weeks, sometimes months, sewing the bedding she would take with her, quilts of silk in red and blue, the colors of a married couple, mattresses and pillows, all padded with the best new cotton the family could buy. Those wedding quilts were carried to the groom's house in the bridal procession, and laid out on the wedding night, and kept for years afterward, folded at the top of the stack, for best.

Lady Jeong's wedding quilts are in the little room across the hall tonight. They are red silk on top and blue silk underneath, with a white band along the edge, and her mother sewed every stitch of the padding with her own hands, at night, in the lamplight, all through the winter before the wedding. Lady Jeong did not know it at the time. Her mother only told her the morning she left.

Here is what that warm floor felt like to a stranger. A little over a century after our night, in the 1890s, the English traveler Isabella Bird made several long journeys through Korea, and in 1898 she published a book about them, called Korea and Her Neighbours. She slept, night after night, on the heated floors of country inns, where the fire under the floor was also the fire that cooked the food for the travelers' ponies. And she found them hot, far hotter than she liked. By her own account, the usual temperature of those rooms was somewhere between eighty and eighty-five degrees Fahrenheit, and it was frequently over ninety-two. More than once, she found a room too hot to sleep in at all.

Her Korean hosts, sleeping soundly on those same floors, would have found that very funny. A floor too hot for an Englishwoman was, to a Korean traveler coming in from a winter road, exactly right.

You are in the inner room now, watching the beds being laid. Lady Park kneels by the tall cabinet and lifts down the bedding, piece by piece, and passes it to Lady Jeong, who has come back across the hall for a moment to help, and Lady Jeong passes it to Suni, who carries each folded quilt across the floor in both arms, very seriously, as if it were something alive. The quilts are heavy and soft and smell faintly of the sun, because they were aired in the yard only three days ago, on a bright morning, hung over a rope between the persimmon tree and the eaves. Lady Park lays out the first mattress close against the kitchen wall, at the warmest end of the room. She smooths it with both hands. She lays the quilt over it, and turns down one corner, and sets the pillow at its head. That bed is for Lady Yun. The second bed goes next to it, a little smaller, for Suni. The third goes beside that, for Lady Park herself. You can feel the warmth of the floor rising through the thin mattress when you press your hand on it, a deep, even, living warmth, like the side of a sleeping animal.

Along the wall behind Lady Yun's bed stands the inner room's own folding screen. It has eight tall panels of painted silk, faded now to soft browns and greens, and it shows the ten things that were said to live forever. The sun. The clouds. The mountains. Water. Rocks. The pine tree. The turtle. The deer. The crane. And a little sacred mushroom, the herb of long life. Screens like this, painted with the ten symbols of long life, stood in many Korean houses where an old person slept, as a quiet wish for many more years. In the daytime, folded back against the wall, they were decoration. At night, opened around the head of a bed, they kept off the upper draft, and filled the dark with cranes and pines.

Lady Yun has slept beside this screen for twenty years. In the dark, she does not need to see it. She knows where every crane is.

Not everyone who slept on those floors agreed about them, any more than Isabella Bird and her hosts did. Many Koreans, then and now, believed that sleeping on a warm floor was good for the body, especially for old bones and aching backs, and that it was best for the feet and the body to be warm and the head to be cool, which is exactly what a heated floor and a drafty room provided. A good many foreign visitors, used to beds and fireplaces, found the hot floors hard to sleep on, and the cold air above them strange. Both, it seems, slept best in the way they had been raised.

Now lie down yourself, if you like, on the mattress that has been left for you, a little farther along the floor, toward the cooler end of the room. It is not cold there. It is only less warm. Lower yourself onto it slowly, as Lady Yun did, and feel the warmth rise to meet you, first through your hands as you lean on them, and then through your hips and shoulders, and then all along your back, steady and gentle and deep. Draw the quilt over you. It is heavy, heavier than you expect, and it settles over you like a hand laid flat on your chest. The pillow is firm and cool under your neck, and when you turn your head on it the buckwheat husks inside shift with a faint, dry whisper. Your feet, at the bottom of the quilt, are warm for the first time since this afternoon. Your face is cool. You can feel the air of the room on your cheeks and your forehead, crisp and clean and a little smoky, and that coolness on your face, above the warmth of the floor, is surprisingly pleasant, like lying in a warm bath in the open air on a winter night. The paper door is a pale gray square in the dark. Somewhere outside, very far off, a dog barks once. And through the floor beneath you, if you lie very still, you can almost feel the slow, patient work of the fire, far away under the stones.

Lady Yun is the last to be settled, and the most carefully. Lady Park helps her out of her padded vest, and folds it, and lays it at the foot of the bed, where she can reach it in the night. The winter cap stays on. Lady Yun lowers herself onto the mattress slowly, one hand on the floor and one on her daughter-in-law's arm, and lies back, and lets out a long sigh of comfort as the warmth of the floor comes up through the mattress into her back.

"Ah," she says. "That is the best moment of the day."

Lady Park draws the quilt up over her and tucks it along her side, and smooths it over her feet. She sets a small brass chamber pot, with its lid on, near the door, where anyone can find it in the dark without going out into the frozen yard. Every room had one. On a January night, nobody wanted to cross the yard.

Suni has crawled into her own bed and pulled the quilt up to her nose. Her eyes are bright over the edge of it. She has been thinking about something since supper.

"Mother," she whispers. "Why does Grandmother always sleep by the kitchen wall, every night?"

Lady Park looks at her over the lamp.

"Because she is Grandmother," she says. "Go to sleep."

That is not really an answer, and Suni knows it, and her mother knows it too. But it is very late for a girl of nine, and the floor is warm, and the quilt smells of the sun, and Suni's eyelids are heavy. She decides she will ask again tomorrow. She does not quite manage to decide anything after that.

Lady Park watches her for a moment over the low flame. Then she leans across and tucks the quilt more firmly around the small shoulders, and lifts the end of Suni's braid off her face and lays it on the pillow, and draws her own hand back slowly, so as not to wake her. She has done this every night for nine years. She did it for Hyeong-ju before that. Her own mother did it for her, in another house, in another village, by the light of another small lamp, a long time ago.

Lady Jeong bows to her mother-in-law and her husband's grandmother, kneeling, with her hands together, and says good night, and slides the door shut softly behind her, and crosses the cold hall to the little room on the other side, where her husband has already lain down, and the plum-blossom screen stands at the head of their quilts.

Across the yard, in the men's quarters, Kim Myeong-hwan has unrolled his own bedding, a plain mattress and a quilt of dark blue cotton, on the warm end of his own floor. He lies on his back, with his hands folded on his chest, and looks up into the dark, and thinks about the boundary of a field, and then about his son's voice dropping at the end of every line, and then about his own father, and then, quite suddenly, about nothing at all.

In the little room across the hall, Hyeong-ju and Lady Jeong lie under the red and blue quilts, side by side, not quite touching, with the plum-blossom screen standing at their heads. The floor of their room is not as warm as the inner room. Its fire mouth is smaller, and it was lit later. But it is warm enough, and the quilts are thick, and the screen keeps the upper draft off their faces. For a while she lies awake, listening to the house, to the creak of the beams as the cold tightens them, and the soft breathing of her husband beside her, and the wind paper on the door. She thinks about her mother's hands, sewing these quilts by lamplight. She thinks about Grandmother Han, saying that she has a light hand. She thinks, a little, about the sungnyung.

And then she is not thinking at all.

In the gate quarters, Dolsoe has been asleep for some time already, rolled in his quilt on his small hot floor, with the ox breathing on the other side of the wall.

In the inner room, the little lamp flame burns on for a while, no bigger than a seed, and then Lady Park reaches out and pinches the wick, and it goes out with a tiny hiss, and the room is dark. A thread of sweet smoke rises from the wick and fades.

She lies down. The floor is warm beneath her. Through the wall, behind her head, she can hear the faintest sound from the kitchen, a soft click as an ember settles in the fire mouth, and then nothing but the wind paper, humming its one thin note on the door.

## Part Ten — Around Midnight: Whispers About the New Year

It is the middle of the night, the deepest part of the third watch, and the house on the hill is silent. Only the oldest person in it is awake. Lady Yun lies with her eyes open in the dark, listening to the wind paper sing, as old people often lie awake for a while in the small hours, not troubled, simply awake.

Why does everyone in this house expect to grow a year older on the very same morning?

In Joseon Korea, as in much of East Asia, people did not count their age from their birthday. They counted the years they had lived in. A baby was one year old on the day she was born, because she was already living in her first year. And then, on the first morning of each new lunar year, everyone in the kingdom, from the king to the smallest child, became one year older together. Birthdays were remembered and could be celebrated, especially the great ones, like a sixtieth. But the age you gave, if someone asked, went up on New Year's Day.

So Lady Yun, who is seventy-two tonight, will be seventy-three on the first morning of the first month, a little less than four weeks from now. Suni, who is nine, will be ten. Lady Jeong will be nineteen. Even the ox, Dolsoe likes to say, will be a year older, though nobody has ever asked the ox.

Here is the small surprise in that. A child born in the last month of the year, in the twelfth month, the month we are in tonight, would be one year old at birth, and two years old a few weeks later, on New Year's morning, while still a tiny baby in her mother's arms. In Korean families, that was simply how it was, and nobody thought it odd.

Lady Yun stirs. Beside her, in the dark, a small voice whispers, "Grandmother. Are you awake?"

Suni has woken too, the way children sometimes wake in the night for no reason at all, warm and puzzled, not knowing where they are. She has wriggled across the gap between the two mattresses until her head is close to her grandmother's pillow.

"I am awake," Lady Yun whispers back. "Hush. Your mother is sleeping."

"Is it nearly morning?"

"No. It is the middle of the night. Lie still, and I will tell you something, and then you will go back to sleep."

So Suni lies still, with her quilt pulled up and her cheek on the edge of her grandmother's pillow, and Lady Yun tells her, in a whisper, about the New Year.

She tells her about the last day of the old year, when the whole house will be swept and scrubbed and every debt paid, if it can be. On that last night, every room in the house, the inner room and the men's room and the kitchen and even the gate quarters and the storerooms, will have a lamp lit in it, and the lamps will burn all night long, so that the old year goes out in light and no corner of the house is left dark. And the children of the house, she says, will try to stay awake all night with the grown-ups, to see the new year come, because it was said that a child who slept on that night would wake to find that their eyebrows had turned white.

"Did yours turn white?" Suni whispers.

"Every year," says Lady Yun. "I always fell asleep. And every year your great-grandmother dusted them with rice flour while I slept, and in the morning everybody laughed at me."

Suni giggles into the quilt, and Lady Yun lays a hand on her head.

She tells her about the morning. Before dawn, everyone will wash, and put on new clothes, or the best clothes they have, made over and pressed. Suni has a new jacket of rose-pink silk waiting folded in the chest, though she is not supposed to know that. Then the family will make the first offering of the year to the ancestors, at the little shrine at the back of the house, with the master and his son in their best robes and the table laid with food. And then the bows will begin.

The New Year bow was called sebae. The young bowed to the old, in turn, kneeling and touching their hands and foreheads to the floor, and the old gave them blessings and good wishes for the year, and often a sweet or a small coin. Suni will bow to her grandmother first, and then to her father and mother, and to her brother and his wife. Then the neighbors will come, and the children of the lane will go from house to house to bow to all the old people of the village, and come home with their sleeves full of sweets.

And everyone, Lady Yun says, will eat a bowl of rice-cake soup, with white slices of rice cake floating in a clear broth, because you cannot become a year older without it.

"Then I will eat two bowls," Suni whispers, "and be eleven."

"Then you will have a stomachache," says Lady Yun, "and be ten."

You are lying very still on your own mattress, a little way along the floor, listening to them whisper in the dark. You cannot see their faces. You can only hear the two voices, one very old and dry and soft, like the rustle of paper, and one very young and quick, breaking now and then into a muffled giggle. The room is black and warm. The floor under you is still warm, though less warm than it was at bedtime, and the air on your face is colder now, much colder. You can feel the night pressing against the paper doors, the great cold of a clear January night on a hill, and you are inside it, under a heavy quilt, on a floor that holds the memory of a fire. The wind paper hums. A beam in the roof gives a single sharp crack as the frost tightens it, and you hear Lady Yun say, quite calmly, "That is the cold," as if introducing an old and familiar acquaintance. Then the whispering goes on.

Respect for the old was never only a household matter in Joseon Korea. It was part of how the kingdom saw itself. And five years after our night, it would be shown in one of the most famous events of the whole reign.

In the spring of 1795, King Jeongjo traveled south from the capital to the new fortress city he was building at Hwaseong, in what is now Suwon, to hold a great celebration for the sixtieth birthday of his mother, Lady Hyegyeong. The journey and the festivities lasted eight days, and a royal record of them was later compiled, in words and in long, detailed painted scenes, and completed in 1797. Among the events it records was a banquet for the old people of the district, held in one of the halls of the temporary palace there. The elders of the town were brought in and seated and served at the king's own command, as a mark of honor to age itself. The paintings of that record, and its careful lists, survive, and they are among the most beautiful documents of the dynasty.

The sixtieth birthday mattered so much because of the old way of counting years. The calendar of East Asia ran in great cycles of sixty years, each year with its own name, made from the pairing of ten heavenly signs and twelve earthly ones, the twelve animals among them. A person who reached sixty had lived through one whole cycle and come back to the year of their birth. That birthday was called hwangap, the return of the cycle, and in a Korean family it was the greatest birthday of a life, celebrated with a feast and towers of fruit and rice cakes, and with every child and grandchild bowing in turn.

Lady Yun's own sixtieth birthday feast was held years ago, in this very house. The table was laid in the wooden hall, and the whole village came up the hill. Kim Myeong-hwan, in his best robe, poured her the first cup of wine with both hands, and Hyeong-ju, then a very small boy, recited a poem he had learned for the day and forgot the last line, and burst into tears, and was comforted with a rice cake. She remembers it better than almost anything. She remembers that the weather was fine.

A king honoring his mother on her sixtieth birthday, and the old men and women of a provincial town sitting at a feast in his presence. To people like the Kims, that was not a strange or distant thing. It was the same idea that ran through their own house, written large across a kingdom.

Historians who study Joseon households sometimes describe two kinds of order living side by side in the same house. One was the written order of Confucian ritual, set down in books of family rites, kept by men, centered on the ancestors and the shrine and the proper way to bow and mourn and marry. The other was the older, unwritten order of the household spirits, the spirit of the hearth and the spirit of the house beam and the spirit of the storeroom, tended mostly by women, with bowls of clean water and small offerings of rice. Some scholars stress the tension between the two, and the efforts of officials to discourage the second. Others stress how comfortably, in most ordinary homes, they simply lived together, the way the Kims' New Year will begin at the ancestors' shrine and end, that night, with a bowl of fresh water at the hearth.

Suni's whispers are growing slower. The gaps between them are growing longer.

Lady Yun goes on talking anyway, more softly now, more for herself than for the child. She talks about the New Year kites that the boys will fly over the frozen fields in the first weeks of the year, high and square and bright, with their strings tugging in the cold wind. She talks about the full moon of the first month, and the nuts that everyone will crack between their teeth that morning, for strong teeth all year. She talks about her own mother, who made the best rice cakes in her village, and who used to let her eat the broken ones from the edge of the steamer, still hot, dipped in a little honey from the jar on the shelf.

"Grandmother," she murmurs at last, "why do you always sleep by the kitchen wall?"

Lady Yun smiles in the dark.

"Ask me in the morning," she says. "When you are awake enough to remember the answer."

But Suni is already asleep, her breath slow and even, her cheek still on the edge of her grandmother's pillow.

A little while later, there is a soft rustle on the far side of the room. Lady Park has woken, as she always wakes at about this hour on the coldest nights, without needing to be called. She lies for a moment with her palm on the floor beside her, feeling its warmth, and judges that it is cooling a little faster than she would like. So she rises, quietly, and pulls her padded jacket around her shoulders, and slides the door open just enough to slip through, and closes it behind her.

The hall is bitterly cold under her stockinged feet. The stars are very bright over the courtyard. She goes down the kitchen steps, carefully, in the dark, feeling for each one with her toes. You follow her, if you like, as quietly as you can. The cold in the kitchen is a different cold from the cold of the hall, a damp, earthy, mineral cold, smelling of ash and clay and old smoke, and it climbs up through your feet from the beaten floor. The kitchen is completely black, except for one thing. Low down in the wall in front of you, around the edges of the stone that covers the fire mouth, there is a thin, broken line of red light, like the light under a door in a dark corridor. It is the only light in the whole house. You crouch beside Lady Park in front of it, and you feel its warmth on your knees and your face before she has even moved the stone. At the bottom she kneels in front of the fire mouth and lifts the flat stone away.

Inside, the embers are still glowing, low and red, under a soft gray skin of ash. She breathes on them gently, once, and they brighten. She lays on one small handful of dry brushwood, and a few pine needles, and a little flame wakes and runs along the twigs and stands up, yellow and quiet. Not a roaring fire. Just enough. She watches it for a few breaths, warming her hands, her face lit gold from below in the dark kitchen. Then she sets the stone back against the mouth, leaving its narrow gap, and climbs the steps, and slips back into the warm room, and into her bed.

Under the floor, the new warmth begins its slow journey along the road of stones, toward the place where the old woman and the little girl lie sleeping.

## Part Eleven — Around One in the Morning: The Warmest Place on the Floor

The fourth watch of the night has begun, and the little fire that Lady Park woke has settled again into embers. Under the floor of the inner room, its new warmth has reached the near end of the stones, by the kitchen wall, and is spreading slowly outward into the dark.

Where would a Korean family keep a bowl of rice warm, through a long winter evening, for someone who was coming home late?

The answer, in house after house, was the same. They kept it on the warmest part of the floor, by the kitchen wall, tucked under the edge of a quilt. A bowl of rice with its lid on, set down on the hot floor near the fire mouth's side of the room and covered with a folded quilt, would stay warm for hours, ready for a husband coming home late from market, or a son coming back from a long walk in the snow. Many Koreans who grew up in such houses, long into the twentieth century, remember that covered bowl, waiting under the quilt, as one of the gentlest signs of being cared for.

That warmest part of the floor had a name, as we heard at supper. It was the araenmok. And it was never just a place. It was a place of honor.

Behind that honor stood a short passage from a very old book. The Book of Rites, one of the classics of the Confucian tradition, put together in China more than two thousand years ago, sets out the small duties of a son or daughter toward their parents. And among them it says, in a few plain words, that in winter a child should keep the parents warm, and in summer keep them cool, and in the evening settle them for the night, and in the morning ask how they have slept. That passage was copied into the Lesser Learning, a book of moral lessons for the young compiled in China in 1187, which Joseon children of families like the Kims' learned almost by heart. Koreans came to sum it up in four short syllables, honjeong sinseong, settling in the evening and inquiring in the morning. It is what Hyeong-ju was doing tonight, when he knelt outside his grandmother's door and asked whether the floor was warm enough.

Historians who read the old books of family manners are careful to say that they describe an ideal, and that real households were always looser and kinder and more muddled than any book. A grandmother might give up the warm place to a sick grandchild. A tired daughter-in-law might be told to lie down by the wall for once. But they agree that the ideal itself was widely shared, by rich and poor alike, and that it shaped where people sat and slept, every night, for centuries.

And so, here is the answer to our third question. Who gets the warmest place on the floor, and why?

The eldest. In this house, it is Lady Yun. The warm end of the inner room, by the kitchen wall, closest to the fire mouth, is hers, and it has been hers for many winters, as it was her own mother-in-law's before her. In the men's room across the yard, the warm end belongs to the master, Kim Myeong-hwan, and if a guest of standing came to stay, he would give it to his guest. And the cooler end of each room, the witmok, near the chimney, belongs to the youngest.

The reason has two sides, and they fit together like the two halves of a folded quilt. One side is order. In a Korean household of this time, the old came before the young, in greeting, in serving, in seating, in speech. That was one of the great relationships on which a good life was thought to rest: between elder and younger, there should be order. The warm place by the wall was simply that order, made into warmth. The other side is care. The old feel the cold most. Their hands ache, and their backs, and their knees, and they lie awake. So the warmest stones are given to the body that needs them most, by the hands of the people that body once fed and carried and kept warm, many winters ago.

It is honor. And it is also love, spoken in the only language a floor understands.

And the young do not go cold for it. Look at Suni, asleep tonight with her cheek on the very edge of her grandmother's pillow, as close to the warm wall as she can wriggle. The araenmok is the grandmother's place. But a grandmother's place, in the dark, has always had room at its edge for a child.

Now the house is quiet, all of it, from one end to the other.

You are lying on your mattress in the inner room, in the soft dark. The floor beneath you has grown warm again, gently, from the fire Lady Park fed at midnight. You can feel it rising through the thin mattress, through your back, slow and even. The quilt lies heavy over you. Your feet are warm. Your face is cool. The air of the room smells of clean cotton and old smoke and very faintly of the lamp oil that burned here earlier. You can hear Lady Yun's breathing, slow and soft, with a little catch at the top of each breath. You can hear Suni's breathing, quicker and lighter, beside it. You can hear Lady Park, farther along the floor, breathing deep and steady, the sleep of someone who has worked hard since before dawn. The wind paper on the door has fallen silent. The wind has dropped. The night outside is perfectly still now, and perfectly clear, and very cold, and you are perfectly warm.

There is nothing for you to do. Nothing to watch, nothing to carry, nothing to remember. The fire is fed. The gate is barred. The jars are covered in their straw hut. The ox is asleep. The house is asleep.

Let your hands rest on the quilt. Let your breathing slow, the way theirs has.

Outside, the whole hill is sleeping too.

The frost is settling now, the deep frost of the hours after midnight. It is forming on the tiles of the roof, along the ridges and in the curved hollows of each tile, a fine white fur of ice that glitters faintly under the stars. It is forming on the straw roof of the little hut over the kimchi jars, so that every stalk of straw has its own thin white edge. It is forming on the wooden bar across the gate, and on the rim of the turned-over bucket by the well, and on the flat stone at the top of the garden where Suni will stand tomorrow to look at the river.

Down below, the river lies under its ice, gray and silent, all the way to the dark line of the far hills. The ice men have gone home. Their sledges stand on the bank, frosted white. The great ice house at the foot of the hill is shut and dark, full to the roof with blocks of winter, packed in straw, waiting for August.

Far away to the east, the city sleeps behind its wall. The great gates are closed. The streets are empty. The bell hangs silent in its pavilion on Bell Street, waiting for the morning. In house after house, thousands upon thousands of them, inside the wall and out of it, families like this one lie on their warm floors, in their quilts, in the dark, with the old by the kitchen walls and the young along the cooler stones, and small fires breathe softly in fire mouth after fire mouth, all through the sleeping valley.

Behind the inner house, the short chimney gives out a last thin thread of warmth into the still air. It rises a little way, and spreads, and is gone. If you could see it, it would be like a breath on a cold morning. Like the house breathing out in its sleep.

You do not need to see it. You can feel it, under you, all around you. The house is breathing. Slowly. Warmly. In, and out.

Think, if you like, of the small things of this long day, one by one, the way you might turn over smooth pebbles in your hand. The pine needles crackling as they caught in the fire mouth this afternoon. The smell of the rice when the great lid came off, and the cloud of white steam rising into the cold. The little brown bowl of sungnyung, and the old woman saying, run it a little high tomorrow too. Two chestnuts, black and split, on a scrap of paper on a scholar's table. Two inkstones, warming side by side on the floor. The quick wooden rhythm of the clubs on the cloth, and the whole village answering in the dark. Lady Jeong's patient voice, saying smaller, smaller than you think. The lantern in the straw hut, and the ice on the radish water. The far, slow strokes of the great bell. A red and blue quilt. A painted crane.

Let them go, one by one, as easily as they came.

There is nothing left in this house tonight that needs you. Everything has been done, and done well, and it will all still be here in the morning.



Across the hall, in the little room with the plum-blossom screen, Hyeong-ju and Lady Jeong are asleep under the red and blue quilts that her mother made. Somewhere in the night, without waking, she has turned toward him, and his arm lies loosely over the quilt across her shoulder. Her hair is coming loose from its pin. Her face is calm. The cool end of their small floor is no colder than it was. They do not notice it at all.

Across the frozen yard, in the men's quarters, Kim Myeong-hwan sleeps on his back on the warm end of his own floor, his hands still folded on his chest, his breathing slow. On the writing table beside him, the two inkstones sit side by side, cold now, where his son left his beside his own. The old fur vest hangs on its peg. The brazier has gone out, and its ash is gray and soft.

In the gate quarters, Dolsoe sleeps rolled in his quilt like a caterpillar in a leaf, with one arm flung out across the warm floor, toward the wall where the ox lies breathing on the other side. Every so often the ox shifts in its straw, and its blanket rustles, and Dolsoe, without waking, murmurs something that might be its name.

And in the inner room, Lady Yun has turned her head a little on her pillow, toward the child. Her hand has found Suni's small hand on the quilt between them, and holds it loosely, as she has held a great many small hands on this floor, over more than fifty years, in winters gone by. The silver pin lies beside her pillow, where she always puts it. The painted cranes stand silent on the screen behind her head.

The warm place by the wall is doing what it was made for.

And at the far side of the room, Lady Park sleeps on her side, with one arm stretched out on the floor beyond the edge of her quilt, her palm flat on the warm paper, as if even in her sleep she were keeping a hand on the house. She has been awake since before dawn. She drew the first water from the well, and poured it into the white bowl on the hearth, and lit the morning fire, and cooked two meals for seven people, and beaten and pressed a length of cloth, and fetched the kimchi, and fed the fire again at midnight. Now, at last, she has nothing more to do until the morning. Her face is smooth and quiet. Her breathing is the slowest in the room.

Beside her, the keys rest in the chest where her hand can find them. Beside them, the folded sock with its neatly turned toe waits for Suni to finish it tomorrow. The lamp stands cold on its tall stand, its little dish of oil dark and still.

Everyone is asleep now. Every single one.

The old woman by the warm wall. The little girl at her pillow's edge. The mother with her hand on the floor. The young wife and her husband under the red and blue quilts. The scholar in the men's room, with his hands on his chest. The hired man by the ox. And the ox.

Seven people, and one patient animal, and one house on a hill, all breathing together in the dark.

Nothing moves in the house now except the warmth. It moves very slowly, the way warmth always moves, from the fire to the stones, from the stones to the clay, from the clay to the paper, from the paper to the quilts, and from the quilts, at last, to the sleepers. It asks nothing of anyone. It simply goes where it is needed.



Down in the kitchen, in the deep dark, the fire mouth glows behind its flat stone, a thin red line in the blackness, and the warmth goes on flowing, slowly, patiently, under the floor, along its road of stones, toward the old woman and the sleeping child.

## Part Twelve — From the Small Hours to First Light: The Fire Mouth at Dawn

The hours go by, slowly, softly, one after another. The fifth watch comes. The stars wheel over the courtyard. And the house on the hill sleeps on.

What is the first sound of morning, in a house like this one?

It is not a sound you would notice, if you were not listening for it. Long before any light, far away to the east, beyond the river and the fields, the great bell in the city begins to speak again. This time it is the morning ringing, the slow strokes that open the gates. On a still night like this one, it comes across the frozen country so faintly that it is less a sound than a feeling, a soft, deep stirring in the air. One stroke. A long silence. Another.

No one in the house wakes for it. Lady Yun's breathing changes a little, and then goes on. Dolsoe turns over in his quilt. The ox lifts its head in the dark, and listens, and lays it down again on the straw.

You hear it, though. You can lie and listen to it, if you like, and not count the strokes, and let each one come and go like a slow wave on a quiet shore.

Somewhere down the lane, a rooster stirs on its perch, and thinks about crowing, and decides it is still too early.

Outside, the night is at its coldest now. This is always the coldest hour, just before the dawn, when the ground has given up the last of yesterday's warmth to the sky. The frost on the roof tiles is thick and white. The straw hut over the jars is stiff with it. The water in the big jar by the kitchen door has a skin of ice on top, thin as paper.

But inside, under the quilts, on the warm floors, nobody feels it.

Think back to the Dutchman, Hamel, and the words he wrote so long ago about the floors of Korea. Warm, he said, like a baker's oven. He had lain on floors like these, through thirteen Korean winters, far from home. And in all the cold of those years, every night, the floor had been warm beneath him.

It is warm beneath you now.

Let yourself sink into it. Let it hold you up. Your back, your shoulders, your arms, your legs, all heavy and loose and warm. Your feet warm at the bottom of the quilt. Your face cool in the dark air. Your breathing slow, so slow, almost as slow as Lady Yun's.

There is no hurry. There is nowhere to be. The night is nearly over, but not quite, and the part of it that remains belongs to sleep.

The hours pass. The bell has long since fallen silent.

Let us walk through the sleeping house one last time, very quietly, room by room, the way Dolsoe walked through the yard at bedtime. You do not need to get up. You can go in your mind, and leave your body here, warm under the quilt.

Begin here, in the inner room. The dark is soft and brown. The screen with its cranes and pines stands folded around the old woman's head. The child lies curled at her pillow. The mother lies with her hand on the floor. Three slow breaths, one quick one. The floor holds them all.

Slide the door open, just a crack, and slip out onto the wooden hall. The cold touches your face like cool water. Above the courtyard, the stars are still there, but fewer now, and paler. Across the hall, the little room with the plum-blossom screen is silent. Two people breathing, very close, under red and blue silk. Leave them be.

Step down, in your mind, into the frosty yard. The frost crunches very softly under your feet. The well stands dark, with its bucket turned over on the stones. The kimchi hut stands white with frost in the corner of the garden, its straw roof glittering faintly. The big brown jars on the terrace wear little caps of white on their lids.

Cross to the men's quarters. The paper door is dark. Behind it, a scholar sleeps on a warm floor, beside two cold inkstones and a pair of folded hands. Under the eaves, the small fire mouth is quiet, its stone in place, a little warmth still breathing from the gap.

Go on to the gate. The bar lies across it, frosted. Beside it, the gate quarters, and the ox in its stall, a great warm shape breathing slow clouds into the dark, its straw blanket white at the edges with frost. On the other side of the wall, the hired man sleeps with his arm flung out toward it.

And then come back, slowly, across the yard, past the frosted jars, down the three earth steps into the dark kitchen. There, low in the wall, behind its stone, the thin red line of light is still glowing. Crouch beside it for a moment. Hold out your hands. Feel its warmth, small and steady, on your palms and on your face.

Then come back up the steps, and across the hall, and into the inner room, and lie down again in your own warm place on the floor, and pull the quilt up to your chin.

There. Everyone is where they should be.

And then, very slowly, something begins to change.

Through the paper of the door, which all night has been a dim gray square, there is a faint new paleness. It is so slight at first that you are not sure you have seen it. It is not light yet. It is only a little less dark. Over the hills to the east, beyond the sleeping city, the sky is turning from black to the deepest blue, and then to a gray, very soft, very faint, along the line of the ridges, like the first gray of ash on a fire that is going out, or the first gray of dawn on a fire that is about to wake.

The rooster down the lane makes up his mind at last, and crows, once, rusty and cheerful. Another answers from farther away. A dog shakes itself and yawns.

The river below is still gray and white and silent, but now you can see it again, a long pale band in the dark, and the dark line of the far bank beyond it, and the shapes of the trees.

On the flat stone at the top of the garden, the frost begins, very faintly, to sparkle.

The gray along the ridges grows a little wider, and a little paler. Then, very slowly, the lowest edge of it warms, from gray to the faintest rose, the color of the inside of a shell. The stars over the courtyard go out, one by one, the brightest last, until only one or two are left, very high, very small. The tiles of the roof come out of the dark, frost-white, ridge after ridge.

Down the hill, in another house, someone has woken early. A thin new thread of smoke rises from a short chimney there, straight up into the still air, gray against the rose, and leans a little, and drifts away over the river. Then another, a little farther off. The village is beginning, very gently, to light its floors.

But in this house, not yet.

The paper door of the inner room has turned from gray to a soft, pale pearl. In that faint light you can just make out the shapes in the room now. The tall stand of the cold lamp. The folded vest at the foot of the old woman's bed. The round, embroidered end of a pillow, with its little silk flowers. The child's braid, dark across the white edge of a quilt. The mother's hand on the floor, palm down, fingers loose.

Everything is still. Everything is warm. Everything is exactly as it should be.

A single sparrow lands on the frosted ridge of the roof, and fluffs out its feathers against the cold, and sits there, round and brown, waiting for the sun. It does not sing yet. It is too early even for that.



In a little while, Lady Park will wake. She will open her eyes in the gray light and lie still for one moment, as she does every morning, with her palm on the floor, feeling how much warmth is left. Then she will rise, and wrap her padded jacket around her, and go out into the cold, and cross the frosty yard to the well, and draw the first water of the day, the cleanest water, and carry it down into the kitchen, and pour it, fresh and clear, into the small white bowl on the shelf above the hearth.

Then she will kneel in front of the fire mouth, and lift away the flat stone. She will rake back the soft gray ash with the iron poker, and find beneath it, still glowing, the little red heart of last night's fire. She will lay on a few pieces of Dolsoe's pine root, dark and sticky with sap. And she will lean down and breathe on the embers, gently, once, twice.

And the pine root will catch with a bright, sweet, crackling flame, and the fire will wake, and leap up yellow and orange in the dark kitchen, and begin, once again, its long journey under the floor.

In a little while after that, Lady Jeong will wake, and remember where she is, and smile, and go to help. Dolsoe will wake, and stretch, and go out to the ox, and the ox will want its porridge. The master will wake in the men's room and lie for a moment looking at the gray light on the paper door. Hyeong-ju will wake and reach for his book. And Suni will wake, last of all, and remember the stone at the top of the garden, and the river, and the ice men, and jump up, and be told to walk like a young lady.

And Lady Yun will wake on her warm place by the kitchen wall, and feel the new warmth already coming into the stones beneath her, and know, without being told, that the fire has been lit.

All of that is still to come. The water, the fire, the rice, the voices, the ordinary bright noise of a winter day. It will come in its own time, the way it has come every morning in this house for longer than anyone can remember, and the way it will come again tomorrow, and the day after, and every morning until spring.

You do not have to wait for it. You do not have to be awake for any of it.

You can stay just where you are, on the warm floor, under the heavy quilt, with your eyes closed and your hands loose and your breath slow. The stones beneath you still hold the last of last night's fire. They will hold it a little longer. Long enough. Let the warmth come up into you one more time, softly, through your back and your shoulders, and let it carry you down, the way warm water carries a leaf, slowly, gently, all the way down into sleep.

But not yet. Not yet.

For now, everyone is still asleep.

Lady Yun is asleep by the warm wall, her silver pin beside her pillow, her hand still resting near the little hand of her granddaughter. Suni is asleep at the edge of her grandmother's pillow, her braid across her cheek, dreaming, perhaps, of rose-pink silk and two bowls of rice-cake soup. Lady Park is asleep with her palm flat on the floor, keeping a hand on the house. Lady Jeong is asleep under her mother's red and blue quilts, her face calm, a long way from her mother's village and not lonely at all. Hyeong-ju is asleep beside her, his grandfather's fur vest folded at his head. Kim Myeong-hwan is asleep in the men's room, his hands on his chest, his two inkstones side by side on the table, waiting. And Dolsoe is asleep in the gate quarters, rolled in his quilt by the warm wall, with the old brown ox breathing slow and steady on the other side.

The house is quiet. The house is warm. The night has passed safely, every hour of it.

And down in the kitchen, in the first gray light, low in the clay wall beneath the great iron cauldron, behind its flat stone, under its soft blanket of ash, the fire mouth of the floor is still glowing, red and patient and alive, just as it was when we found it yesterday afternoon. It has warmed the stones all night. It has kept an old woman and a sleeping child warm through a January night on a frozen hill. And it is waiting, quietly, for the morning, and for the hands that will wake it.

If you enjoyed spending this winter night with the Kims, a like or a subscription would mean a great deal, but only if you truly enjoyed it. And if there is a time or a place you would love to spend a night in, leave it in the comments, because your ideas truly help.

Next time, we will visit a very different kind of house, on a very different kind of night, somewhere far from the warm floors of Korea.

Sleep well, friend. Good night.
