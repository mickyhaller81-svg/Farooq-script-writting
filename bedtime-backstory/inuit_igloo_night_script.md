# How the Inuit Survived the Arctic Night in Igloos | History for Sleep

## Part One — Around Half Past One in the Afternoon: The Last Blue Light

It is December, in the year 1922, on the frozen sea of the Central Canadian Arctic, and the short blue light of noon is already going. Out on the sea ice, a small cluster of white domes sits low against the snow. Inside one of them, a stone lamp shaped like a half moon holds a long, even row of small yellow flames. The air outside is thirty-five degrees below zero, and so dry that it squeaks underfoot. Inside, the snow walls glow faintly gold, and an old woman leans forward to touch the wick.

Not the igloo of the picture books, with a penguin standing politely at the door. Penguins, as it happens, live at the other end of the world. Just one ordinary family, on one ordinary winter night, in one ordinary house made of snow.

And without a few old tricks, you would not have lasted until morning, not out here, where there is no tree, no coal and no sunrise for weeks on end.

So tonight I would like you to carry three small questions with you. The first is this. How can a house made of snow be warm? The second. How do you cook a supper, and melt your drinking water, when there is no wood for hundreds and hundreds of miles in any direction? And the third. How do you keep time, how do you know the hour and the day and the season, when the sun does not rise at all?

These are not three separate questions. They are three windows into the same small house, on the same long night, and we will look through each of them in its own time.

This family never quite existed under one name, but every detail of their night is real.

So let your head grow heavy now. Let your shoulders loosen and drop. Your eyes may close whenever they like, and the lamp will keep burning without you. Let us go back, gently, to the last blue light of a December afternoon, far out on the frozen sea. So let us begin.

The village is very small. There are three snow houses here, and a few smaller domes beside them for storing meat and gear, and a scatter of sleds lying on their sides with their runners turned up to the sky. The houses stand on the sea ice of Foxe Basin, a great shallow bay of the Arctic Ocean, not far from the island of Iglulik, off the coast of the Melville Peninsula. The people who live here call themselves Iglulingmiut, which means, simply, the people of the place of houses.

The light is strange. It is not quite day and it is not quite night. Low in the south, along the edge of the world, there is a long band of pale lemon and rose, and above it the sky deepens through green into a clear, cold blue. The snow takes the color of the sky, so that everything around you is faintly blue, as if the whole world were under water. There are no shadows. And the sun, which should be somewhere behind that glowing band, is not there at all.

The sun has not risen over this village since the last days of November. It will not rise again until the middle of January. For about six weeks in the very heart of winter, the only daylight here is this long twilight around noon, a few hours of blue and rose and gold, rising and fading again without the sun ever lifting its edge above the horizon. Today is the twenty-first of December. It is the shortest day of the year, and tonight will be the longest night.

Now let us meet the family in the largest of the three houses.

The old woman by the lamp is called Ivalu. She is sixty-six years old, a widow, and the mother of the man of the house. Her name means sinew thread, the fine, strong thread drawn from the back of a caribou, which every woman here uses for sewing. It suits her, because Ivalu is the finest seamstress in the village. But her first work, before anything else, is the lamp. She is its keeper. She tends it in the morning and in the evening and through the night, and it has not gone fully dark in her care for as long as anyone in this house can remember.

Beside her, mending a boot, is her daughter-in-law, Arnatsiaq, who is thirty-five. Arnatsiaq is quick and practical and quietly funny, and on her back, inside the deep hood of her parka, a baby girl is asleep. The baby is called Ukpik, which means snowy owl. She is a little over one year old, and she rides warm against her mother's skin for much of the day, inside the great pouch of the woman's parka that is called an amauti.

Sitting cross-legged on the sleeping platform, with a tangle of sealskin line in her lap, is Qaunaq, who is eight. She is supposed to be untangling the line. She is mostly watching the door.

Because two people are missing. Out on the sea ice, beyond the last of the houses, the man of the house is hunting. His name is Kunnuk, and he is forty-one, a patient, broad-shouldered seal hunter, known for his good dogs and his steady hands. With him is his son Kaujak, who is thirteen, and who has only this winter been allowed to wait at a seal's breathing hole on his own.

So that is the whole household. Six people under one roof of snow.

In the other two houses live their neighbors. One belongs to Kunnuk's cousin and his wife and their three small sons. The other belongs to an old couple and their grown daughter, who have hunted and traveled with this family for many winters. Out here, nobody lives entirely alone. The three households move together, camp together and share their seal meat together, because on the winter ice a family that keeps to itself is a family that sooner or later goes hungry. But tonight we will stay in this one house, with these six people, from the last of the blue light until the first of it returns.

Why do they live out here, on the frozen sea, and not on the solid land a few miles away?

The answer is the seal. In summer, the people of this country spread out along the coasts and inland, hunting caribou, fishing in the rivers, gathering what the short warm season gives. But in the dark of winter, the caribou are far away and thin, the rivers are frozen solid, and the one great source of food is under the ice. The ringed seal lives all winter beneath the frozen sea, and it must breathe. So, all winter, it keeps a few small breathing holes open through the ice, scratching them clear with the strong claws of its front flippers as the ice thickens around it.

And so, every winter, the families of this coast move out onto the ice itself, to live on top of the seals. They build their houses on the frozen sea, often several miles from shore, close to the places where the hunters can find breathing holes. When the hunting grows poor, they pack their sleds and move on, and build new houses somewhere else. A snow house is not meant to last forever. It is meant to last as long as it is useful, and then to be left behind, like a coat you no longer need.

Here is something that may surprise you. The word igloo, which the rest of the world uses for a dome of snow, simply means house in the language of these people. Iglu can be a house of stone and turf, or a house of wood, or the snow house you are sitting in tonight. The particular word for a house built of snow is a longer one. And in fact, across the wide Arctic, most Inuit did not live in snow houses at all. In Alaska and in Greenland, winter homes were usually built of turf, stone, whalebone and driftwood. The true snow-house country was the middle of the Arctic, in what is now Canada, including this coast of Foxe Basin, where snow houses were the ordinary winter home of every family.

One of the first outsiders to write about these houses in careful detail was a young German scientist called Franz Boas. In 1883 he sailed to Baffin Island, across the water from here, and spent a long winter traveling with Inuit families by dog sled, sleeping in their snow houses and writing down everything he could. Five years later, in 1888, he published a long report called The Central Eskimo. In it he describes how a snow house is built, and his description is very plain. "Two men unite in building a house," he wrote, "the one cutting the blocks, the other building." Boas later became one of the best-known scholars in America. But it was a winter of snow houses and seal-oil lamps on Baffin Island that first set him on that road.

And this very winter, as it happens, another expedition is in this country, writing things down. Not far to the south, near the bay that the whalers called Repulse Bay, a party of Danish and Greenlandic travelers has made its headquarters on a small island. They are the Fifth Thule Expedition, led by the Greenlander and Dane Knud Rasmussen, who speaks a language very close to the one spoken in this house. He and his companions have spent the past year visiting families along this coast, listening to their stories and their songs. We will hear some of what he wrote down, later tonight.

You might picture a modern winter, with a furnace humming in the basement and a light switch by every door. Here there is no switch and no furnace. There is one stone lamp, and the snow, and the people.

Historians are not quite sure how old the snow house is. Some believe it was developed by the Thule people, the ancestors of today's Inuit, who spread east across the Arctic from Alaska roughly a thousand years ago, bringing dogs and sleds and new ways of hunting. Others suspect that the idea is far older, and simply invisible to us, because a house made of snow leaves almost nothing behind when spring comes. Both views are honest guesses. A snow house melts, and its story melts with it.

Inside the big house, Ivalu is not thinking about any of this. She is thinking about the lamp.

You are sitting beside her now, on the edge of the sleeping platform, with a caribou skin around your shoulders. The lamp is in front of you, resting on a little stand of wood and snow. It is carved from a single piece of soft gray stone, about as long as your forearm, shaped like a flat half moon. The curved side faces you. Along the straight front edge runs a narrow lip, and along that lip lies the wick, a low, neat ridge of dried moss mixed with the white fluff of Arctic cotton. Behind the wick, in the shallow bowl of the lamp, there is a pool of clear, pale golden oil, and in the oil there are a few lumps of white seal fat, slowly melting. The flame is not one flame. It is a long, low row of small flames, side by side, like a little fence of light.

It smells faintly of the sea. It smells of warm fat and of moss and of fur. And it makes almost no smoke at all.

Ivalu picks up a slim stick of bone, no longer than a pencil. With its tip, she nudges the wick, very gently, pressing a little more of it down into the oil, and lifting another part of it up. The flames shrink in one place and grow in another, until the whole row is even again, each little tongue of fire the same height, bright yellow, steady, with no dark smoke curling from the top. She is doing it without looking. She has done it so many thousands of times that her hands know the lamp the way they know her own face.

Above the lamp, a wooden rack hangs from the snow ceiling on cords of sealskin. On the rack, a pair of small fur stockings is drying, and a pair of boots. And above that, the whole curved roof of the house is glowing, very softly, with the reflected light, like the inside of a shell.

Arnatsiaq looks up from her boot.

"They will be back soon," she says. "Aupaluk will be hungry."

Aupaluk is the lead dog, a big, rust-colored animal with one ear that never quite stands up. Qaunaq laughs at that, because Aupaluk is always hungry, and the baby stirs in the hood and settles again.

Ivalu says nothing. She only nudges the wick once more with her bone stick, and the row of little flames grows a fraction brighter, ready for the men coming home in the dark.

Outside, the band of rose along the southern sky is fading to gray. In the house, the lamp burns on.

## Part Two — Around Half Past Two in the Afternoon: The Hunters Come Home

By half past two, the rose along the southern sky has faded to a thin gray line, and the first stars are coming out above it, one by one, in a sky that is still faintly blue.

How do you catch a seal that you cannot see, through ice that is thicker than a man is tall?

That is the question Kunnuk has been answering all day, standing very still in the cold. And the answer begins not with the hunter, but with his dogs.

By late December, the ice of Foxe Basin is several feet thick, and it is covered with a deep crust of wind-packed snow. A seal's breathing hole is hidden somewhere under that crust. From the surface, there is nothing at all to see, only a faint, low hump in the snow, no bigger than an upturned bowl, and most of the time not even that. But a dog can smell the seal. So the hunter walks out across the ice with one or two of his dogs on a line, letting them nose along the snow, until one of them stops, and sniffs, and whines, and digs at a particular spot.

Then the hunter takes over. He drives a long, thin probe of antler or bone down through the snow, feeling carefully, until he finds the small, round opening in the ice below. He clears the snow very little, because the seal must not see light or hear footsteps. Over the hole he sets a tiny indicator, often a thread of fine down or a slender sliver of bone, resting so that it will tremble when the water beneath it rises. And then he stands, with his harpoon ready, and waits.

He may wait for an hour. He may wait for five hours. He may wait until the twilight is gone and come home with nothing. Many hunters stand on a little pad of fur, to keep their feet from creaking on the snow and to keep the cold from coming up through their boots. They do not stamp. They do not sing. They hardly move at all.

And then, perhaps, the down trembles. There is a soft breath of air from beneath the snow, the sound of a seal breathing out. The hunter thrusts the harpoon straight down through the snow and into the hole with all his strength. The harpoon head is made to come away from the shaft and turn sideways inside the seal, so that it holds fast, tied to a strong line of sealskin. And then the long struggle begins, of hauling the seal up through the narrow hole, which the hunter must quickly widen with an ice pick.

Here is something that may surprise you. A ringed seal does not keep just one breathing hole. It keeps several, spread out over a wide area of ice, and it visits them in turn, so that a hunter at one hole may be waiting for a seal that is breathing quietly, a little way off, at another. That is why the families of a winter village often hunted together. If several men each wait at a different hole, the seal must come up at one of them sooner or later, and whoever catches it shares the meat with everyone. Patience, here, is not a single man's virtue. It is the whole village's.

And here is something else. The opening in the ice beneath that snow can be astonishingly small. At the surface of the ice, a breathing hole is often no wider than a man's thumb, a tiny round funnel that opens out into a broad chamber below, where the seal's nose rises to the air. To catch an animal of well over a hundred pounds through an opening so small, in the dark, by sound and by touch, is one of the great skills of the Arctic.

These very waters, and these very families, were described a hundred years before our night by a British naval officer called William Edward Parry. In the early 1820s, Parry brought two ships into Foxe Basin, searching for a sea passage around the top of America. His ships spent one winter frozen in at a place called Winter Island, and a second winter at Iglulik itself, close to where our village stands tonight. Parry and his officers came to know the families who camped near the ships. One of them, a woman called Iligliuk, drew a remarkable chart of the coast for him with a pencil on paper, showing bays and islands and straits that nobody on Parry's ships had ever seen.

In the journal he published in 1824, Parry described the winter houses of these families. "In their winter habitations," he wrote, "I have before mentioned that the only materials employed are snow and ice; the latter being made use of for the windows alone." That is the whole architecture, in a single sentence. Walls of snow. And, over the door, a single window made of ice.

That window is a small marvel of its own. Sea ice is no good for it, because salt water freezes cloudy and soft, full of tiny pockets of brine. So a family that can manages to carry a slab of clear freshwater ice, cut from a lake or a frozen pond on the land, and kept on the sled from one house to the next. The slab is shaped with a knife until it fits snugly into a hole in the wall, and the joint is sealed with a little wet snow, which freezes at once. Through it comes the gray noon light, on the days when there is any, and out of it, when the lamp is lit, comes that soft golden glow. You cannot see through it clearly. You were never meant to. It is a window for light, not for looking.

You are standing outside now, at the edge of the village, in the deep blue cold.

The air is so still that you can hear the blood moving in your ears. Your breath leaves your mouth in a thick white cloud, and drifts, and does not fade, and you can feel tiny crystals of frost forming on your eyelashes and in the fur of your hood. The cold is not like any cold you have known. It is dry, and clean, and it seems to press very gently on every part of your face at once. When you move your feet, the snow does not crunch. It squeaks, high and thin, like a cork being turned in a bottle.

Behind you, the three houses sit low on the ice, white on white. You can only tell where they are by their windows. Each house has one, set into the wall above the low entrance, a slab of clear ice about the size of a dinner plate, and through each window the lamplight inside shows as a soft, round, golden glow. Three golden moons, low on the frozen sea. They are the only warm color anywhere in the whole wide world around you.

And then you hear the dogs.

First a single yelp, far out on the ice. Then the whole team, barking and singing, and the hiss of sled runners on hard snow. Out of the gray dusk, a long, low shape comes toward you, a sled with a man walking beside it and a boy riding on top. The dogs run spread out on separate lines, in a fan, the way they are harnessed in this country, with Aupaluk out in front, his crooked ear flopping as he runs. And on the sled, lashed down with a cord, there is a dark, rounded shape.

A seal.

You might picture a hunter today coming home in a truck, with the heater running and a radio playing. Kunnuk comes home on foot, beside his sled, in a silence so deep that the dogs can be heard a mile away.

Historians do not all agree about how to read the old naval journals like Parry's. Some see Parry and his officers as unusually friendly and careful observers for their time, men who learned names and drew faces and wrote down a great deal that would otherwise be lost. Others point out that they saw everything through the eyes of the Royal Navy, misunderstood much of what they were told, and often described the people around them as curiosities rather than as neighbors. Both things can be true of the same book. It is a window, but the window has a frame.

At the village, everything happens quickly now.

Qaunaq is out of the house before anyone can stop her, crawling out through the low tunnel and bursting into the cold with her hood flying. The baby wakes and cries at the sudden noise, and Arnatsiaq jogs her gently in the amauti and laughs. People come out of the other two houses. Kunnuk's cousin claps him on the shoulder, and the old man from the third house bends to look at the seal, and nods, and says something that makes everyone smile.

It is a fat ringed seal, a young male, gray and silver with pale rings across his back. Kunnuk caught it at a hole a long way to the east, after nearly four hours of waiting. Kaujak did not catch anything. He waited at a hole of his own, a little way off, until his toes ached, and nothing came. But it was the down over his father's hole that he saw tremble first, from where he stood, and he will be telling that part of the story for days.

While the women gather round the seal, Kunnuk and Kaujak see to the dogs and the sled. The dogs are unharnessed one by one, each untangled from its own long trace, and they flop down on the snow, panting, their thick coats frosted white around their muzzles. They will not be fed yet. Hunters here usually fed their dogs once a day, in the evening, and they work best a little hungry.

The sled is turned over on its side, so that its runners face the sky. You can see now that they are not bare wood or bone. They are coated with a thick, smooth layer of something dark, which was put on in the autumn as a wet paste of mud and moss and water, and then left to freeze as hard as stone. Over that dark layer, Kaujak now lays a thin skin of ice. He fills his mouth with water from a small skin bag he has kept warm inside his coat, sprays it out in a fine mist over a pad of bearskin, and rubs the wet fur quickly along each runner. The water freezes almost as soon as it touches. In a few moments, the runners shine like glass. On runners like these, a heavily loaded sled glides over the cold, dry snow as if it were greased, and the dogs pull it far more easily. It is one of the cleverest small tricks of the Arctic winter, and every boy learns it early.

The seal is dragged into the house, not through the snow, but carefully, along the floor of the entrance tunnel, and laid on the floor of the snow house in front of the sleeping platform, in the warm light of the lamp.

And then Arnatsiaq does something very old and very quiet. She takes a little fresh water, melted from snow and kept warm beside the lamp, and lets a few drops of it fall into the seal's mouth. Among the families of this coast, a seal that has given itself to a hunter is offered a drink of fresh water, because it has lived all its life in salt water, and is thought to be thirsty. It is a small courtesy, and a respectful one. The seal has been generous. The household is grateful.

Then she takes her ulu, the curved woman's knife with its handle of bone, and begins to cut. She opens the seal along its belly with long, smooth strokes. The steam rises from it in the cold air near the floor, and the smell of fresh blood and salt fills the house. The liver comes out first, dark and glistening. A piece of it goes at once to Kunnuk, who has earned it, and another to Kaujak, and another, after a moment, to Qaunaq, who is hovering at her mother's shoulder. They eat it warm, standing up, in small bites.

Then the sharing begins. Before the household thinks of its own supper, pieces of the seal go out of the house again. A good share of meat and fat goes to the cousin's house, and another to the old couple, who have no young hunter of their own this winter. It is not a gift that anybody will thank Kunnuk for, exactly, and it is not a debt that anybody will count. It is simply what happens with a seal in this country. Tomorrow, or next week, it will be the cousin who comes home with the seal, and the meat will flow the other way, along the same short paths between the same three doors. A good hunter, here, is not a man who keeps a great deal of meat. He is a man who gives a great deal of meat away.

Ivalu watches from her place by the lamp. She takes a strip of the thick white fat from under the skin, cuts it into small squares with her own ulu, and lays one or two of them gently in the bowl of the lamp, behind the wick.

The little row of flames drinks the new fat, and brightens, and steadies.

## Part Three — Around Half Past Three in the Afternoon: A House Cut From One Storm

By half past three, the last gray of the twilight is slipping away below the southern horizon, and the stars have thickened overhead, hard and white and very close. There is still just enough light to work by, if you work quickly.

And Kunnuk means to work quickly. With a whole seal to store, and the dogs' food to keep safe, the household needs a little storehouse beside the main house, a small dome of snow just big enough to crawl into. He and Kaujak will build it now, before the darkness is complete.

What kind of snow makes a good house?

Not just any snow. That is one of the first things a builder in this country learns. Fresh, soft snow is useless. It crumbles and slumps. Snow that has been lying for a long time, packed down in many thin layers by many different winds, is no good either, because the blocks split along the layers like the pages of a wet book. What a builder wants is a drift laid down by a single storm, packed hard and even all the way through by one steady wind. Snow like that can be cut into blocks as firm as cheese, but light enough to lift with one arm.

So before any block is cut, the builder tests the snow. He walks across the drifts with a long, slender probe of caribou antler or bone, and pushes it down, again and again. He is feeling for the moment when the probe slides smoothly, with an even resistance, from top to bottom, with no sudden soft patch and no hard crust partway down. When he finds that, he has found his house.

Franz Boas, in the report he published in 1888, put the rule in a single careful sentence. The snow, he wrote, must be "very fine grained, but not so hard that it cannot be readily cut with the saw or the snow knife." That is exactly what Kunnuk is feeling for, out on the darkening ice, with his probe. Fine grain. Even texture. And not too hard.

The tool he will use is the snow knife. It is a long, flat, slightly curved blade, about as long as your forearm, with a handle at one end. In older times it was carved from caribou antler, bone or walrus ivory. By 1922, many families along this coast have a snow knife of steel, made from a blade traded from the whaling ships or from the new trading posts to the south, and set into a handle of bone. It is held in one hand, like a long-bladed saw, and it slices into the snow with a soft, dry, whispering sound.

Here is the surprising part. A snow house has no frame. There are no poles inside it, no beams, no scaffolding to hold the roof up while it is being built. The builder stands inside the circle of the house the whole time, and builds the walls up around himself. And he does it with a trick that sounds simple and is not. He lays the blocks in a rising spiral.

He begins with a ring of blocks on the snow, each one leaning a little inward. Then he cuts the tops of the first few blocks at a slant, so that the ring becomes a ramp, rising gently from nothing to the height of one block. And from there, every new block is laid on top of the one before, climbing round and round in a single long spiral, each block leaning a little more inward than the last, each one resting on two blocks below it and pressing against the block beside it. Because the spiral is continuous, every block has a neighbor to lean on as soon as it is placed. The walls hold themselves up as they grow.

Near the top, the blocks lean so far inward that they are almost lying flat. The opening grows smaller and smaller, until there is only one small, ragged hole left in the very crown of the dome. For that hole, the builder cuts the last block, the key block, a little larger than the opening. He lifts it up through the hole from the inside, turns it, lowers it into place, and shaves its edges with his knife until it drops snugly home. And the house is closed.

A pair of skilled builders, one cutting and one laying, could build a small snow house like this in about an hour. A large family house took longer, perhaps two or three hours, with the women and children helping to chink the gaps. And the blocks were usually cut from inside the circle of the house itself, so that by the time the walls were finished, the floor had been sunk a foot or more below the surface of the snow.

The family's own house, the big one with the glowing window, was built in just this way nine days ago, when the three households moved out here from their last camp, closer to the shore. That move took a single short day. The sleds were loaded in the gray noon light with everything the families owned: the rolled sleeping skins, the lamps wrapped carefully in fur, the soapstone pots, the drying racks, the harpoons and lines and spare clothing, the bags of frozen meat, and, on top of one load, a sleepy Qaunaq. The dogs pulled. The adults walked. And when they came to a place where the dogs had found a promising spread of breathing holes, and the old man of the third house had tested the drifts with his probe and nodded, they stopped, and began to cut.

By the time the twilight faded that day, all three houses were standing.

The big house is larger than the little storehouse, and more carefully made. Its dome is about twelve feet across inside, and high enough for a grown man to stand upright in the middle. You do not walk straight into it. You come in through a long, low tunnel, built of snow blocks and roofed over, so low that you must stoop or crawl. Partway along the tunnel there is a small side chamber, where the harnesses and spare gear are kept, and where a dog or two might be allowed to curl up on the very coldest nights. Then the tunnel rises, and you climb up through a round hole in the floor of the main room, like a seal coming up through the ice.

Some families along this coast went further still. They built two or three domes side by side and joined them with short passages, so that several households could live as one, each with its own lamp, visiting back and forth without ever going out into the cold. When the Danish archaeologist Therkel Mathiassen of the Fifth Thule Expedition traveled through this country, he measured snow houses of many kinds, from small single domes to great joined houses with four lamps burning at once. The snow house was not one design. It was a whole family of designs, shaped to the size of the household and the length of the stay.

A hundred years before our night, the snow houses of this coast astonished the British sailors who first came to know them. In February of 1822, when Parry's two ships were frozen in at Winter Island, a group of families built a small snow village a short walk from the ships. One of Parry's captains, George Francis Lyon, visited them often, and drew them, and wrote about them in a private journal that he published in 1824. His drawings show the inside of a snow house as a high, round, airy room, with a broad sleeping platform, women seated beside their lamps, and the walls curving up into the shadows. He noticed, too, that some families lined the inside of their snow houses with skins, and that this made them warmer. Lyon was a lively, curious man and a gifted artist, and his journal returns to those houses of snow again and again.

You are standing beside Kunnuk now, inside a ring of snow blocks that comes up to your knees.

It is almost dark. The stars are very bright, and the snow seems to give off a faint light of its own, so that you can still see the shapes of things, gray on gray. A little way off, Kaujak is cutting blocks. You can hear the snow knife hissing through the drift, a long slow cut down one side, then across the bottom, then a quick sideways jerk, and a block comes loose with a soft crack. He lifts it in both arms, a slab of snow about as long as your arm and as thick as your hand is long, and carries it over.

Kunnuk takes it from him without a word. He sets it on the rising spiral, leaning in, and taps it once with the side of his knife. He trims a corner. He taps it again. It settles into place with a faint squeak, and stays. You reach out and touch it. It is cold and dry and solid as wood.

The two of them work in a steady rhythm, without hurry and without stopping. Cut. Carry. Set. Trim. Tap. The walls rise around you. The ring becomes a ramp, and the ramp becomes a spiral, and the spiral begins to lean inward over your head, until you are standing in a little cave of snow, and the stars are only a small round patch of sky above you. Then the patch closes. Kunnuk is lifting the last block through the hole, from the inside, and turning it in his mittened hands, and letting it down.

It is suddenly very quiet. The wind, what there was of it, has gone. You are standing in a perfect small dome of snow, in absolute darkness, and you can hear yourself breathing.

You might think of the cranes and scaffolds and steel girders that hold up a building today. This little house is held up by nothing but the snow itself, and the way it leans.

People who study these houses do not entirely agree about the secret of their shape. Some engineers point out that a well-built snow dome is very close to a particular mathematical curve, the shape of a hanging chain turned upside down, which carries its own weight with almost no strain at all. Others say the builders were not thinking of curves, and did not need to. They were following the spiral, and judging the lean of each block by eye and by touch, and the right shape simply grew out of that. Perhaps both are true. The mathematics was there. The builders knew it in their hands.

Outside, Qaunaq has come to help. She has a short paddle of bone, and she is going around the outside of the little storehouse, scooping up loose snow and packing it into the cracks between the blocks, smoothing it with her mittens. Her father showed her how to do this when she was four. She is very proud that nobody needs to show her any more.

"Not too thick," Kaujak tells her, with all the authority of thirteen.

"I know," says Qaunaq.

Kaujak has earned a little of that authority. Children here learn to build the way they learn almost everything, by watching and then by playing. Small boys and girls build tiny snow houses of their own beside the real ones, with blocks the size of loaves of bread, and crawl into them, and are delighted when they hold. Kaujak built his first proper small dome when he was ten. It stood for one whole afternoon, and then, when his younger cousin climbed on top of it to show how strong it was, it fell in. Everyone laughed, including Kaujak, eventually. His second one stood for a week.

This winter, for the first time, his father has let him cut the blocks for a real house. It is the harder half of the work, in its way. A block that is cut too thin will crack. A block cut too thick is too heavy to lift easily and wastes good snow. A block with a crooked face will not sit flush against its neighbor, and will leave a gap for the wind. Kunnuk never praises him much. But tonight, when the little storehouse was closed, his father put a mittened hand on the back of his neck for a moment, and that was enough.

Kunnuk cuts a low doorway in the side of the new dome with three quick strokes, and crawls out through it, grinning, with frost in his eyebrows. Then the meat goes in. The seal, now in neat pieces, is stacked on the floor of the storehouse, with a block of snow set across the doorway to keep the dogs from thinking about it too much. Aupaluk watches the whole business with his crooked ear cocked, and sighs, and lies down in the snow with his nose under his tail.

The last of the twilight is gone now. It is full night, though it is not yet four o'clock in the afternoon.

Kunnuk stands for a moment and looks around at the three domes and the little new one. Then he looks at his own house, at the round golden glow of its window, set above the door.

Inside it, Ivalu has seen them coming. She has lifted the wick a little with her bone stick, so that the window will shine a fraction brighter, to guide them in.

## Part Four — Around Half Past Four in the Afternoon: Why the Snow Is Warm

By half past four, the sky is fully dark, the stars are blazing, and out on the ice the cold has deepened, as it always does once the twilight is gone. The men are coming in.

And here is a small puzzle to carry through the door with them. Why does a snow house not simply melt, from the inside, with a flame burning in it all day and all night?

Before Kunnuk and Kaujak crawl into the tunnel, they stop outside the entrance and beat their clothes. Each of them has a short, flat paddle of wood or bone, and with it they knock every last crumb of snow from their coats and trousers and boots, briskly, all over, front and back. Snow that is brought inside will melt in the warmth, and wet fur is the enemy of every Arctic traveler. A coat that gets damp will freeze stiff the moment it goes outside again, and lose most of its warmth. So the snow stays outside, where it belongs.

Then they crawl in, down into the low tunnel, along it, and up through the round hole into the house.

The first thing you would notice, coming in behind them, is that the house is not one temperature. It is many. And the way those temperatures are arranged is the whole secret of the snow house.

Begin with the snow itself. A block of good house snow feels solid, and it is strong enough to stand on. But it is mostly made of air. Between its tiny crystals of ice are countless small, still pockets of air, sealed off from one another, unable to move. And still air is one of the best insulators there is. Heat passes through it only very slowly. So a wall of snow blocks, a foot or so thick, works like a thick quilt laid over the whole house. It keeps the warmth of the lamp and the people inside, and it keeps the cold of the night outside, with remarkably little passing between them.

Then there is the shape. A dome has the smallest surface for the space it holds, so there is as little wall as possible for the warmth to escape through. And there are no corners, where cold air could gather and sit.

Then there is the floor. Remember that the tunnel is low, and that you climb up through a hole into the house. This is not an accident. Cold air is heavy. It sinks. In a snow house, the coldest air in the whole building drains down out of the living room and settles in the low entrance tunnel, like water running into a ditch, and stays there. The entrance works as a trap for the cold. Even with the doorway open to the night, the freezing air outside cannot easily climb up into the house, because it would have to flow uphill to get there.

And then there is the sleeping platform. Across the back half of the house, the builders left a broad, raised bench of solid snow, about as high as a table, covered with skins. Warm air rises. So the warmest air in the house is always up at the level of that platform, and above it, where the family sits and works and sleeps. The floor near the entrance hole may be close to freezing. The platform, a few feet higher, can be pleasantly warm.

Then there is the breath of the house. A family of six, sitting around a lamp, breathes out a great deal of warm, damp air, and the lamp itself makes a little more. If all of that stayed inside, the air would grow stale and heavy, and the damp would settle on every skin and every coat. So near the top of the dome, a little to one side, the builder leaves a small hole, about the size of a fist. Warm, moist air rises through it and escapes into the night, as a thin, pale thread of vapor that you can sometimes see standing over a village on a still evening, like smoke. And because warm air is leaving at the top, a little fresh air is drawn gently in at the bottom, through the long tunnel, warming as it comes. On the very coldest nights, the hole can be made smaller with a plug of snow. On a warm, crowded evening, someone pokes it wider with a stick. The house breathes, and the people decide how deeply.

So here is the answer to our first question, the question of how a house made of snow can be warm. It is warm because the snow is mostly still air, and works like a thick quilt. It is warm because its dome shape has the least possible wall. It is warm because the cold air drains away downhill into the tunnel, and the warm air rises to the platform where the people are. And it is warm because inside it there is a lamp, and a family, all giving off heat, which the snow will not let go.

As for the puzzle at the door, the reason the walls do not melt is that the cold outside is so strong. The inside surface of the dome does melt, a very little, when the lamp first warms it. But the cold coming through the snow from outside freezes that thin film of water again, and turns the inner face of the walls into a smooth, hard glaze of ice. That glaze makes the house stronger, not weaker. A snow house that has been lived in for a few days is far tougher than a new one. People said that a well-used house could bear the weight of a man standing on its roof.

Here is something that may surprise you. On a night like this one, the air outside may be fifty degrees below zero, Fahrenheit. Inside a well-built snow house, warmed only by the bodies of the people in it, the air near the platform has been found to sit anywhere from the high teens up to around sixty degrees above zero. Add a lamp and a skin lining, and it can be warmer still. At its best, that is a difference of something like a hundred degrees, held back by a single foot of snow. The readings vary a great deal from house to house, with the snow, the size of the dome, and how many people are inside. But the difference is always large.

The Fifth Thule Expedition measured exactly this. In the report he published in 1928, on the tools and houses and clothing of the families of this coast, Therkel Mathiassen gave readings of the temperature inside snow houses of different kinds, taken on nights of different cold. His figures showed what every family here already knew: that a house with a skin lining, hung on cords a few inches inside the snow walls, and warmed by its lamps, stayed far warmer than a bare dome. The lining does two things. It holds a thin layer of still air between the skins and the snow, which is one more quilt. And it lets the people inside keep the air warmer than freezing, without melting the ceiling over their heads, because the warm air never quite touches the snow.

You are climbing up through the hole in the floor now, behind Kaujak.

The change comes at once. In the tunnel, the air was bitter and still, almost as cold as the night outside. As your head rises through the opening, it is like putting your face into warm water. The warmth is soft and close and smells of seal oil and fur and boiled meat. Your eyes, after the dark, are dazzled. The lamp is a long row of gold to your left, and the whole curving room is lit by it, the gleaming ice-glazed walls, the hanging rack of boots, the brown and white fur of the sleeping skins piled on the platform.

You haul yourself up onto the floor of trodden snow, and stand, and feel the warmth climbing with you. At your feet, the air is cool. At your knees, it is milder. At your face, it is warm. Up on the platform, where Qaunaq is sitting with her legs tucked under her, it is warm enough that she has pushed back the hood of her inner parka and her cheeks are pink.

And you hear it now, the small sounds of the house. The faint, fat sizzle of the lamp. The soft drip, somewhere above you, of a single drop of water falling from the ceiling. The baby gurgling. The creak of skins as Ivalu shifts her weight.

You pull off your own mittens, and your fingers, which were stiff and white outside, begin to tingle and burn as the blood comes back into them. You hold them out toward the lamp, not too close. You can feel its warmth on your palms like the warmth of a cup of tea. Someone shifts over on the platform to make room for you, and you climb up and sit, and the caribou fur beneath you is thick and soft and gives under your weight. You lean back. Behind you, through the skins that hang along the wall at the back of the platform, you can feel the faint chill of the snow wall, a few inches away, but it does not reach you. It is like sitting in a warm tent pitched inside a cold cave. You let out a long breath, and for the first time since the sun went down, you are not cold at all.

You might think of the insulation inside the walls of a modern house, the pink fiberglass or the foam boards. Every one of them works by the same trick as the snow, trapping air so that it cannot move. The families of this coast simply built their whole house out of the trick itself.

Travelers did not all agree about how comfortable snow houses really were. Some wrote of sitting in them with their coats off, sweating, so warm that they could hardly believe there was a blizzard outside. Others complained of dripping ceilings and damp sleeping skins and cold that crept up from the floor. Both kinds of report are honest. A new, unlined house with one small lamp could be chilly. A house that had been lived in for a few days, with a skin lining and a good lamp and a household of six, was another place entirely.

In the house, the men are stripping off their outer coats.

Kunnuk sits on the edge of the platform and pulls his heavy outer parka over his head, fur side out, and hands it to Arnatsiaq, who turns it inside out and hangs it on the rack above the lamp to dry. Then his outer trousers, then his boots, then the soft fur stockings inside them. Kaujak does the same. In a few minutes they are sitting in their inner clothes, and then, because the house is warm, Kunnuk pulls off his inner parka too, and sits bare to the waist on the furs, stretching his shoulders and sighing. Out here, on a winter evening, in a good house, that is a perfectly ordinary thing to do.

Arnatsiaq lifts Ukpik out of the hood of her amauti and lays her on a caribou skin on the platform. The baby kicks her bare legs in the warm air and laughs at the lamp.

Then the story of the hunt is told, properly, for the first time.

Kunnuk tells it briefly. He went east, he says, past the old pressure ridge, where Aupaluk found a hole. He waited. The seal came. That is all. Arnatsiaq smiles at the floor, because that is how he always tells it, as if it were nothing.

Then Kaujak tells it, and it takes much longer. There is the long walk out, and the way Aupaluk stopped and whined, and the other hole, his own, which he was certain was the better one. There is the cold in his toes, and the stars coming out, and how he saw the little thread of down over his father's hole begin to shiver, from twenty paces away, before anyone. There is the sound of the harpoon going in, and the line jerking, and his father's arms straining at the hole. Qaunaq listens with her mouth slightly open. Ivalu listens with her eyes half closed, nodding at the good parts.

"Next time," says Kaujak, "it will be my hole."

"Next time," says his grandmother, "perhaps it will."

Then there is a drip. A cold drop of water lands on the back of Qaunaq's neck, and she squeals. Everyone looks up. Directly above the lamp, a small, shiny bulge has formed in the ceiling, where the snow is softening.

Ivalu does not say anything. She reaches down beside the platform, takes a handful of loose, dry snow from the little heap that is kept there for this very purpose, and reaches up, and presses it firmly against the wet spot. The dry snow drinks up the water. It freezes into a hard little patch. The drip stops.

Then she sits down again, and looks at the lamp, and with the tip of her bone stick she lowers the flame along that end of the wick, just a little, so that it will not soften the ceiling again. The row of small flames shortens, and evens, and burns on.

## Part Five — Around Half Past Five in the Evening: Boots Over the Lamp

By half past five, out over the eastern ice, three bright stars in a short, straight row are lifting clear of the horizon, one after another. They will climb all evening, and cross the southern sky around midnight. We will come back to them later.

Inside, the rack above the lamp is full. There are two outer parkas hanging on it now, and two pairs of fur trousers, and four boots, and four soft fur stockings, all steaming very faintly in the rising warmth. And beneath the rack, the women are at work.

Why does a winter coat in this country have two layers of fur, one with the hair turned in toward the body, and one with the hair turned out toward the cold?

The answer is air, again, as it was with the snow. A winter suit here is made almost entirely of caribou skin. The inner parka is worn with the fur facing inward, soft against the skin. The outer parka is worn over it, with the fur facing outward, to the wind. Between the two layers, and within the deep fur of each, there is a great deal of still, trapped air, and that air holds the warmth of the body the way the snow walls hold the warmth of the lamp. Two thinner layers of fur, with air between them, are far warmer than one thick one.

And caribou hair has a special gift of its own. Each hair is hollow, filled with tiny cells of air, so that even the hair itself is an insulator. That is why caribou skin, rather than seal or bear or fox, was the great winter material of this coast. It is light, and very warm, and it does not let the heat of the body slip away.

But a good suit must not be too warm, either. That sounds strange, on a night of fifty below. Yet the greatest danger for a hunter, out on the ice, is not the cold itself, but sweat. If he works too hard in clothes that are too tight, he sweats, and the sweat soaks into his inner fur, and then, when he stops moving, it freezes. So the parkas here are cut loose, hanging well below the hips, with no tight belt. A man who feels himself growing hot can loosen the neck of his hood, or lift the hem of his parka, and let a little of the warm air out. The clothing breathes, like the house.

Here is something that may surprise you. A single family, to clothe itself through one winter, might need the skins of a great many caribou, perhaps several dozen. And those skins had to be taken at the right time of year, in late summer and early autumn, when the animals' new winter coats have grown in thick but not yet too long, and the hide is still sound. Skins taken in the depth of winter shed their long hair. Skins taken in spring are full of holes from insects. So every autumn, the families of this coast traveled inland after the caribou, and the women spent weeks scraping and stretching and softening the hides, so that the winter's clothes could be sewn before the dark came.

There was another reason for all that autumn sewing. Among many families of this coast, there were old and serious customs about keeping the things of the land and the things of the sea apart. Caribou belonged to the land, and seals to the sea, and the two were not to be mixed carelessly. So in many households, the cutting and sewing of new caribou clothing was finished on the land, in the autumn, before the families moved out onto the sea ice for the winter seal hunt. Once they were living on the ice, among the seals, the women mended and patched, and worked with sealskin, but the great sewing of caribou was over until the next year. Customs like this varied from place to place, and from family to family. But they show how carefully the year was ordered, and how much thought went into every stitch.

The boots are something else again. For the soles, the women use the tough skin of the bearded seal, which is thick and strong and keeps out water. For the uppers they use caribou skin or the skin of the ringed seal. And the seams of a good boot are sewn so cleverly that they are waterproof. The needle does not pass right through the skin. It goes only halfway in and comes out again on the same side, so that no hole goes all the way through. The thread is caribou sinew, which swells a little when it gets wet, and closes the seam even tighter.

Franz Boas, writing in 1888, described the work of the woman of a household plainly and with respect. "It falls to her share," he wrote, "to make the inner outfit of the hut, to smooth the platforms, line the snow house." Elsewhere in his report he shows the women at their sewing, their cooking and their lamps. In the snow-house country of his time, the house was built by the men, but everything that made it a home, from the lining on its walls to the clothes on every back, came from the women's hands.

You are sitting beside Ivalu now, close to the lamp, where the light is best.

She has a piece of soft, pale sealskin across her knees, the upper of a new boot for Kaujak, whose feet have grown again since the autumn. Beside her on the platform lies a small, worn case of skin, and in it are her treasures. A row of needles, two of them steel, bought from a trader for a fox skin, and one of them very old, made of bone, which belonged to her own mother. A thimble, made not of metal but of a little piece of tough sealskin, which she slips over her finger. And a bundle of sinew thread, long, pale fibers stripped from the back of a caribou, dried, and split into strands as fine as hair.

She takes one of those strands now and puts it in her mouth. You watch her wet it, and roll it quickly between her palm and her knee, twisting it into a fine, strong thread. Then she threads the steel needle, holding it up to the light of the lamp, squinting a little. She is sixty-six. Her eyes are not what they were. But her hands do not hesitate at all.

She begins to sew. The stitches are tiny, close together, perfectly even, and she makes them very fast. Push, pull, draw tight. Push, pull, draw tight. The needle flashes in the lamplight. You can hear the faint, dry whisper of the sinew drawing through the skin.

You reach out, and she lets you hold the edge of the seam. On the outside, you can barely see the stitches at all. It is like holding the edge of a single piece of skin.

You try to imagine wearing all of this. Ivalu seems to guess what you are thinking, because she nods toward Kaujak's inner parka, hanging over the edge of the platform, and lets you pick it up. It is astonishingly light. You had expected a fur coat to be heavy, like a blanket soaked in water, but this weighs hardly anything at all. You slip your arm into one sleeve. The fur inside is short and dense and very soft, and as soon as it closes around your arm, you feel your own warmth coming back to you, as if the coat had been waiting for it. You run your other hand down the outside. The skin is supple, scraped thin and chewed soft, with a faint smell of smoke and oil. It moves with you like cloth.

Then she holds up one of Kunnuk's boots for you to see. You turn it over in your hands. The sole curves up neatly around the toe in a row of tiny, even pleats, each one pressed in by a woman's teeth. You look for the seam where the sole meets the upper, and at first you cannot find it at all.

You might think of modern winter clothing, the base layers and the fleece and the shell, and the advice to dress in layers and avoid sweating. It is the same advice. The women of this coast were giving it, in their own way, long before anyone wove a single thread of fleece.

Historians do not entirely agree about how to describe the division of work in these households. Some older writers made it sound strict and almost rigid, with men's work and women's work kept firmly apart. Others, and many Inuit elders speaking in later years, describe it rather as a partnership, in which each person's skill depended completely on the other's. A hunter without a good seamstress could not hunt in winter at all. His clothes would fail him. And a seamstress without a hunter would have no skins to sew. Neither kind of work came first.

On the other side of the lamp, Arnatsiaq is working on a boot.

It is one of Kunnuk's, and the sole has grown stiff from the cold. She holds the edge of the sole in her teeth and chews it, slowly and steadily, working her way along the curve, a little at a time. It looks odd, to a stranger. But chewing is the surest way to soften tough sealskin, and to crimp it into the neat little pleats that let a sole curve up around the toe and heel. Women here did it for hours, and their teeth showed it.

Arnatsiaq's own coat is the most remarkable garment in the house. The amauti, the woman's parka, has a hood so large that a baby can ride inside it, against the mother's bare back, held in place by a sash tied around her waist beneath the pouch. The shoulders are cut very wide, wide enough that a mother can slide her baby round from her back to her front, inside the coat, to nurse it, without ever taking it out into the cold air. On the trail, in a wind of fifty below, Ukpik has spent whole days in there, warm and fed and asleep, while the world outside froze. The amauti is a cradle, a blanket and a coat, all in one, and each woman cuts and sews her own.

Qaunaq has been given a small piece of scrap skin and a needle of her own. She is sewing a boot for a doll, a tiny boot no longer than her thumb, and her stitches are large and wandering and not at all waterproof. Ivalu leans over to look, and says nothing about the stitches. She only takes the little boot, turns it over, and shows Qaunaq how to hold the needle so that it goes only halfway through the skin. Then she hands it back.

Qaunaq tries again. The next stitch is a little smaller.

A little later, Qaunaq holds up the doll's boot. It is finished. It is lopsided, and one side is clearly bigger than the other, and a little tail of sinew hangs from the heel. But the last few stitches, the ones along the toe, are small and close and nearly even.

Ivalu takes it and turns it over in the lamplight, very seriously, as if it were a real boot for a real hunter. Then she tells a story. When she herself was about Qaunaq's age, she says, a long time ago, on a different coast, her mother gave her a piece of scrap skin just like this, and she made a boot for her own doll. It was so badly sewn that when she put it on the doll's foot, the whole sole fell off. Her mother laughed so much that she had to put down her own sewing. Then her mother picked up the sole, and sewed it back on for her, with stitches so tiny you could not see them. Ivalu still remembers those stitches. She has been trying to make them that small ever since.

Qaunaq looks at her grandmother's hands, moving so quickly over Kaujak's new boot, and then at her own little boot, and decides it is not so bad after all.

Kaujak, meanwhile, has taken his fur stockings down from the rack and is turning them inside out, to dry the inside. He holds them out to the warmth of the lamp, one in each hand, the way he has seen his father do, and wrinkles his nose at the smell, and grins at his sister.

Tomorrow, out on the ice, those dry stockings will be warm on his feet, and he will not think about them at all. That is the way of good clothing here. It is noticed only when it fails.

Above them all, on the rack, the boots and coats and trousers hang in the rising warmth, slowly giving up their damp. By morning they will be dry and soft and ready for the ice again. That is one more thing the lamp does, quietly, all night long. It does not only light the house and warm it. It dries every stitch of clothing that keeps this family alive.

Ivalu glances up from her sewing, checks the row of little flames, and nudges the wick once, so that the warmth rising to the rack is even all along its length.

## Part Six — Around Half Past Six in the Evening: Frozen Fish and Warm Liver

By half past six, the three stars in a row have climbed a little higher over the eastern ice, and in the house the work of the afternoon is giving way, slowly, to supper.

Over the lamp, a deep stone pot has been hanging since the seal came in, full of meat and water, and it is not nearly ready yet. It will not be ready for a long while. We will come back to that pot, and to the reasons it is so slow, a little later tonight. But nobody in this house is going to sit and wait for it with an empty stomach.

How do people stay healthy, all through a winter like this, without a single fruit or vegetable?

The answer surprised outsiders for a very long time, and it is lying right there on the floor of the house. It is the seal. The families of this coast lived, in winter, almost entirely on meat and fat, from seals, walrus, fish and, in their season, caribou. And they stayed healthy on it, year after year, because of how that meat was eaten. Much of it was eaten raw, or frozen, or only lightly cooked. Raw liver, raw fat, raw skin and fresh frozen fish all contain small amounts of vitamin C, the very vitamin whose lack had killed so many sailors on long voyages. Cooking destroys a good deal of it. Eating raw keeps it.

So the liver that Kunnuk and his children ate warm, standing up, a few hours ago, was not only a treat. It was medicine.

And the fat was fuel. In this cold, a hunter who stands for hours at a breathing hole burns a great deal of energy just keeping warm. Seal fat, eaten in small pieces with the meat, gives him that energy, slowly and steadily, through the long dark day. The white blubber of the seal is food and lamp oil at the same time, and the same seal that feeds the family also feeds the flame.

Not all of the winter's food is fresh. Much of it was put away months ago. In the autumn, when the walrus hunting was good, the families of this coast cut up some of the meat and fat and sealed it inside bundles of walrus skin, and buried the bundles under heaps of stones and gravel along the shore. There, through the last mild weeks of autumn, the meat slowly aged and ripened before the frost locked it in. People here call it igunaq. It has a strong smell and a strong, rich taste, something like a very old cheese, and many people from outside could not bear it. But the families of this coast prized it, as people elsewhere prize aged cheese or cured ham, and an elder might speak of a good igunaq the way another grandmother might speak of a good wine. Those stone caches along the shore were the household's savings, put by for the hungry weeks of late winter, when the seals grow scarce and the storms keep the hunters at home.

Here is something that may surprise you. Six years after our night, in 1928, the Arctic explorer Vilhjalmur Stefansson, who had lived for years among Inuit families farther to the west, agreed to an experiment in New York City. To prove that a diet of meat and fat alone could keep a person healthy, he and a companion ate nothing else for a whole year, under the eye of doctors at Bellevue Hospital. They came through it in good health. The doctors were astonished. The families of this coast would not have been astonished at all. They had known it for generations.

So supper, tonight, begins cold.

Arnatsiaq brings in, from the little storehouse outside, a whole frozen fish, an Arctic char caught in the autumn when the families were at the river mouths, and laid down then in the cold. It is as hard as wood. She sets it on a flat board on the floor and cuts it into thin slices with her ulu, the curved blade rocking back and forth, and the slices fall away pink and glittering with frost. She cuts thin strips of frozen seal meat from an older seal, too, and small cubes of white fat.

The frozen fish is eaten just as it is. It melts on the tongue.

Meals here do not keep to fixed hours, the way they do in a city. People eat when they are hungry, and when there is food, and a hunter out on the ice may eat nothing at all from morning until he comes home. But the evening, after the hunters return, is the time when the whole family is together, and when the pot is put on, and when anyone who comes to the door is fed. A visitor who crawled in through the tunnel at suppertime and was not offered meat would have been very surprised. In a good house, there was always something on the board.

One of the men who wrote down what the families of this coast thought about their food, and their lives, was Knud Rasmussen. In the early part of 1922, during the Fifth Thule Expedition, he spent many evenings in a snow house with an older man named Aua, a respected spiritual healer of the Iglulik people, and his family. Rasmussen asked him, again and again, why his people kept so many careful rules about hunting and eating, about seals and caribou and the sea. In the end, Aua tried to explain, in a long answer that Rasmussen wrote down and later published in English. Part of it began, "We fear the weather spirit of earth, that we must fight against to wrest our food from land and sea."

It is an honest sentence. Every meal on this coast, in every winter, was won from a hard and unpredictable world, from the ice and the wind and the cold. The rules, as Aua saw it, were a way of living carefully in that world, of showing respect to the animals and the weather on which every life depended.

You are sitting on the platform now, with a slice of frozen char in your fingers.

It is so cold that it numbs the tips of your fingers, and you hold it as you would hold a piece of ice. You put it in your mouth. For a moment, there is no taste at all, only cold. Then it begins to soften, and the taste comes slowly, mild and clean and a little sweet, like the sea on a still morning. It melts away on your tongue, cool and smooth, and you reach for another piece.

Around you, the family eats. Nobody uses a plate. The meat lies on its board in the middle of the floor, and each person leans forward and takes a piece. Kunnuk holds a strip of seal meat in his teeth, and with a small knife in his other hand, he cuts it off, quick and neat, just in front of his lips, with the blade moving away from his face. It looks frightening, the first time you see it. Nobody here gives it a second thought. They have eaten this way since they were small.

You try a cube of the white seal fat. It is softer than you expected, and rich, and faintly salty, a little like cold butter with the taste of the sea. Ivalu, watching you, smiles and nods at it, as if to say, eat more, it is good for you in this cold. You eat one more cube. It is more than enough.

And all the while, the lamp burns, and the pot hangs above it, and the smell of the slowly warming meat drifts through the house.

You might think of a supermarket in winter, with its long aisles of oranges and lettuces and vitamin pills, carried from the far side of the world. Here, every vitamin the family needs is in the animals of the sea beneath them.

Historians and translators do not all agree about how to read Aua's words. Some take them as a clear window onto the beliefs of the Iglulik people before outside religions came, set down by a listener who spoke their language well. Others point out that by the time Rasmussen met him, Aua and his family were already hearing of Christianity from the south, and that every translation, from his own language into Danish and then into English, changes something. Not long afterwards, Aua himself would choose to become a Christian. Both views take him seriously. He was one thoughtful man, speaking in a time of change.

When the board is nearly bare, Kaujak pulls his dried boots and outer coat back on, takes an armful of frozen meat from the storehouse, and crawls out through the tunnel to feed the dogs. You go with him.

Outside, the cold hits your face like a door slammed shut. The stars are thick and bright, and the snow is faintly gray beneath them. The dogs are curled in the snow around the house, each one a tight round ball with its nose tucked under its tail, and the frost has settled on their fur so that they look like snow-covered stones. As soon as Kaujak appears, they leap up, every one of them, howling and jostling, and Aupaluk shoulders his way to the front. Kaujak throws each dog its share, one at a time, calling them by name, making sure the smallest ones get theirs before the big ones can steal it. In a minute, it is over. The dogs gulp their meat, frozen and all, and then lick the snow, and turn around three times, and curl up again into their tight gray balls.

They will sleep out here all night, in fifty degrees below zero, warm in their thick coats. They are the village's guards, as well as its engine. If anything comes near in the dark, a wandering fox, or one of the great white bears of the sea ice, the dogs will know it long before any person, and the whole team will wake the village with their barking. That has not happened on this stretch of ice all winter. Tonight, the dogs are only full, and drowsy, and content.

You crawl back in through the tunnel behind Kaujak, and up through the hole into the warmth, and it feels like coming home.

In the house, Kaujak is telling his sister about the seal again.

Qaunaq, by now, can tell it herself, and she does, in a loud voice, with her mouth full, until her mother puts a finger to her own lips and nods toward the baby. Ukpik has fallen asleep, lying on her back on the caribou fur, with her arms flung up above her head and a tiny piece of chewed fish still in one fist. Arnatsiaq gently takes the fish away and covers her with a small skin.

Kunnuk has eaten a great deal, quietly, as hunters here do after a long day on the ice. Now he leans back against the skins at the back of the platform and closes his eyes for a moment. His face, in the lamplight, is deep brown from the wind and the reflected glare of the snow, with fine lines at the corners of his eyes.

Ivalu, chewing slowly, is remembering something. When she was a young woman, newly married, there was a late winter on another stretch of this coast when the storms came one after another for weeks, and the hunters could hardly go out at all. The families in her village opened their stone caches one by one, and shared what was in them, every household with every other, until the weather broke and the seals came back to the holes. Nobody in that village went without, she says, because nobody kept anything back. She tells it quietly, not as a sad story, but as a plain one, the way you might tell a child why you always keep a little money put aside. Kaujak and Qaunaq have heard it before. They listen anyway. It is the kind of story that explains why the meat of today's seal is already lying in three different houses.

Arnatsiaq, for her part, eats quickly and neatly, between other tasks, the way mothers everywhere eat. A slice of fish while she checks the baby. A strip of meat while she turns a boot on the rack. A cube of fat while she looks into the pot and shakes her head, not yet. She has been up since long before the noon twilight, and she will be among the last to sleep, and she does not seem to mind either.

Ivalu eats last, and least. She takes a few slices of fish, and one piece of the fresh seal fat, which she chews slowly. Then, before anything else, she takes another strip of fat from the board, cuts it into small squares, and lays them carefully in the bowl of the lamp, one by one, behind the wick.

She does this the way another grandmother might feed a cat. Gently, without fuss, and without being asked. It is the first thing she does after every meal, and the last thing she does before every sleep.

"The lamp eats first," she says, to nobody in particular, and the little row of flames brightens as if it had heard her.

## Part Seven — Around Half Past Seven in the Evening: The Pot That Takes All Evening

By half past seven, the three stars in a row have swung up toward the southeast, and the house has grown drowsy and warm with food. But the stone pot over the lamp has still not come to the boil.

Why does it take so long to cook a pot of meat over a lamp?

Look at the lamp, and you can see why. Its flame is not one strong flame, like a fire of wood, roaring up under a kettle. It is a long, low row of small, gentle flames, spread out along the straight edge of a stone half moon. Each little flame gives only a small amount of heat. Together they give a steady, even warmth, enough to light and heat a house, but not a fierce one. And the pot that hangs above them is made of stone, the same soft gray stone as the lamp, carved out into a deep, rectangular basin. Stone is heavy, and it takes a long time to warm right through. Once it is hot, it holds its heat beautifully. But getting it hot is slow.

So a pot of meat over a lamp is not boiled, the way you might boil water in a kitchen. It is warmed, and then simmered, gently, for a long time. A pot of snow, for drinking water, could take an hour or more to become water, and longer to become hot. A good stew of seal meat took most of the evening. Nobody minded. The pot was put on when the hunters came home, and the house simply went about its business underneath it, until it was ready.

But why cook over a lamp at all? Why not build a fire?

Because there is nothing to burn. That is the plain, hard fact behind everything in this house. The nearest standing trees are many hundreds of miles to the south, beyond the edge of the great northern forest. Here on the coast of Foxe Basin, there are none. On the land, in summer, there are low mats of Arctic heather and willow no higher than your ankle, and families camped inland would gather the heather and dry it, and burn it in small open fires to cook their meat. But in winter, on the sea ice, the heather is buried under feet of snow, and far away.

Driftwood, washed up on the shore, was precious, and rare along this coast. When a family found a piece, they did not burn it. They saved it for the things that could be made of nothing else: the crosspieces of a sled, the shaft of a harpoon, the frame of a drying rack, a handle for a knife. Wood was for tools. It was much too valuable to put in a fire.

And so, on the winter ice, there is only one fuel. It is the fat of the seal.

Here is something that may surprise you. The soft gray stone of the lamp and the pot is called soapstone, and it cannot be found just anywhere. It comes only from certain places, outcrops of rock along certain coasts, where people had quarried it for generations. A good piece, big enough for a lamp or a pot, might be carried hundreds of miles from where it was cut, passed along from family to family in trade, or kept for years before it was carved. A finished lamp was treasured. It might be used by one woman for her whole life and then passed to her daughter, or her granddaughter. In many families, a young woman received a lamp of her own when she married and set up her own household. For a woman on this coast, her lamp was a little like what a hearth was to a woman in other lands, the center of her home and of her work.

Ivalu's lamp is older than Kunnuk. It belonged to her mother. On one side, near the curve, there is a small crack, mended long ago with a strip of sinew drilled through two neat holes in the stone. You can only see it when the lamp is cold.

Franz Boas, in his report of 1888, described this whole arrangement carefully, the lamp on its stand, the pot hanging over it, the rack above. And he noticed something that most visitors missed. The woman, he wrote, sits in front of a lamp, "beneath which is placed a vessel which receives the oil." A lamp, when it is full and warm, can sometimes overflow a little, or drip from its edge as the fat melts. So under the lamp sits a second, smaller dish, to catch every precious drop. Nothing is wasted. Not one spoonful of oil.

That is one reason the lamp is so rarely allowed to go out. Lighting it from nothing is slow work. Before traders brought boxes of matches to this coast, a flame was made by striking a lump of iron pyrite against a stone, so that the sparks fell into a little nest of dry tinder, the fluff of willow catkins or dried moss, which was then breathed on very gently until it glowed. Or it was made with a bow drill, a stick spun fast against a block of wood until the dust at its tip began to smoke. Some families in 1922 have a few matches, carefully kept dry, bought from a trading post. But matches run out, and the old way takes patience. It is much easier to keep a flame than to make one. So the lamp is fed and trimmed and watched, and when the family moves to a new house, the first thing a woman does, once the dome is closed, is to set up her lamp and light it. Only then is the house a home.

The fat itself must be prepared, too. A lump of raw seal blubber laid straight on the lamp gives its oil only slowly. So the women cut the blubber into small pieces and often beat it with a little stick or a pounder of bone, breaking it up so that the oil runs out more freely. Some of it is laid in the bowl of the lamp, and some on the sloping back of the lamp, above the pool, where the warmth of the flames melts it gently and lets the oil trickle down to the wick. A woman who knows her lamp can keep it burning at exactly the height she wants for hours, just by the way she places the fat.

You are sitting beside the lamp now, watching the pot.

It hangs on four cords of braided sinew, tied to the corners of the wooden rack above, so that its flat bottom sits just above the tips of the flames. You can see a faint ripple of heat rising around it. The stone is darker where the flames have touched it, black with soot along its bottom, smooth and gray above. Every so often, a small bubble rises inside the pot and breaks at the surface, and a puff of steam lifts away toward the dome. The smell is rich and warm, of seal meat and fat and salt.

Beside the pot, at the side of the rack, hangs a second, smaller thing, a bag made of skin, bulging with lumps of white. It is ice. Not snow, which is mostly air and takes a great many armfuls to make a little water, but chunks of old, hard ice, chopped from a piece of ice that has stood through more than one summer. Old sea ice loses its salt as it ages, and becomes good to drink. The lumps in the bag are melting slowly in the warmth rising from the lamp, and water drips from the bottom of the bag into a little skin bucket beneath. That is the household's drinking water. It is cold, and clean, and it tastes of nothing at all. On this coast, nobody would think of wasting it.

You hold your hand out, flat, above the row of flames, high up, near the rack. The warmth is gentle and steady, like sunlight on a spring day. You lower your hand slowly. It grows warmer, and warmer, until, a few inches above the flames, you take it quickly away.

Archaeologists do not entirely agree about the history of lamps like this one. Stone lamps burning animal fat have been found in Arctic sites that are many centuries old, among the people the Inuit call the Tuniit, who lived in this country before the ancestors of the Inuit arrived. Some scholars think the newcomers learned the soapstone lamp from them, or from their quarries. Others think the two peoples each developed their lamps in their own ways, from much older traditions, and that the resemblance is simply that both had the same problem to solve. The lamp itself does not say.

In the house, at last, the pot is ready.

Arnatsiaq tests it first, lifting a piece of meat out with a long, curved ladle of horn, and pressing it with her fingers. Then she nods. Ivalu lifts the pot carefully off its cords, using a pad of fur to protect her hands, and sets it down on a flat stone on the floor. And now there is boiled meat, hot and tender, falling off the bone, and a broth of seal fat and blood and meat juices, dark and steaming, which everyone drinks in turn from a ladle and from a small cup of horn.

The broth is the best part. Kunnuk drinks it slowly, holding the cup in both hands, letting the steam warm his face. Qaunaq drinks so fast that she burns her tongue, and then drinks more. Even Ukpik, wakened by the smell, is given a little on the tip of her mother's finger, and smacks her lips.

While they eat, Qaunaq is sent to refill the ice bag. This is her job, and she takes it seriously. She crawls down into the tunnel, to the place near the entrance where a pile of chopped ice blocks is kept, carried here on the sled from a stranded piece of old ice a little way along the shore. She chooses the clearest lumps, the ones with a faint blue color, because her grandmother has told her that the blue ice is the oldest and the sweetest. She brings them back in her arms, one at a time, and drops them into the bag, and wipes her cold hands on her trousers, and climbs back up onto the platform beside her brother.

Kaujak has taken the baby, so that Arnatsiaq can eat. He holds Ukpik on his knee, a little awkwardly, the way boys of thirteen hold babies everywhere, and lets her pull at the fringe on the front of his inner parka. She is fascinated by the steam rising from the cup in his other hand. He blows on it for her, and she laughs, and grabs at the steam, and is astonished when her hand closes on nothing.

Then the cup comes round to you. You take it in both hands, the way Kunnuk did. It is a small cup carved from horn, smooth and warm, worn thin at the rim by many years of lips. The broth inside is dark and cloudy, with little golden circles of fat floating on its surface. You lift it to your face, and the steam is warm on your nose and your eyelids. You sip. It is salty and rich, and it tastes deeply of the sea and of meat, and you feel its warmth go all the way down, and then spread outward through your chest and into your arms, the way warmth spreads from a stove into a cold room. You had not known how cold you still were, deep inside, until now. You drink again, slowly, and pass the cup on to Ivalu, who takes it and nods at you, pleased.

So here is the answer to our second question, the question of how you cook a supper, and melt your drinking water, with no wood for hundreds and hundreds of miles. You cook with the seal. You take the white fat from under its skin and lay it in a shallow lamp of soapstone, behind a wick of moss and Arctic cotton, and you light it, and you tend it all day and all night. Above it, you hang a pot of the same stone, and you let it simmer slowly, for as long as it needs. Beside it, you hang a bag of old ice, and let it drip. Much of your food, you do not cook at all, because raw and frozen meat and fish keep you healthy. The animal that feeds you also gives you the fire to cook it on.

And when the meal is finished, and the broth is gone, Ivalu wipes out the pot with a handful of moss, and fills it again with lumps of ice, and hangs it back on its cords, so that there will be warm water in the morning. It is the last of her kitchen work for the day.

Then she looks at the lamp. The fat she laid in it before supper has nearly melted away. She cuts a few more pieces, and lays them in, and nudges the wick with her bone stick, and the long row of flames settles down to its steady evening burn.

## Part Eight — Around Half Past Eight in the Evening: A Drum in the Snow House

By half past eight, the three stars in a row are well up in the southeast, bright above the ice, and from the tunnel there comes a scraping and a stamping and the sound of voices. The neighbors are coming to visit.

They come in one after another, up through the hole in the floor, beating the last of the snow from their shoulders, laughing, blinking at the light. First Kunnuk's cousin, a tall, thin man with a wide smile, and his wife, carrying the youngest of their three small sons, and then the other two boys, scrambling up after them. Then, more slowly, the old couple from the third house, the old man helped up through the hole by his grown daughter, who comes in last. There are fourteen people in the house now, and three dogs' worth of noise. The platform is full, and the floor in front of it, and the warmth of all those bodies rises at once to the dome.

Ivalu reaches up with a long stick and pokes the vent hole in the roof a little wider. A thin thread of steam begins to rise through it into the night.

In a larger village, on an evening like this, the families might have gathered in a special house, built just for the purpose, a great dome of snow much bigger than any family's home, sometimes large enough to hold everyone in the camp at once, for singing and dancing and games. Here, with only three households, the biggest house will do. And this is the biggest house.

Why does every person here have songs of their own?

Because, among the families of this coast, a song was not something you learned from a book or bought from a stranger. It was something a person made. A hunter might make a song about a particular day on the ice, a seal that came or did not come, a journey through a storm. A woman might make a song about her children, or about a place she had loved when she was young, or about a sorrow she could not otherwise speak. Once made, the song belonged to the person who had made it. Others could sing it, with permission, and some old songs were passed down in families for generations. But each song had an owner, and everyone knew whose it was.

So when the drum comes out tonight, each person who sings will sing their own songs, and everyone listening will know where each one came from.

The drum is large and round and flat, like a great tambourine without the bells. Its frame is a hoop of bent wood, and stretched across it, very tight, is a thin membrane of skin, the skin of a caribou or the lining of a seal. It has a short handle on one side. The drummer holds it up by the handle, and does not strike the skin at all. He strikes the rim of the frame, from beneath, with a short, thick stick, first on one side and then on the other, tilting the drum back and forth as he does, so that the whole skin booms and shivers. The sound is deep and round, and it fills the snow house like water filling a bowl.

Here is something that may surprise you. In the snow-house country of this time, songs could do far more than entertain. When two men had a quarrel that could not be settled, it was sometimes settled by a song contest, in front of the whole village. Each man would make songs about the other, clever and mocking and funny, and sing them in turn, with the drum, while the listeners laughed. Whoever's songs won the most laughter and the most approval was felt to have won the quarrel. It was a way of letting out anger without anyone getting hurt, and it worked, often enough, to keep the peace through long winters in small houses.

Knud Rasmussen, during the Fifth Thule Expedition, wrote down a great many songs from the people of this coast, in their own words, and translated them. One of the men he met, a storyteller of the Iglulik people named Ivaluardjuk, sang him an old song about the hardships of the year, and it has become one of the most often quoted of all the songs he collected. It speaks of the two great miseries of the Arctic year, which Rasmussen gave in English as "cold and mosquitoes." One belongs to winter, and the other to summer, and the singer notes, with a kind of dry humor, that at least they never come together.

And in 1923, farther to the west, Rasmussen met a hunter and poet named Orpingalik, who told him that he could not say how many songs he had. He only knew that he had many, and that his whole being was song, and that he sang as he drew breath. Songs, Orpingalik said, come to a person when he is moved by a great feeling, a joy or a sorrow too great for ordinary words, and then the words simply come up, all by themselves.

Translators do not all agree about these songs as we have them in English. Some admire Rasmussen's versions enormously, and say that he caught the spirit of the singers better than anyone else could have, because he spoke their language from childhood. Others point out that his English songs are free and poetic, and that the shape of the original songs, their repetitions and rhythms and the chorus of sounds that the listeners sang back, is mostly lost in them. Both are fair. We have the songs, but only through a window, and some of the light is caught in the glass.

You are sitting at the very back of the platform now, against the skins, with a small boy you do not know leaning sleepily against your arm.

The house is full of people and warmth and the smell of fur. The lamp's long row of flames is the only light, and the faces in front of you are lit gold from below, the shadows deep behind them. On the floor, in the middle of the room, the old man from the third house is standing, with the drum.

He does not begin at once. He stands for a while, with the drum held up, swaying a little on his feet, looking at nothing, and the room falls slowly quiet. Then he strikes the rim. Once. The sound booms around the dome. Once more. And then he begins to drum steadily, turning the drum back and forth, slow at first and then faster, his knees bending, his body turning with the drum, his eyes half closed.

Then the singing begins. It is not his voice alone. The women along the platform are singing, too, a rising and falling chorus of sounds, steady as the drum, over and over, and the old man's voice rises above it, telling his song. You cannot understand the words. But you can hear that it is a story, and that the story is a happy one, because every so often everyone in the room laughs at once, and the old man grins, and drums harder.

You can feel the drum in your chest. You can feel it in the platform under you. The little row of flames on the lamp trembles with every beat.

The song ends. Everyone shouts and laughs and claps the old man on the back as he sits down, breathing hard, wiping his face. And the drum goes to Kunnuk's cousin, who stands up, very solemnly, and then sings a song about Kunnuk's seal, in which the seal is enormous and Kunnuk is very small and very cold, and everyone laughs until they can hardly breathe, and Kunnuk laughs most of all.

Then the drum goes round. Kunnuk sings a short song about a journey he made as a young man, along this same coast, in a spring of bright sun. His cousin's wife sings a song for her sons. The children are pulled up to dance, and stamp and turn in the little space on the floor, bumping into each other and shrieking, while the grown-ups sing. Qaunaq dances with the cousin's middle boy, very seriously, holding his hands.

Then the cousin's wife catches your eye, and laughs, and beckons. You shake your head. She beckons again, and the whole platform turns to look at you, smiling, and there is nothing for it. You climb down onto the floor. Someone puts the drum in your hands. It is lighter than you expected, and the skin is drum-tight and faintly warm from the lamp. You hold it up by its handle, the way the old man did, and you tap the rim with the stick, very timidly. It makes a small, flat sound, and everyone laughs, kindly. The old man reaches over, and turns your wrist a little, and shows you how to tilt the drum as you strike, so that the edge comes down to meet the stick. You try again. This time the drum booms, deep and round, and the sound goes up into the dome and comes back down over you all, and the women along the platform cheer and begin to sing. You drum, badly, for a few long beats, and your face is hot, and you are grinning. Then you hand the drum back, and climb up onto the platform again, and the small boy settles back against your arm as if you had never left.

And then, at last, someone says Ivalu's name.

She does not stand. She sits where she is, by the lamp, with her sewing in her lap. Someone passes her the drum, and she holds it a moment, and then hands it to Kunnuk instead, and nods to him to drum for her. And she sings.

Her voice is thin and old and very steady. The song is one she made long ago, when she was a young woman, about a summer camp beside a river on another coast, where the char were running, and the sun went round and round in the sky all night, and her husband, who was young too, came back from the hunt with his kayak full. It is not a sad song. It is a song about a good day. But the room is quiet while she sings it, and when it is over, nobody laughs. They only nod, and murmur, and the old man from the third house says something softly to her that makes her smile.

Kunnuk, still holding the drum, looks at his mother for a moment. The young man in the song, coming home with his kayak full, was his father. He died when Kunnuk was about Kaujak's age, and Kunnuk remembers him mostly through songs like this one, and through the way his mother still sets out a second, small piece of the best fat whenever she tells stories of that river. He does not say anything. He only lays the drum down very gently beside her, on the furs, the way you might lay down something that belongs to someone else.

The visitors stay a long while after the singing. There is meat for everyone, from the board, and the last of the broth, warmed again. Before the visitors leave, the young men play a game or two, in the small space on the floor. The cousin and Kaujak sit facing each other, feet against feet, and each hooks one finger through the other's, and pulls, until one of them is dragged forward off his seat. Kaujak loses, and demands a second try, and loses again, and laughs. Then the cousin hangs a small piece of bone from the ceiling on a thread, a little higher than his own head, and tries to kick it with one foot, jumping from the floor and landing again on the same foot. Games of strength and balance like these filled many long winter evenings in the snow houses of this coast. They needed no light but the lamp, and no space but a floor, and they kept the young men strong and supple through the months when the hunting was slow. Everyone has a turn. Even the old man from the third house tries the kick, once, to great applause, and comes nowhere near the bone, and sits down again very pleased with himself.

The children fall asleep in heaps on the platform, among the furs, the small cousins with Qaunaq, all tangled together like puppies. The grown-ups talk quietly about the ice, and the weather, and where the seals might be found tomorrow, and about the Danish travelers to the south, and the trading post, and the price of a rifle in fox skins.

At last, the neighbors gather up their sleeping children, and pull on their coats, and go out, one by one, down through the hole and along the tunnel and out into the cold. The house is quieter, and a little cooler, at once.

Ivalu pushes a plug of snow back into the vent hole, to make it smaller again. Then she leans forward and looks at the lamp. In all that singing and dancing, the flames have burned a little unevenly, taller in one place and shorter in another. She takes her bone stick, and patiently, without hurry, she trims the wick from one end to the other, until the whole long row is even again.

## Part Nine — Around Ten O'Clock at Night: A Story About the Sun

By ten o'clock, the three stars in a row have climbed high into the southern sky, and the village is still. The neighbors have gone home to their own lamps. In the big house, the six are alone again, and the warmth has settled, and nobody is in a hurry to do anything at all.

Qaunaq is lying on her front on the platform, with her chin in her hands, looking at a loop of sinew string that she has found among the furs. She lifts it, and turns it over, and then, a little reluctantly, puts it down again.

You watch her put it away. You are sitting at the edge of the platform, with your boots off and your feet tucked under a skin, and you feel the house settling around you, the way a house does at the end of a long, full evening. The floor in front of the platform is scuffed and trodden from the dancing. A small heap of bones lies on the meat board, picked clean. The drum leans against the wall where Kunnuk laid it down. The air is warm and close and smells of fur and oil and people, and of the faint, clean coldness of snow. You can hear the small sounds of six people getting comfortable: a sigh, a rustle of skins, the creak of the platform as someone shifts their weight. And under it all, so steady that you hardly notice it any more, you can hear the soft sizzle of the lamp.

Why does a child who loves string games put her string away tonight?

The string games of this coast are wonderful things. With a single loop of sinew or thong, held between the fingers and woven back and forth, a child or a grown-up can make dozens of different figures, each with its own name, each one a picture of something in the world: a caribou, a fox, a kayak, a drum dancer, two people dragging a seal. Some figures move as you play them, the caribou running, the dancer turning. On a winter evening in a snow house, string games were among the best-loved pastimes of all, for young and old alike.

But in the Iglulik country, as elders there later remembered, there was a season when the strings were put away. During the great darkness, the weeks around midwinter when the sun does not rise, string games were set aside, and in some families old strings were cut, until the sun came back. Only when the light returned, in the new year, would the strings come out again. Customs like this differed from family to family. But Ivalu keeps this one, and so does her granddaughter, a little reluctantly, every year.

And there is a gentler reason too, one that every child on this coast knows. There is a spirit, the old people say, that loves string figures more than anything in the world, and that sometimes comes in the dark to a child who is playing with string all alone, late at night, when everyone else is asleep. It challenges the child to a contest of string figures. So it is wiser, the grandmothers say, to put the string away at bedtime, and not to be the last one awake. Qaunaq knows this. She tucks her string under the edge of the furs, and pulls her caribou skin up over her shoulders, and looks at her grandmother instead.

"Tell the one about the sun," she says.

There are other stories, too, for other nights. There are the long adventures of Kiviuq, the great traveler, who journeyed across the sea in his kayak and met giants and spirits and strange peoples, and whose story was so long that it could be told across many evenings, a little at a time, like a book read aloud a chapter at a bedtime. There are stories of how the animals came to be the way they are, why the raven is black and the fox is clever and the seal lives beneath the ice. There are stories of real people, of grandparents and great-grandparents, of journeys and hard winters and good hunts, told so often that children knew them by heart and could correct the teller if a single detail was wrong. Stories were the library of this coast, and the elders were its keepers, and the long dark evenings of winter were when the library was open.

The name of this month, in the speech of this coast, means something like the great darkness. It is the darkest time of the whole year. And it is the time when people here most liked to tell, and hear, the story of where the sun came from, and why it goes away.

There are many versions of that story, told along the whole Arctic coast, from Greenland to Alaska. Franz Boas wrote one down during his winter on Baffin Island, and published it in his report of 1888. In the version he recorded, a sister and her brother lived together in a village long ago. A visitor came to the sister in the dark, again and again, and she could not see who he was. So one night she blackened her hands with soot from her lamp, and touched his face, and in the morning she saw that the visitor was her own brother, with the soot still on him. She was so upset that she seized a burning torch and ran out of the house, and he seized a torch and ran after her. They ran so fast, and so far, that they rose up into the sky. She became the sun, still carrying her bright torch. His torch had nearly gone out, and gave only a faint glow, and he became the moon, still following her round and round the sky, and never quite catching up. And the dark marks on the moon's face, people say, are the soot from her lamp.

People who study these stories do not all agree about why there are so many versions. Some think the differences grew up slowly over centuries, region by region, as families moved apart along the coast. Others think much of the difference comes from the tellers, because every good storyteller shapes a story a little, to suit the listeners and the night. It is probably both. A story is like a snow house. The plan is old, but every one is built fresh.

You are lying on the platform now, beside Qaunaq, under a caribou skin, listening.

The lamp is lower than it was at supper. Ivalu has turned the wick down, so that the row of little flames is shorter and softer, and the light in the house has grown deeper and more golden. The walls of the dome have gone from bright to dim. The highest part of the roof is in shadow. The drying rack hangs above the lamp, dark, with the shapes of boots and coats on it, and those shapes are thrown as large soft shadows up onto the curve of the ceiling, and they move, very slightly, whenever the flames move.

Ivalu tells the story slowly. Her voice is low. She does not tell it the way Boas wrote it down. She tells it her own way, with the sister's name, and the name of the village, and what the house looked like, and how cold it was the night the sister ran. She makes the brother's torch sputter and spit. She makes the sister's torch blaze. Her hands move in the lamplight as she talks, and you can almost see the two of them running up across the dark sky, one bright, one faint.

Ivalu herself first heard the story of the sun from her own grandmother, in a snow house on another coast, more than half a century ago. She remembers almost nothing of that house now, except the voice, and a lamp much like this one, and the feeling of lying very warm under a skin while the story went on above her in the dark. She remembers that her grandmother always paused, just before the sister seized her torch, and looked at each of the children in turn, to see who was still awake. Ivalu does the same thing tonight, without thinking about it. She pauses, and looks at Qaunaq, and at Kaujak, and at you. Then she goes on. Somewhere, long ago, that pause began with a grandmother nobody now remembers by name. It has come down the generations to this house, like the lamp, and like the story itself, carried by voices instead of hands.

Then, in her own words, she adds the part that a sleepy child most wants to hear. However long the great darkness lasts, she says, the sister always comes back. First only a little glow along the southern edge of the world, at noon. Then the edge of her face. Then, day by day, more of her, until in the spring she does not set at all, and the whole world is light all night.

You listen to the steady voice, and the faint sizzle of the lamp, and the small sounds of the house around you. Somewhere above you, very faintly, a little wind is passing over the roof of the dome. You can hear it whispering against the snow, but you cannot feel it at all. In here, the air is perfectly still.

Out on the ice, very far off, there is a deep, slow sound, like a door closing somewhere at the bottom of the sea. Then another, nearer, a long groan that ends in a crack. You lift your head. Nobody else does. It is only the sea ice, settling in the cold, as the night deepens and the ice shrinks and shifts a little against itself over the slow breathing of the tide beneath. The families of this coast hear it every winter night. To them it is as ordinary as the creak of a wooden house in the wind is to you, and as comforting, in its way. It means the ice is thick, and strong, and holding. You lay your head down again. The sound fades away into the distance, and does not come again.

Qaunaq's eyes are closing.

"Does she always come back?" she asks, very sleepily.

"Always," says Ivalu.

On the other side of the platform, Arnatsiaq is nursing the baby. She has slid Ukpik round to the front, inside the wide shoulder of her amauti, and the baby is feeding quietly, with one small hand resting against her mother's collarbone. Arnatsiaq's head is tipped back against the skins on the wall. Her eyes are half closed too. She has heard the story of the sun a hundred times, from her own grandmother, long ago, on another coast, and she likes it just as much as Qaunaq does.

Kunnuk is sitting near the lamp with his harpoon line across his knees, working on it by feel. A short stretch of the sealskin line was frayed today, where it rubbed against the edge of the breathing hole while he was hauling the seal up, and he is cutting out the frayed part and splicing the clean ends together, tucking and pulling, tucking and pulling. He does not need to look. He listens to his mother's story while his fingers work.

Kaujak is lying on his back with his hands behind his head, looking up at the shadows on the ceiling. He is thirteen, and too old, he would say, for stories about the sun. But he has not moved since his grandmother began.

Kaujak, still looking at the ceiling, asks his grandmother whether the moon was out tonight. He did not see it. Ivalu did. Earlier, she says, in the last of the afternoon twilight, she saw it for a little while, very low in the south, the thinnest curved sliver, like a paring from a fingernail, and then it went down. The brother is only just beginning to follow his sister again, she says. In a week or two he will be bright, and high, and will stay up all through the dark hours, and the hunters will be glad of him on the ice. Kaujak nods, as if he had expected exactly that. Then he folds his hands behind his head again, and goes on looking at the shadows on the ceiling, and says nothing more.

When the story ends, nobody says anything for a while.

Then Ivalu reaches out to the lamp. With the tip of her bone stick, she lowers the flame a little more, all along the wick, until the row of tiny flames is low and soft and steady, like a row of glowing beads. Her hand is very steady. She does not hurry. For a moment she sits and watches the flames settle, the way a mother watches a child settle into sleep, and only when every one of them is the same height does she lay the bone stick down on the stand beside the lamp, where her fingers will find it in the dark. It will burn like this, quietly, for many hours. The pot hangs above it, black with soot along its bottom, the same soot that the sister in the story put on her brother's face, long ago, in a house very like this one.

And the house grows dimmer, and warmer, and softer, around its little line of light.

## Part Ten — Around Half Past Eleven at Night: Under the Sleeping Skins

By half past eleven, the three stars in a row stand almost due south, at the very top of their long climb, and the house is going to bed.

How do six people sleep warm, in a house made of snow, on a night of fifty below?

They sleep together, side by side, on the broad platform at the back of the house, where the warmest air gathers. And they sleep on, and under, a great deal of fur.

Bedtime begins with the platform itself. The snow of the platform is cold, and a body lying on it would lose its warmth downward, into the snow, very quickly. So the platform is covered first with a thick layer of skins, laid fur side down, and then with more skins on top, fur side up, soft and deep, until the snow beneath is far away. Some families along this coast laid a mat of willow twigs or dried heather under the skins too, gathered on the land in the autumn and carried out onto the ice, to keep a layer of air between the furs and the snow. On top of all this, the family lies down in a row. Over them goes a great sleeping cover of caribou skins, sewn together into a broad blanket, with the fur turned in.

And under that cover, people sleep with their clothes off.

That surprises visitors, at first. But it makes perfect sense. Clothes worn all day hold a little damp from the body, and damp is the enemy. So at night, the inner clothes come off too, and go up onto the rack above the lamp to dry, along with everything else, and the family sleeps bare beneath the furs, warm skin against warm fur, each person warming the person beside them. The cover traps the warmth of six bodies at once. Nobody sleeps alone, and nobody sleeps cold. By morning, the clothes are dry and warm, and ready to be put on again.

Here is something lighter. The year 1922, as it happens, was the year that the rest of the world first saw a snow house from the inside. In June of that year, in New York, a film opened called Nanook of the North, made by the American filmmaker Robert Flaherty, about an Inuit family on the east coast of Hudson Bay, far to the south and east of here. Its star was a hunter named Allakariallak, whom the film called Nanook. One of its most famous scenes shows the family going to bed in their snow house, undressing and slipping under the furs, while the lamp burns beside them.

But there was a secret. As Flaherty later explained, the camera of his day was much too big, and needed much too much light, to film inside an ordinary snow house. So Allakariallak and his companions built one twice the usual size, and then cut away half of the dome, so that the daylight could fall in. The family in that tender bedtime scene, snuggling down under the furs, was in fact going to bed in the open air, under a half roof, in the full cold of the Arctic. They must have been very glad when Flaherty said that was enough.

People do not all agree about how to remember that film. Some see it as a great and loving portrait of Inuit life, often called the first feature-length documentary ever made, made by a filmmaker who spent many months with the family and relied on them for everything, from building the sets to fixing his camera. Others point out how much of it was staged, and that by 1922 the real Allakariallak hunted with a rifle, not only the harpoon he carries on screen. Both are true. It is a beautiful film, and it is a performance. The people in it knew the difference, even if the audiences in New York did not.

You undress the way the family does, slowly, one layer at a time. Your inner parka comes off over your head, and the warm air of the house touches your bare shoulders, and it is not cold at all. You hand the parka to Arnatsiaq, and she hangs it on the rack above the lamp, fur side out, beside Kaujak's. Your fur stockings go up too, turned inside out. Your trousers. Everything you were wearing is up there now, among the family's clothes, in the gentle rising warmth, and you are sitting on the edge of the platform with a caribou skin round you, watching it all hang there, still and soft, like a row of sleeping animals.

Then you slide under the cover. The first touch of the fur on your bare skin is cool, for a moment, and then, almost at once, it is warm. It is the strangest and simplest thing. There is nothing between you and the fur, and nothing between the fur and the warmth of your own body, and so nothing is wasted. You pull it up to your chin.

You are lying on the platform now, under the great caribou cover, between Kaujak and the wall.

It is very soft. The fur beneath you is deep enough to sink into, and the cover above you is heavy and warm, and smells of caribou and smoke and sleep. You can feel the warmth gathering around you, slowly, from your own body and from Kaujak's beside you, until it fills the whole space beneath the cover, like warm water filling a bath. Your feet, which have been cold all evening, are warm at last. Your shoulders loosen. You let your head sink into the rolled fur that serves you for a pillow.

The lamp is still burning, low, at the side of the platform. From here you can see it if you turn your head, a soft little line of gold, very steady. It throws just enough light to show the curve of the dome above you, dim and golden near the lamp and fading into darkness at the top. The rack hangs above the flames, with all the clothes of the household on it, and all their shadows, very still.

You can hear everyone breathing.

If you could drift up out of the furs now, and rise softly through the dome, and out into the night, you would see the whole village from above, sleeping on the frozen sea.

There are the three snow houses, low and round, with their small domes of storehouses beside them. Each one glows, faintly, from within, a dim gold light showing through its window of ice above the door, and through the snow of the walls themselves, where they are thinnest, like a lamp seen through a paper shade. From the top of each dome, a thin thread of pale vapor rises straight up into the still air, and hangs there, and slowly fades. Around the houses, the dogs lie curled in the snow, gray mounds with frost on their fur, breathing slowly, fast asleep. The sleds lie on their sides, their glassy runners catching the starlight. Beyond them, the ice stretches away in every direction, flat and gray and silent, to the edge of the world.

And above it all, the sky is enormous. It is full of stars, more stars than you have ever seen, so thick in places that they seem to run together into a pale, glittering haze. High in the south, the three stars in a row are shining, bright and even, at the very top of their climb. Everything is still. Nothing moves anywhere, as far as you can see, except the slow threads of breath rising from the three warm houses.

Then you drift down again, through the roof, into the gold dimness, and back under the furs, and you are warm.

Arnatsiaq is singing to the baby.

She is lying near the far end of the row, with Ukpik tucked against her under the cover, and she is singing very softly, hardly more than a breath. It is Ukpik's own song. On this coast, a child often has a little song of its own, made for it by someone who loves it, a mother or a grandmother or an aunt, full of the child's name and small pet words and soft sounds, and sung to it over and over, at bedtime and on the trail and whenever it cries. Arnatsiaq made this one herself, the winter Ukpik was born. It is about a little owl who sits on a white hill and looks at the whole world with her round eyes. It goes up and down, over and over, like a cradle rocking. Nobody else sings it. It belongs to Ukpik.

Ukpik's eyes are closed already. Her mouth is a little open. One hand is curled against her mother's chin.

The song grows slower, and softer, and the gaps between the lines grow longer. Then it stops altogether, in the middle of a line, and does not start again. Arnatsiaq is asleep. Her day began before anyone else's, with the lamp and the baby and the boots, and went on through the seal and the supper and the singing, and now, at last, it is over. Her arm lies across Ukpik under the cover, loose and heavy. Her face, turned toward the dim gold of the lamp, is smooth and quiet. In the morning she will be up again early, but that is a long way off, and for now there is nothing at all that she needs to do.

Next to them lies Kunnuk. He is asleep already. He lay down, and pulled the cover up to his chin, and sighed once, very deeply, and that was all. He has been awake since before the noon twilight, and walking and standing on the ice for most of the day, and now he sleeps the way a tired hunter sleeps, completely, without moving, his breathing long and slow and even.

Qaunaq is curled on her side between her father and her brother, with her knees drawn up and her hands under her cheek. She was asleep before the story of the sun had quite finished, and someone lifted her and laid her here, under the cover, and she did not wake. Her loop of sinew string is still tucked under the edge of the furs, where she left it, waiting for the sun. Her breathing is slow and soft, with a tiny catch at the end of each breath, the way small children breathe when they are deeply asleep and dreaming of nothing at all.

Kaujak is lying on his back, beside you, with his eyes open, looking at the dim gold dome. He is thinking about his breathing hole, and the seal that did not come, and the one that will.

He drifts, half awake, through the whole long day again. The walk out across the ice in the blue noon light, with the dogs straining at their lines. The hole under the snow, and the long waiting, and the cold creeping into his toes. The thread of down trembling. His father's arms. The little storehouse closing over his head, and his father's hand on the back of his neck. The drum. The cousin's song, and everyone laughing. His grandmother's voice, and the sister with her torch. One by one, the pictures grow dimmer and softer and slip away, like the stars going out at noon. His eyes close. They open again. They close, and stay closed.

Only Ivalu is still sitting up.

She sits at the edge of the platform, beside the lamp, with a skin around her shoulders, and looks at the little row of flames. Then she reaches out with her bone stick, and makes one small adjustment, pressing a short length of the wick a little deeper into the oil. She lays two more pieces of fat in the bowl, behind the wick, where they will melt slowly through the night. She looks at the pot above the lamp, full of ice for the morning, and at the drying clothes, and at the drip-catcher underneath, and at the plug of snow in the vent hole above. Everything is as it should be. The house is ready for the night. And so, at last, is she.

Before she lies down, she does one more small thing. Beside the lamp, in a little pouch of skin, she keeps her store of wick: dried moss and the soft white heads of Arctic cotton, gathered on the land in late summer and packed away. She takes out a small pinch of it, and rolls it between her fingers, and lays it ready beside the lamp, on the stand, where she can find it in the dark. If any part of the wick burns down too far in the night, it will be there.

Then, at last, she lies down too, at the very end of the row, nearest the lamp, where she can reach it without getting up. She pulls the cover over her shoulders, and turns on her side, so that she is facing the flame.

She will wake in the night, as she always does, without being called, to look at it. She has done this almost every night for most of her life.

For now, she lies quietly, and watches the little line of light, and her eyes slowly grow heavy.

The lamp burns on, low and gold and steady, at the side of the sleeping house.

## Part Eleven — Around Four in the Morning: The Runners Going Down

The hours go by softly now, in the dark. Midnight. One. Two. Three. Nobody counts them. There is no clock in this house, and no one in it has ever needed one. And toward four in the morning, Ivalu wakes.

What wakes an old woman in the night, in a house with no clock and no bell?

Nothing you could hear. She simply wakes, as she has woken at about this time of the night for most of her life, because some part of her has been listening to the lamp even in her sleep. The fat she laid in it at bedtime has nearly melted away. The pool of oil has grown shallow. The flames have sunk a little, and the soft sizzle they make has changed its note, very slightly, the way the sound of a kettle changes before it boils. She could not tell you how she knows. She only opens her eyes, and the lamp is there, and it needs her.

She does not get up. She only reaches out from under the cover, with her bare arm, and feels for the bone stick on the stand. Then she lifts the drooping parts of the wick, gently, one after another, and presses a short length of fresh moss from her little pinch into a place where the old wick has burned too low. She lays two more pieces of fat in the bowl. And slowly, as the fat begins to melt, the row of flames rises again, and steadies, and the dome above her brightens from deep bronze to a soft, warm gold.

You are awake too, lying very still under the furs, watching her.

The house is utterly quiet. You can hear the breathing of the sleepers along the platform, each one slow and different, like the sound of a calm sea. You can hear the tiny crackle of the new fat as it begins to melt into the oil. Far away, outside, a single dog sighs in its sleep. Above you, the dome is a soft curve of gold and shadow, and the clothes hang still on their rack, and nothing moves at all except the little flames.

Ivalu sees that you are awake. She smiles, and lifts a finger to her lips, and then, very quietly, she slips out from under the cover, pulls her inner parka down from the rack and over her head, wraps a skin around her shoulders, and climbs down off the platform. At the hole in the floor she looks back at you, and nods toward the tunnel. You follow her.

The tunnel is cold and dark. You crawl along it behind her, feeling the trodden snow under your hands, until you reach the low doorway at the end, and the night.

She does not go out. She only kneels in the doorway, with her skin pulled close around her, and looks up. You kneel beside her, and look up too.

The sky is clear from one edge of the world to the other. The stars are so many, and so bright, that the snow on the ice beneath them glows faintly gray, and you can see the shapes of the sleds and the dogs and the little storehouse quite clearly. There is no moon. There is no wind. And low in the west-southwest, a little above the ice, three bright stars in a short, straight row are sinking slowly toward the horizon.

"Ullaktut," says Ivalu softly. The runners.

These are the three stars we watched rising over the eastern ice in the early evening, the three stars that much of the world knows as the belt of Orion. To the families of this coast, they are three hunters, running. Behind them, a little apart, there is a fainter star that some families say is a fourth hunter, who dropped his glove and stopped to pick it up, and has been trailing behind the others ever since. Every winter evening the runners come up out of the east, and climb across the southern sky through the night, and go down into the west before the noon light comes. They are running now, low over the ice, toward their setting.

And that is the beginning of the answer to our last question.

People who study the star knowledge of this coast do not all agree about every star. In one community, a group of stars might be known by one name and one story, and in another community, a few hundred miles away, by a different one. Some scholars have tried to draw up a single Inuit map of the sky. Others say that there was never one map, but many, each belonging to its own region and its own families, and each just as true as the others. Both are right, in their way. The sky was shared. The names were local.

Many years after our night, in the late twentieth century, a researcher named John MacDonald sat down with the elders of Iglulik, the very place near which our village stands, and recorded what they remembered of the stars, over many years of patient conversations. In 1998 he published it all in a book called The Arctic Sky. In it, the elders of Iglulik describe how the stars were used to tell the time of night, by watching how far certain stars had climbed or sunk; how the moon was used to count the months; and how one particular pair of stars, called Aagjuuk, was watched for with special care in the very darkest weeks of the year.

Aagjuuk are two stars, one bright and one fainter, close together, which much of the world knows as Altair and its small companion, in the constellation of the Eagle. Through the autumn, they are seen in the evening sky. Then, as the year darkens, they slip away into the glare of the low southern twilight and are lost. And then, around the very shortest days of the year, they appear again, rising in the northeast in the dark hours of the morning, just before the noon twilight begins. Their return, the elders said, meant that the darkest time had turned, and that the sun was on its way back. For many families of this coast, the first sight of Aagjuuk in the morning sky marked the beginning of a new year.

So here is the answer to our third question, the question of how you keep time when the sun does not rise. You keep it with the moon, counting the months by her coming and going, and naming each one for what happens in it, so that this month is the great darkness, and the next will be the month when the sun comes back. You keep the hours of the night with the stars, watching the runners climb from the east and cross the south and sink into the west. You keep the turning of the year with Aagjuuk, rising again in the dark of the morning, just when the darkness is deepest. You keep the day with the long blue twilight at noon, growing a little longer and brighter each week, after midwinter. And you keep the small hours of the night, as Ivalu does, by the lamp, which tells her, by the note of its sizzle and the height of its flames, when it is time to wake and feed it.

Ivalu looks toward the northeast, where the sky is still deep and dark and full of stars. She does not say anything. She knows that in a little while, before the twilight comes, two stars will lift there above the ice, one bright and one faint, close together. She has watched for them every midwinter for longer than she can remember. In all her winters, they have never once failed to come.

For a little while longer, Ivalu stays where she is, kneeling in the low doorway, looking out.

The cold is very deep, but it is still, and it does not hurt. It rests on the face like a cool hand. Breath rises in slow white clouds, and hangs, and drifts upward, and fades among the stars. Nobody speaks. There is nothing that needs to be said.

The ice is silent. The dogs are silent. Even the sea ice, which groaned and settled in the evening, has gone quiet now. There is nothing to hear at all, except slow breathing, and once, very faintly, the soft creak of the snow as Ivalu shifts her weight.

Above you, the stars go on turning, very slowly, the way they have turned over this coast for every winter night that anyone remembers. Nobody can see them move. But they are moving, the runners going down in the west, and the night going round with them, and the morning coming, somewhere far below the edge of the ice. Nobody needs to watch for it. It will come by itself.

Then she turns, and crawls back along the tunnel, and you follow her.

The warmth closes around you again as you rise up through the hole into the house. It is like slipping into a warm bath after a cold walk. The gold light of the lamp is soft and even. The sleepers along the platform have not stirred. Ivalu hangs her parka back on the rack, and slides back under the cover at the end of the row, beside the lamp, and settles with a long, slow breath.

You climb back under the furs too, into the warm hollow you left, beside Kaujak.

The fur is still warm from your body. You sink into it, and it holds you, and the warmth comes back into your hands and your feet and your face, slowly, gently, all the way through. You let your arms lie loose at your sides. You let your breathing slow until it matches the breathing of the sleepers around you. In, and out. In, and out. Like the sea beneath the ice, rising and falling, very slowly, in the dark.

Your eyelids grow heavy. You let them close. Behind them, the gold of the lamp glows faintly, warm and soft, the way the sun glows through closed eyes on a summer afternoon. You do not need to see it. You know it is there.

Everything is the same as it was. The lamp is burning. The clothes are drying. The pot of ice hangs over the flame, slowly melting into water for the morning. The baby is asleep against her mother. Kunnuk has not moved at all. Qaunaq has turned over onto her other side, and one bare foot has crept out from under the cover, and Ivalu reaches over and tucks it gently back in.

Kaujak, beside you, murmurs something in his sleep, a word or two, too soft to hear, and smiles, and is still again. Perhaps he is at his breathing hole. Perhaps, in his dream, the down is trembling, and it is his turn at last.

Arnatsiaq sleeps with her cheek against the top of the baby's head. Ukpik sleeps with her fists curled up beside her face, the way very small children sleep everywhere in the world. Their breathing goes together, one slow and one quick, rising and falling under the cover.

Kunnuk sleeps on his back, with one arm flung up over his head, as still as a stone. His harpoon line lies coiled at the edge of the platform, mended, ready for the morning. But the morning is a long way off yet.

The dome is dim and gold above the sleepers.

It is very peaceful here, in the small hours, in the warm. Outside, the runners are going down toward the ice, one by one. The night is very old now, and very quiet. There is nothing left to do but rest.

In the other two houses, the neighbors are sleeping too. The cousin and his wife lie with their three small sons between them, in a warm tangle of arms and legs and fur, the youngest with his thumb in his mouth. The old couple lie side by side, as they have lain for more than forty winters, with their grown daughter at the end of the row, near their own small lamp. In each house, the lamp is low and gold and steady. In each house, an old woman will wake once or twice before morning to tend it, and then sleep again.

The dogs sleep in the snow, curled tight, noses under tails. The sleds sleep on their sides. The little storehouse sleeps with its block of snow across the door. The whole village is asleep on the frozen sea, under the turning stars, three small warm lights in an enormous, quiet dark.

And beneath it all, far down, beneath the ice, the sea moves gently in its long, slow tide, rising and falling, the way it has risen and fallen all night, and every night, and the seals drift sleepily from one breathing hole to another, and breathe, and drift on.

Ivalu lies on her side, facing the lamp, and watches it for a little while, the way you might watch a sleeping child. The row of small flames burns low and even and gold, exactly as she left it.

Her eyes close.

For a little while, before sleep takes her, she thinks of small, warm things. Her mother's hands on this same lamp, long ago, trimming the wick with a stick of bone much like her own. The summer camp beside the river, and the sun going round and round all night. Her husband's voice, singing, on a spring evening, outside a tent of skins. Qaunaq's little lopsided boot. The pause in the story of the sun, and the children's faces in the lamplight, waiting.

None of it is sad. All of it is close, and gentle, and gold, like the light of the lamp on the curve of the dome.

Her breathing slows. Her hand, resting on the edge of the furs near the lamp, grows still. And the old keeper of the lamp sleeps, warm and safe, beside the small row of light that she has tended all her life.

The tunnel is dark and still. The storehouse is closed. The rack is full of warm, dry clothes. The house is perfectly quiet now. Six people sleep under one cover of fur, in one house of snow, on the frozen sea. And the lamp burns on.

## Part Twelve — Around Eight in the Morning: Two Stars in the Northeast, and the First Blue Light

The hours go by softly. Five. Six. Seven. The lamp burns low and gold through every one of them, and the house sleeps around it.

Up close, in these last dark hours, the lamp is a very small thing. A half moon of gray stone. A narrow lip along its straight edge. A low ridge of moss. A shallow pool of oil, clear and pale. And along the wick, the long row of tiny flames, each one hardly taller than a grain of rice, each one perfectly still in the still air. It is hard to believe that so small a light could hold back so large a dark. But it has, all night, and it is holding it back now.

What does a snow house sound like, in the last hours of the longest night of the year?

Almost nothing at all.

You are lying under the furs, half awake, half dreaming, and you are listening. There is no wind. There are no voices. There is no drum, and no laughter, and no sound of a snow knife whispering through a drift. There is only the breathing of the sleepers, slow and deep and even, all along the platform. There is the faint, soft sizzle of the lamp. And now and then, very small, there is the tiny sound of a drop of water falling from the bag of ice into the bucket beneath it. Drip. And a long, long pause. And drip.

Each drop is a little slower than the last, it seems, as if the ice itself were growing sleepy. You find yourself waiting for the next one, and forgetting to wait, and drifting, and then hearing it again from very far away, soft and small and round, like a pebble dropped into a still pool. Drip. And the long, quiet pause, filled with nothing but breathing.

That is all. And it is enough.

So let your breathing slow, the way theirs has. In, and out. Let the furs be heavy and warm on your shoulders. Let your hands lie loose and still. Feel the soft, even warmth of the lamp on one side of your face, and the deep, quiet dark of the dome above, and the great still cold of the night beyond the snow, which cannot reach you here. You are safe. You are warm. There is nothing at all that you need to do, and nowhere at all that you need to go.

The air under the cover is warm and still. The fur is soft against your skin. Your arms are heavy. Your legs are heavy. Your head lies deep in the rolled fur of the pillow, and you could not lift it now if you wanted to, and you do not want to.

Above you, the dome is a soft curve of gold near the lamp, fading upward into gentle shadow. The clothes hang on the rack, dry now, and warm, and very still, their shadows spread across the curve of the ceiling like the shadows of sleeping birds. The pot hangs above the flame, full of clear water melted from the ice, ready for the morning. The drum leans against the wall. The meat board lies on the floor, wiped clean. Everything in the house is resting.

The snow walls hold the warmth in, patiently, as they have all night. The tunnel holds the cold away. The furs hold the sleepers. And the lamp holds its small, steady light at the center of it all, the one bright, living thing awake in the house.

Over the doorway, the round window of ice is still dark. Later, when the noon twilight comes, it will turn a soft, pale gray, and then a faint, clear blue, and the first light of the day will come in through it, gently, and lie across the floor. But not yet. For now, it holds only the faint gold of the lamp, reflected back into the room, like a small, round, sleepy eye.

If you could drift up now, softly, out through the dome, and into the night, you would find the sky beginning to change.

Not much. Only a very little. Far off in the northeast, low over the ice, where the sky was deep and black and full of stars a few hours ago, two stars have risen, close together, one bright and one fainter. Aagjuuk. They hang there, low and steady, just as Ivalu knew they would. And far off in the south, along the very edge of the world, the black of the sky is thinning, very slowly, into a deep, dark blue. It is not light yet. It is only a little less dark. As if someone, very far away, had breathed gently on a dark window.

The runners have gone down. The night has turned. And the long blue twilight of the noon is on its way back, a little earlier and a little longer than yesterday, as it will be every day from now on.



Over the ice, the dogs sleep on, gray mounds in the gray snow. The thread of breath still rises from the roof of the house, pale and straight. And the two stars in the northeast climb a little higher, very slowly, as the sky behind them begins, at last, to soften. In a little while, the blue along the south will grow, and the faintest stars will fade into it, one by one, the way a sleeper's thoughts fade into dreams. Aagjuuk will be among the last to go.You drift back down, through the roof, into the warmth, and under the furs again, and you are glad to be there.

Our last source of the night is a song. Among the many songs that Knud Rasmussen wrote down on the Fifth Thule Expedition, there was one that Aua told him about, a song made long before by a woman of the Iglulik people called Uvavnuk. It was published in English in 1929, and it has been translated many times since, and every translation is a little different. But all of them say the same thing, in their own words. The great sea moves her, the song says, and sets her adrift, and she moves with it, the way a weed moves in a river. The sky and the strong wind move her too, and carry her away. And her whole inner self trembles, not with fear, but with joy.

That is a good song to hold in your mind, in a warm house of snow, in the last dark hours before the light.

Let it move you, too, if it likes, very gently. The great sea, far beneath the ice. The sky, wide and dark and full of stars. The slow tide, rising and falling. And you, warm and still, adrift in the middle of it all, carried softly along toward the morning, with nothing to hold on to, and nothing that you need to hold on to.

People who think about nights like this one sometimes wonder whether we picture the old snow-house winters too kindly, and forget the cold, and the hard work, and the long, hungry weeks. Others say we are right to remember their warmth, because the families who lived them remembered it too, and sang about it, and told it to their children. Perhaps both are true, here, in the dark. The hard work of the day is done. The warmth is what is left.

You think about the long night that has passed in this room. The last blue light at noon, and the band of rose along the southern sky. The dogs singing out of the dusk. The seal, and the drink of fresh water given to it. The glassy runners. The little storehouse closing over your head, block by leaning block. The warmth rising up through the hole in the floor, like warm water. The boots on the rack. The frozen fish melting on your tongue. The pot that took all evening. The drum, and the laughter, and the old man's kick. Ivalu's song about the river. The sister with her torch. The thin new moon going down. The runners crossing the south. And two stars rising in the northeast, just when the dark was deepest.

All of it is quiet now. All of it is folded away, like the clothes on the rack, to be kept.

The night has been long, the longest of the whole year. But it has been gentle, every hour of it. And now it is nearly over. Nothing more will happen in it. Nothing more needs to.

Somewhere in the hours to come, Ivalu will wake again, as she always does, and reach out from under the cover, and feed the lamp, and lift the wick with her bone stick, so that the house will be bright when the others wake. Then Arnatsiaq will wake, and the baby, and the pot will be warmed, and the dogs will stretch and shake the frost from their coats, and Kunnuk and Kaujak will pull on their dry boots and go out into the blue noon light, toward the breathing holes.

Qaunaq will wake and look at once for her loop of string, and remember, and leave it where it is. Kaujak will wake and tell his sister that today it will be his hole. Ukpik will wake and laugh at the lamp. And the cousin will crawl in through the tunnel with frost in his eyebrows, to ask whether Kunnuk is ready, and the whole small round of the day will begin again, the same and not the same, in the blue light.

But not yet. Not yet.



The village lies quiet on the frozen sea. The three houses glow faintly in the starlight. The threads of breath rise from their roofs, straight and pale and slow. The dogs sleep. The sleds sleep. The ice lies still and gray to the edge of the world, and beneath it, the sea rises and falls, very gently, in its long, slow tide.For now, everyone is still asleep.

Ivalu is asleep at the end of the row, nearest the lamp, lying on her side, with her bare arm resting on the edge of the furs, where she can reach the flame without waking fully. Her face is turned toward the light. Her bone stick lies on the stand beside her.

Arnatsiaq is asleep with her cheek against the top of her baby's head, her arm loose and heavy across the little body under the cover. Her long day is over, and the next one has not yet begun.

Ukpik is asleep against her mother, warm and small, with her fists curled up beside her face, breathing quickly and softly, like a little bird.

Kunnuk is asleep on his back, as still as a stone, one arm flung up over his head, his mended harpoon line coiled at the edge of the platform, waiting for the day.

Qaunaq is asleep on her side, with her knees drawn up and her hands under her cheek, and her loop of sinew string tucked under the furs beside her, waiting for the sun.

Kaujak is asleep beside you, on his back, with the faintest smile, dreaming of a trembling thread of down over a breathing hole that is his own.

And you are nearly asleep too, warm under the furs, between Kaujak and the wall, in the soft gold dimness, with the sound of six people breathing all around you, slow and deep and safe, like the sound of the sea.

Out in the snow, Aupaluk is asleep, curled in a tight gray ball, with his nose under his tail and his crooked ear folded down over his eyes. And in the two houses beside this one, the neighbors are asleep too, under their own furs, beside their own low lamps.



You let your eyes close. The gold of the lamp glows softly through your eyelids, warm and faint and steady. The furs are soft. The breathing all around you is slow and deep. Far off, beyond the snow, the sky in the south grows a little bluer, and a little bluer still, so slowly that no one could ever see it change.The house is quiet. The house is warm. The longest night of the year has passed safely, every hour of it.

And at the side of the platform, on its little stand, the stone lamp is still burning, just as it was when we found it yesterday afternoon. Its long, low row of small flames lies along the straight edge of the half moon, gold and even and steady, each one the same height as its neighbor. The oil lies clear and pale in its shallow bowl. The pot hangs above it, and the rack above the pot, and above them all, the curved roof of snow glows softly, like the inside of a shell. It has lit this house all night. It has warmed it, and cooked in it, and dried every stitch of clothing in it. It has kept its small row of light burning in the great darkness, the way it has burned through every winter that Ivalu can remember. And it is waiting, quietly, for the morning, and for the sun.

It will be here when the family wakes. It will be here when the hunters come home tonight, a round gold glow in a window of ice, guiding them in across the dark. And it will be here, low and steady and patient, in the soft care of Ivalu's hands, through all the long nights still to come, until the sun climbs back over the edge of the world in the new year, and the great darkness is over.

For now, in the quiet house, on the frozen sea, the little row of flames burns on, gold and even and alive.

If you enjoyed spending this long winter night with Ivalu and her family, a like or a subscription would mean a great deal, but only if you truly enjoyed it. And if there is a time or a place you would love to spend a night in, leave it in the comments, because your ideas truly help.

Next time, we will visit a very different kind of house, on a very different kind of night, somewhere much warmer, and much farther from the frozen sea.

Sleep well, friend. Good night.
