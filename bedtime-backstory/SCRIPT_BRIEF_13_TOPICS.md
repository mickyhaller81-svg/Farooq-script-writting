# Bedtime Backstory — Script Brief for 13 Topics (2026-11-04)

Give this whole file to the script writer, with ONE topic per run.
Part A applies to every script. Part B is the topic card for that one script.
Source of Part A: `~/candlelight/SCRIPT_RULES_3HR.md` (still a DRAFT, not locked by the owner) plus the fixes we had to make on v37–v46.

# PART A — RULES FOR EVERY SCRIPT

## A1. Size
- **26,500–29,000 words** (narrator reads at 157 wpm, so about 170–185 minutes). Never under 23,500.
- **12 parts**, each about **2,300 words** (never under 1,800 or over 2,900).
- Each part starts with a heading in exactly this form, because our chapter tool reads it:
  `## Part One — Around Half Past One in the Afternoon: The Last Blue Light`

## A2. The spine: ONE place, ONE night, ONE household
- **Place:** one roof only (one house, one tent, one tower, one barracks).
- **Date:** a year and a season ("Yakutsk, January 1885").
- **Household:** 4–9 people, each named, with age and job given when we first meet them.
- **Clock:** late afternoon → dusk → supper → evening work → bedtime → deep night → small hours → first light.
- **Anchor:** one object that stays lit or present all night (lamp, fire, stove, light). Introduce it in the first 3 minutes, touch it in every part, and make it the last image of the script.
- **Composite household:** if the household is invented, say so ONCE, plainly: "This family never quite existed under one name, but every detail of their night is real."

## A3. The opening (first ~450 words), in this exact order
1. **Greeting:** "Good evening, friend, and welcome back to Bedtime Backstory."
2. **Scene hook** (60–100 words): the place, the year and season, and one sensory detail of the cold, the dark or the heat.
3. **Myth reframe** (1–2 sentences): "Not the ___ of the picture books. Just one ordinary ___ on one ordinary night."
4. **Stakes** (1 sentence): "Without a few old tricks, you would not have lasted until morning."
5. **Three carry-questions:** concrete and odd. Link them with one line ("These are not three separate questions…").
   - Q1 is answered near minute 50, Q2 near minute 100, Q3 near minute 150.
6. **Appreciation block** (3 sentences): like and subscribe "only if you genuinely enjoy this"; ask listeners to comment with where they are listening from and their local time; then "That is the only time I will ask tonight."
7. **Settle cue** (2–3 sentences): head heavy, shoulders loose, eyes may close whenever they like. Then "let us go back…" into the first scene.

## A4. Every part follows this loop (~2,300 words)
| Beat | Length |
|---|---|
| **Bridge in:** the clock moves on (never "next" or "moving on") | 1–2 sentences |
| **Small question,** answered inside this same part | 1 sentence |
| **Common fact,** told plainly | ~300 words |
| **Surprising detail,** with a number, a name or a document | ~250 words |
| **Source moment:** a REAL named source with its date, quoted briefly | ~150 words |
| **"You" scene:** present tense, second person, sensory | ~400 words |
| **Modern contrast** (first half of the night only) | 2–4 sentences |
| **Historians disagree:** two fair views | ~100 words |
| **Family moment:** back to our named people | ~500 words |
| **Anchor touch** | 1 sentence |

Something **new and specific** must appear at least every ~400 words. A paragraph of mood alone does not count.

## A5. Energy curve
| Time | Job |
|---|---|
| 0–20 min | Densest, most surprising material. Gentle humor is allowed. |
| 20–120 min | Steady: one new detail every ~400 words. Pay off Q1 and Q2. |
| 120–150 min | Bedtime arrives. Fewer numbers, more senses. Pay off Q3. |
| 150–180 min | **Wind-down.** The household falls asleep one by one. No new facts, no danger, no hardship. Softer, shorter sentences. |

**The night always ends safe and warm** (for the Persian topic: safe and cool).

## A6. The close (~350 words)
1. Name every person once more, now asleep.
2. Return to the anchor as the final image.
3. One soft like and subscribe, in one sentence.
4. **Next-night tease** (1–2 sentences) in general words only ("a very different kind of house…"). **Never name a specific video title**: it may not exist yet.
5. End with "Sleep well, friend. Good night."

## A7. Voice
- Scenes in **present tense**; background history in **past tense**.
- The narrator says "we". "You" appears 8–20 times per 1,000 words (hook, "you" scenes, settle, close).
- Sentence average **15–20 words**, with about 10% short soft lines of 6 words or fewer.
- **Contractions rare** ("it is", not "it's"). **American spelling and units first** (feet, miles, °F, with °C in brackets if useful).
- Numbers are spoken in words ("one hundred and sixty acres"), at most one per paragraph.
- At most about one question per 1,000 words, apart from the three carry-questions.

## A8. Honesty gates — our difference from every other channel
- **Every part has at least one REAL named source with a date:** a diary, letter, travel account, record or archaeological find. The sources listed in Part B are real. **Verify every quote before using it.**
- **Never invent a quotation.** If unsure, paraphrase and attribute plainly ("as he later wrote").
- **Bound every absolute** (every, never, only, nobody, the most… in history) with a named place, date or case, or cut it.
- **Do not write a caveat and then ignore it** two paragraphs later.

## A9. Forbidden (each one has cost us a rewrite)
- Exclamation marks, hype words (insane, crazy, shocking), "let's dive in", "stick around", "but first", cliffhangers, any mid-roll CTA.
- **Research asides in the narration:** "I haven't been able to check this", "I'll paraphrase because I couldn't verify the wording", "according to my notes". **The script is the finished broadcast.**
- **Academic citation clutter in the narration** ("Tab. Vindol. II 346", "Smith et al. 2015"). Say "a letter found at Vindolanda". Full citations go ONLY in the Sources section.
- Teasing a video that does not exist.
- An energetic or loud last 15 minutes.
- Graphic violence dwelt on. Any new danger after minute 150.
- Two topics glued together; lists read out as lists.
- Stage directions, emotion tags, [brackets], or audio notes. **Plain prose only** — we add voice tags ourselves.

## A10. Deliver as
One `.md` file:
1. Line 1: `# <Title> | History for Sleep`
2. The 12 `## Part …` sections.
3. A final `## Sources` section: every source with author, title, year and a link where one exists. This section is not read aloud.

## A11. Self-check before sending (any "no" = fix first)
- [ ] 26,500–29,000 words; 12 parts; each 1,800–2,900
- [ ] One place, one night, named household; anchor introduced by minute 3
- [ ] Opening order exactly as A3; Q1/Q2/Q3 paid off near min 50/100/150
- [ ] Every part: small question answered, one surprising detail, one real dated source
- [ ] Nothing new or heavy after minute 150; ends on the anchor, safe and warm
- [ ] No invented quotes, no research asides, no citation clutter, no "!"
- [ ] Contractions rare; American spelling

# PART B — THE 13 TOPIC CARDS

Each card gives the writer a starting point. Household names are suggestions; keep them period-correct.

## 1. How People Survived the Coldest City on Earth Before Electricity — 9.5
- **Place / date:** a Sakha (Yakut) family's log *balagan* just outside Yakutsk, Siberia, January 1885, with the cattle barn (*khoton*) joined to the house.
- **Anchor:** the open clay fireplace (*kamelek*), never allowed to die all winter.
- **Carry-questions:**
  - How do you make a window out of ice?
  - Why do the cows live under the same roof?
  - How does anyone sleep when the air outside can freeze your breath into a whisper you can hear?
- **Real sources:**
  - Wacław Sieroszewski, Polish political exile in Yakutia 1880–1892; his ethnography *Yakuty* (1896).
  - The record cold at Verkhoyansk (February 1892).
  - The Russian explorer and naturalist accounts of Yakutsk in the 1800s.
- **Care:** the Sakha are the heroes of this night. No "primitive" framing. Russians and exiles appear as visitors.

## 2. How Roman Soldiers Survived Winter on Hadrian's Wall — 9.5
- **Place / date:** one barrack block (an 8-man *contubernium*) at a fort on Hadrian's Wall, a December night in the AD 130s.
- **Anchor:** the brazier in the barrack room (or the bath-house furnace next door).
- **Carry-questions:**
  - What did a Roman soldier wear under his tunic in a British winter?
  - Why were the soldiers here not Italians at all?
  - What did they write home for?
- **Real sources:**
  - The **Vindolanda tablets**:
    - the letter sending socks, sandals and two pairs of underpants;
    - the soldier Masclus asking his commander to send beer because the men had none;
    - the birthday invitation from Claudia Severa to Sulpicia Lepidina;
    - the "wretched little Britons" (*Brittunculi*) note.
  - The Wall itself (begun AD 122). The Batavian and Tungrian cohorts.
- **Care:** the Vindolanda letters were written about **a generation BEFORE the Wall was built** (c. AD 97–105), on the same frontier. Say so honestly. Do not pretend they come from the Wall.

## 3. A Victorian Christmas Eve, Hour by Hour — 9
- **Place / date:** a middle-class London townhouse, 24 December 1880, from the afternoon shopping to Christmas morning.
- **Anchor:** the parlor coal fire. Christmas-tree candles are lit only briefly, under watch.
- **Carry-questions:**
  - How did families light a tree with real candles without burning the house down?
  - Why was the pudding made five weeks earlier?
  - Who was awake in the house after everyone was asleep?
- **Real sources:**
  - Mrs Beeton's *Book of Household Management* (1861).
  - The *Illustrated London News* engraving of the royal Christmas tree (1848).
  - The first commercial Christmas card, Henry Cole (1843).
  - Tom Smith's Christmas crackers.
  - Queen Victoria's own journal entries for Christmas.
- **Care:** we already have a Dickens video. Mention *A Christmas Carol* in one line only. **Upload before December.**

## 4. How Japanese Families Survived Winter in Paper Houses — 9
- **Place / date:** a merchant family's townhouse (*machiya*) in Edo, January 1830.
- **Anchor:** the charcoal *kotatsu* under its quilt (and the *hibachi*).
- **Carry-questions:**
  - Why build with paper walls in a city with snow?
  - How do six people sleep warm around one small pot of charcoal?
  - Why does the whole street fear one spark?
- **Real sources:**
  - Edward S. Morse, *Japanese Homes and Their Surroundings* (1886).
  - Engelbert Kaempfer's account of Japan (1690s).
  - The night fire-watch patrols with wooden clappers.
  - The Meireki fire of 1657, as **background only, early in the night**.
- **Care:** no samurai battle content (we have samurai videos). Fire talk stays in the first half.

## 5. How Lighthouse Keepers Survived Winter on a Rock at Sea — 9
- **Place / date:** Skerryvore lighthouse, a tower on a bare rock 12 miles off Scotland, January 1890. Three keepers.
  - Alternative for the US audience: Minot's Ledge, Massachusetts.
- **Anchor:** **the light itself.** It must be lit at sunset and never fail until dawn.
- **Carry-questions:**
  - How do three men share one tower without a fight?
  - How do you keep a flame steady through a whole winter gale?
  - What happens when the relief boat cannot come?
- **Real sources:**
  - Alan Stevenson's account of building Skerryvore (1848).
  - The Northern Lighthouse Board's keepers' rules and logbook duties.
  - The keepers' watch routine.
- **Care:** the famous dramatic "log entries" from the Flannan Isles mystery (1900) are **a later invention**. Do not quote them as real. Mention the mystery early, not after minute 150.

## 6. How Persians Survived the Desert Heat and Made Ice Without Electricity — 9
- **Place / date:** a family courtyard house in Yazd, Persia, **one summer day and night**, July 1670.
- **Anchor:** the cool pool in the basement room (*sardab*), fed by the underground channel (*qanat*).
- **Carry-questions:**
  - How do you make ice in the desert?
  - How does a tower cool a house with no machine?
  - Why does the whole family sleep on the roof?
- **Real sources:**
  - Jean Chardin's *Travels in Persia* (1670s).
  - Pietro della Valle's letters.
  - The *yakhchal* ice-houses.
  - The *badgir* windcatchers.
  - The *qanat* water system.
- **Care:** this one runs on heat, not cold. Use the same structure, but the clock is a full day (dawn → noon heat → evening → night on the roof → dawn), and the ending is **safe and cool**.

## 7. How the Emperor Survived Winter in the Forbidden City — 9
- **Place / date:** the Hall of Mental Cultivation (*Yangxin Dian*) inside the Forbidden City, Beijing, a January night in the 1770s (Qianlong era). The household is the emperor, his eunuchs and palace maids.
- **Anchor:** the heated brick bed (*kang*) and the charcoal fires under the floors.
- **Carry-questions:**
  - How do you heat a palace of wood without burning it down?
  - Where did the summer ice come from?
  - Who stayed awake all night so the emperor could sleep?
- **Real sources:**
  - Qing palace records.
  - The Qianlong emperor's own poems.
  - The palace ice cellars (ice cut from the moat in winter).
  - The famous smokeless palace charcoal.
- **Care:** long-dead names are fine. No palace intrigue or executions.

## 8. How Native Americans Survived Winter in Tipis — 8.5
- **Place / date:** one Lakota family's tipi in a sheltered river winter camp on the northern Plains, January 1870.
- **Anchor:** the tipi fire, and the inner liner that kept the draft off sleepers.
- **Carry-questions:**
  - Why was a tipi warmer than a log cabin?
  - How did a family tell the years apart?
  - Why did the camp choose this exact bend of the river?
- **Real sources:**
  - Luther Standing Bear, *My People the Sioux* (1928) — run his voice through the night.
  - Lakota winter counts (for example, the Lone Dog winter count).
  - George Catlin's letters and paintings (1830s).
- **Care:**
  - Respectful, specific, no stereotypes ("noble savage", war bonnets as costume).
  - No battle scenes.
  - Use the people's own words wherever possible.
  - Night Caravan already has a tipi video. **Our edge = Standing Bear's real memoir.**

## 9. How the Inuit Survived the Arctic Night in Igloos — 8.5
- **Place / date:** an Inuit snow-house on the sea ice, Central Canadian Arctic, December 1922.
- **Anchor:** the stone seal-oil lamp (*qulliq*), tended by the grandmother.
- **Carry-questions:**
  - How can a house made of snow be warm?
  - How do you cook with no wood for a thousand miles?
  - How do you keep time when the sun does not rise?
- **Real sources:**
  - Knud Rasmussen and the Fifth Thule Expedition (1921–24).
  - Franz Boas, *The Central Eskimo* (1888) — use "Inuit" in the narration; the old word only in the book title.
- **Care:** respect, as with the tipi topic. Avoid polar-bear danger after minute 150.

## 10. How Pirates Survived Freezing Nights at Sea — 8.5
- **Place / date:** a pirate sloop riding out a winter night off the coast of New England, January 1720. The crew is the household.
- **Anchor:** the single lantern allowed below decks.
- **Carry-questions:**
  - Why did pirates vote on everything?
  - Why was every light ordered out at eight o'clock?
  - How did men sleep in wet clothes at sea in winter?
- **Real sources:**
  - Captain Charles Johnson, *A General History of the Pyrates* (1724), including Bartholomew Roberts' written articles (the rule that lights and candles go out at eight o'clock).
  - Court trial records of captured crews.
- **Care:** no hangings or violence in detail. Golden-Age pirates mostly sailed warm seas, so say why this crew is in the cold. Our unused `v29_pirate_rules` folder can hold this one.

## 11. How the Mongols Survived Winter in Their Tents — 8.5
- **Place / date:** one family's felt tent (*ger*) on the Mongolian steppe, January 1254.
- **Anchor:** the central hearth under the roof ring (*toono*).
- **Carry-questions:**
  - How can felt keep out the coldest wind on earth?
  - What burns where no tree grows?
  - Why does everyone sit in a fixed place inside?
- **Real sources:**
  - William of Rubruck, who visited the Mongol court in 1253–55.
  - John of Plano Carpini (1245–47).
  - *The Secret History of the Mongols*.
- **Care:** no conquest or battle content. Keep this the home life of the steppe.

## 12. How Koreans Survived Winter on Fire-Heated Floors — 8.5
- **Place / date:** a Joseon-era tile-roofed house (*hanok*) in a village near Hanyang (Seoul), January 1790.
- **Anchor:** the fire mouth of the floor (*agungi*) in the kitchen, and the warmest spot on the floor (*araenmok*), kept for the eldest.
- **Carry-questions:**
  - How can one kitchen fire warm the bedroom floor next door?
  - Why does the whole village make kimchi on the same days?
  - Who gets the warmest place on the floor, and why?
- **Real sources:**
  - Hendrick Hamel's journal (the Dutch sailor shipwrecked in Korea in 1653, who lived there 13 years).
  - Joseon household records.
  - *Gimjang*, the winter kimchi-making (now on UNESCO's heritage list).
- **Care:** respectful of Confucian family order without lecturing.

## 13. How Tibetan Monks Survived Winter in a Mountain Monastery — 8.5
- **Place / date:** one small high monastery in Tibet, a December night in the 1920s. The household is an old lama, monks and novices.
- **Anchor:** the yak-butter lamps in the shrine room, which are never allowed to go out.
- **Carry-questions:**
  - How do you stay warm at fifteen thousand feet with almost no wood?
  - Why do the youngest monks get up the earliest?
  - What is the practice of inner heat, and what do scientists say about it?
- **Real sources:**
  - Alexandra David-Néel, *My Journey to Lhasa* (1927).
  - The 1980s Harvard study of Tibetan monks' body heat.
- **Care:** respectful of the religion. Describe practice, never mock or "explain away". No politics.
